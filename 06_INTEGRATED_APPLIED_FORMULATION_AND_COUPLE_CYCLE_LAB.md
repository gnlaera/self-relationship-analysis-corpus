# 06_INTEGRATED_APPLIED_FORMULATION_AND_COUPLE_CYCLE_LAB

## Self, Relationship and Couple Therapy Research Corpus

**Document role:** Integrated applied formulation engine, episode-analysis protocol, couple-cycle laboratory, hypothesis-testing framework, and practical reasoning guide.

**Status:** Canonical applied file.

**Controlling files:**

**Function:** This file does not restate Files 01-05, 07 or 08. It operationalises them. Where those files contain local applied templates, this file governs the full cross-corpus integrated formulation and final response structure.

Its central question is:

> **Given the material actually available, what is the simplest defensible account of what happened, what mechanisms may be operating, what remains unknown, and what can be tested or changed?**

The governing standard is not interpretive sophistication.

It is the **most defensible useful formulation**.

---

## 1. What this laboratory is for

This file is designed to help analyse:

- one argument;
- a repeated argument;
- a text-message exchange;
- a period of distance;
- pursuit and withdrawal;
- reassurance seeking;
- jealousy;
- distrust;
- an ambiguous interaction;
- a trust rupture;
- betrayal;
- an attachment injury;
- an attempted repair;
- a boundary dispute;
- emotional withdrawal;
- sexual or intimacy difficulties;
- power and recognition conflicts;
- substantive incompatibility;
- relationship uncertainty;
- a dream;
- active-imagination material;
- a recurring self-state;
- a pattern developing across months or years.

The method is deliberately plural.

It can use:

- relationship science;
- attachment theory;
- affect regulation;
- mentalization;
- emotion-focused formulations;
- behavioural formulations;
- systemic formulations;
- multigenerational family-systems formulations;
- relational psychoanalysis;
- object relations;
- Acceptance and Commitment Therapy values and committed-action formulations;
- existential choice and meaning formulations;
- Jungian concepts;
- Lacanian concepts.

It must not treat these as interchangeable.

The laboratory follows nine priorities:

1. **Observation before interpretation.**
2. **The simplest adequate explanation before a more elaborate one.**
3. **Multiple hypotheses where the evidence is ambiguous.**
4. **The other person’s mind remains partly opaque.**
5. **A reciprocal cycle does not imply equal responsibility.**
6. **Intergenerational interpretation is optional and requires actual family evidence.**
7. **When understanding is sufficient, return to values, agency, choice, and committed action.**
8. **Interpretation should eventually lead toward observation, testing, boundary clarification, repair, or deliberate action.**
9. **The laboratory does not decide whether a relationship should continue.**

## 2. The integrated reasoning architecture

The primary analytical layers are:

- **SELF**
- **PARTNER**
- **RELATIONSHIP**
- **SELF-IN-RELATIONSHIP**
- **CONTEXT**

These are not alternative theories. They are different locations at which evidence can be organised.

| Layer | Core question | Typical material |
|---|---|---|
| **SELF** | What is happening within me? | emotion, bodily arousal, thought, fear, desire, memory, attribution, regulation, agency, behaviour |
| **PARTNER** | What do I actually know about the other person? | observable behaviour, direct statements, reported intentions, known circumstances |
| **RELATIONSHIP** | What happens between us repeatedly? | sequences, bids, responses, escalation, withdrawal, reciprocity, trust, repair, power |
| **SELF-IN-RELATIONSHIP** | What happens to my functioning in this relationship? | agency, mentalization, self-respect, regulation, boundaries, autonomy, openness, flexibility |
| **CONTEXT** | What conditions surround the episode? | work, sleep, money, illness, family of origin, extended family, children, culture, logistics, time pressure, social networks, major stress |

The same episode can look different from each layer.

A person may, for example:

- feel rejected at the **SELF** level;
- observe only that the **PARTNER** has become quiet;
- enter a pursue-withdraw sequence at the **RELATIONSHIP** level;
- lose the capacity to tolerate uncertainty at the **SELF-IN-RELATIONSHIP** level;
- while both partners are operating under severe fatigue at the **CONTEXT** level.

None of these statements cancels the others.

## 4. The five epistemic levels

Every applied answer should distinguish the following.

### 4.1 OBSERVATION

What can reasonably be treated as having happened.

Examples:

- “The message was sent at 18:04 and answered at 22:17.”
- “She said she did not want to continue the conversation that evening.”
- “He raised his voice.”
- “They had discussed the same issue three times in two weeks.”

Observation is not interpretation.

---

### 4.2 SUBJECTIVE EXPERIENCE

What somebody reports feeling, thinking, fearing, wanting, remembering or intending.

Examples:

- “I felt abandoned.”
- “I thought he no longer cared.”
- “She reported feeling overwhelmed.”
- “He said he wanted the argument to stop.”

A report establishes that the experience was reported.

It does not establish that the interpretation of external reality was correct.

---

### 4.3 RELATIONSHIP PATTERN

A repeated sequence supported by more than one episode.

Examples:

- uncertainty → reassurance seeking → irritation → more reassurance seeking;
- criticism → defence → escalation;
- difficult topic → withdrawal → pursuit → shutdown;
- rupture → apology → temporary calm → recurrence without behavioural change.

Do not infer a stable pattern from one vivid incident.

---

### 4.4 EMPIRICAL OR CLINICAL FORMULATION

A mechanism that could explain the observations.

Examples:

- physiological flooding;
- attachment hyperactivation;
- attachment deactivation;
- loss of mentalization during high arousal;
- shame activation;
- threat to identity;
- low perceived responsiveness;
- hostile attribution;
- avoidance reinforced by successful escape from conflict;
- incompatible expectations;
- external stress overwhelming available coping resources.

A formulation remains a hypothesis.

---

### 4.5 THEORY-SPECIFIC INTERPRETATION

A deeper interpretation whose meaning depends on a particular framework.

Examples:

- Jungian complex activation;
- shadow projection;
- relational enactment;
- projective identification;
- shared unconscious phantasy;
- Lacanian fantasy;
- repetition organised around demand and desire.

These interpretations may be illuminating.

They must not overwrite observations or simpler explanations.

---

## 5. Confidence language

Use explicit confidence labels.

These labels refer to **confidence in a case-level claim**. They do not grade the quality of the research literature supporting a general mechanism; literature status remains governed by File 00 **Axis B**. **THEORY-SPECIFIC** means that a claim depends on a particular interpretive framework, not that the framework has a particular empirical rank.

| Label | Meaning | Appropriate basis |
|---|---|---|
| **HIGH CONFIDENCE** | The claim is strongly supported by the available case evidence | direct record, direct quotation, repeated behaviour, multiple converging observations |
| **MODERATE** | The claim fits a repeated pattern or well-supported mechanism, but alternatives remain | recurring episodes, convergent reports, mechanism consistent with evidence |
| **LOW** | The formulation is plausible but underdetermined | sparse evidence, ambiguous behaviour, indirect inference |
| **THEORY-SPECIFIC** | The claim only exists within a particular interpretive framework | Jungian, relational psychoanalytic, object-relations, Lacanian interpretation |

Confidence attaches to the **claim**, not to the seriousness of the event.

For example:

- “He transferred the money without informing her” may be **HIGH CONFIDENCE**.
- “He did this because he unconsciously feared dependency” may be **LOW** or **THEORY-SPECIFIC**.

Do not use numerical probabilities unless there is a genuine quantitative basis for doing so.

## 6. The parsimony rule

> **Prefer the least elaborate formulation that adequately explains the available evidence.**

Complexity must earn its place.

A useful escalation ladder is:

```text
LEVEL 0: IMMEDIATE CONTEXT
fatigue, workload, illness, logistics, time pressure, misunderstanding

        ↓ if insufficient

LEVEL 1: ORDINARY RELATIONSHIP PROCESS
different preferences, expectations, communication habits,
conflicting standards, poor timing, low responsiveness

        ↓ if repeated evidence warrants

LEVEL 2: REGULATORY OR INTERPERSONAL MECHANISM
flooding, reassurance seeking, avoidance, shame,
negative attribution, rigid conflict strategy

        ↓ if repeated evidence warrants

LEVEL 3: ATTACHMENT / MENTALIZATION / IDENTITY FORMULATION
hyperactivation, deactivation, attachment alarm,
collapse of reflective capacity, identity threat

        ↓ if useful and evidentially warranted

LEVEL 4: DEPTH-PSYCHOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION
complex, projection, projective identification,
relational enactment, shared phantasy, fantasy, jouissance
```

Do not move upward merely because the higher-level explanation sounds more psychologically sophisticated.

### Example

**Observation:** A partner comes home, says little, showers and goes to bed early.

Possible explanations include:

1. exhaustion;
2. illness;
3. anger;
4. desire for solitude;
5. conflict avoidance;
6. attachment deactivation;
7. punitive withdrawal;
8. relational enactment.

If the person has worked a 14-hour shift and this behaviour is unusual, exhaustion may adequately explain the episode.

Do not automatically invoke:

- attachment deactivation;
- narcissistic injury;
- projective identification;
- abandonment complex;
- Lacanian fantasy.

If the same form of withdrawal appears repeatedly whenever intimacy or conflict increases, more complex formulations become more relevant.

---

## 7. Complexity must add explanatory value

A deeper formulation should answer a question that a simpler one cannot.

Ask:

- What additional observation does this theory explain?
- Does it explain repetition?
- Does it explain why this trigger, rather than another, matters?
- Does it predict anything observable?
- Does it suggest a different useful test?
- Does it clarify the person’s own experience?
- Does it improve action?
- Or is it simply redescribing the same behaviour in more elaborate language?

If the theory adds no incremental explanatory value, leave it out.

---

## 8. Safety gate

Before analysing an ordinary reciprocal couple cycle, ask whether credible evidence suggests:

- physical violence;
- sexual coercion;
- stalking;
- threats;
- intimidation;
- coercive control;
- serious financial control;
- fear of retaliation;
- deliberate isolation;
- monitoring designed to control;
- punishment for asserting boundaries;
- credible concern that disclosure would increase danger.

### If NO

Proceed with ordinary reciprocal-cycle analysis.

### If YES OR PLAUSIBLY YES

Change the frame.

```text
POSSIBLE COERCION / VIOLENCE / RETALIATION
               |
               v
DO NOT MAKE RECIPROCITY THE MAIN EXPLANATION
               |
               v
IDENTIFY ASYMMETRY AND SPECIFIC BEHAVIOURS
               |
               v
ASSESS SAFETY OF DISCLOSURE / CONFRONTATION
               |
               v
PRIORITISE SAFETY, SUPPORT AND SPECIALIST HELP
```

Do not recommend vulnerable disclosure to an unsafe partner.

Do not use:

- “you both contribute to the cycle”;
- attachment insecurity;
- communication style;
- childhood wounds;
- projective identification;

to dilute responsibility for coercive or violent conduct.

A relationship can contain a cycle and still contain strongly asymmetric responsibility.

## 9. Responsibility is not averaged across the couple

Use a behaviour-by-behaviour responsibility map.

| Question | Person A | Person B |
|---|---|---|
| What behaviour occurred? |  |  |
| Was it voluntary? |  |  |
| What impact did it have? |  |  |
| Was a clear agreement broken? |  |  |
| Was deception involved? |  |  |
| Was coercion involved? |  |  |
| What did the person do after learning the impact? |  |  |
| Was responsibility accepted? |  |  |
| Was behaviour changed? |  |  |
| Did retaliation occur? |  |  |

A cycle may be reciprocal while responsibility remains uneven.

Example:

```text
A conceals debt
      ↓
B discovers it and repeatedly demands explanations
      ↓
A withdraws
      ↓
B pursues harder
      ↓
A becomes defensive
```

The pursuit-withdrawal cycle may be real.

It does not make the original concealment equally attributable to both partners.

## 10. Mandatory episode analysis protocol

The following protocol is the default deep-analysis sequence.

---

# A. WHAT HAPPENED?

### Objective

Create a minimally interpreted chronology.

### Record

- who did what;
- when;
- where;
- what immediately preceded it;
- what immediately followed it;
- what external constraints were present;
- what evidence is direct;
- what evidence is reconstructed from memory.

### For a text-message exchange

Preserve where available:

- timestamps;
- exact wording;
- sequence;
- gaps between messages;
- deleted or edited messages if known;
- prior conversation;
- whether the parties were working, sleeping, travelling or otherwise unavailable.

Do not infer too much from:

- punctuation;
- message length;
- delayed response;
- lack of emoji;
- wording that can plausibly support several tones.

### Output

> **OBSERVATION:**  
> At 18:04 A asked whether B was coming home. B replied at 21:46, “Still at work. Will call when I leave.” A sent four further messages between 19:10 and 21:15.

Do not write:

> “B ignored A for nearly four hours because B wanted emotional distance.”

That contains multiple unverified inferences.

---

# B. WHAT DID EACH PERSON ACTUALLY SAY OR REPORT?

Separate:

1. recorded words;
2. remembered paraphrase;
3. later explanation;
4. inferred meaning.

Use a table.

| Source | Directly said or reported | Status |
|---|---|---|
| A during episode | “You never think about me.” | direct wording |
| B during episode | “I cannot do this while I am working.” | direct wording |
| A later | “I was frightened something had changed.” | subjective report |
| B later | “I was overwhelmed and trying to finish a task.” | subjective report |
| Interpretation | “B wanted to punish A.” | inference only |

A later explanation can be relevant without being automatically accepted as complete.

Human beings may:

- misunderstand themselves;
- remember selectively;
- have mixed motives;
- change their interpretation later.

The correct response is not cynical disbelief.

It is calibrated epistemic status.

---

# C. WHAT DID I EXPERIENCE?

Map the experience in several channels.

| Channel | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **Emotion** | What did I feel first? What became visible later? |
| **Body** | Tightness, heat, racing heart, sinking, numbness, agitation, shutdown? |
| **Thought** | What went through my mind? |
| **Meaning** | What did the event seem to mean? |
| **Fear** | What did I fear was happening or would happen? |
| **Desire** | What did I want from the other person? |
| **Action tendency** | Approach, protest, attack, flee, freeze, appease, seek reassurance? |
| **Agency** | Did I still feel capable of choosing how to respond? |

Distinguish:

```text
EVENT:       No reply for three hours.
EMOTION:     Anxiety.
BODY:        Tight chest.
THOUGHT:     “Something is wrong.”
MEANING:     “I am becoming unimportant.”
FEAR:        Rejection.
DESIRE:      Reassurance and contact.
BEHAVIOUR:   Repeated messaging.
```

This distinction prevents a feeling from being mistaken for proof.

“I felt abandoned” is important evidence about subjective experience.

It does not prove that abandonment occurred.

---

# D. WHAT MIGHT THE OTHER PERSON HAVE EXPERIENCED?

This step is mandatory and hypothetical.

Generate at least two plausible alternatives when the other person’s internal state is not directly known.

Example:

**Observed behaviour:** B became quiet and left the room.

Possible experiences:

1. B may have felt flooded and needed reduced stimulation.
2. B may have been angry and unwilling to continue.
3. B may have wanted to punish A through withdrawal.
4. B may have feared saying something damaging.
5. B may simply have wanted temporary solitude.

Do not choose between these solely from theory.

Preferred format:

> **PARTNER HYPOTHESES, LOW CONFIDENCE:**  
> The withdrawal could reflect overload, anger, conflict avoidance, deliberate distancing, or a wish to regulate before continuing. B’s internal experience is not established by the behaviour alone.

The partner-opacity rule is central.

Mentalization means becoming more precise about uncertainty, not becoming more certain about another mind.

---

# E. WHAT DID EACH PERSON DO NEXT?

Describe behaviour rather than inferred personality.

Examples:

- asked a question;
- criticised;
- repeated the question;
- reassured;
- explained;
- defended;
- left;
- followed;
- apologised;
- became silent;
- raised their voice;
- touched the other person;
- changed the topic;
- checked a phone;
- cancelled plans;
- returned at an agreed time;
- did not return.

Behaviour provides the bridge between subjective experience and the dyadic cycle.

---

# F. WHAT CYCLE EMERGED?

Build the smallest sequence that captures the interaction.

```text
TRIGGER
  ↓
A'S MEANING
  ↓
A'S EMOTION / AROUSAL
  ↓
A'S ACTION
  ↓
B'S MEANING
  ↓
B'S EMOTION / AROUSAL
  ↓
B'S ACTION
  ↓
A'S NEW MEANING
  ↓
NEXT ROUND
```

Example:

```text
B becomes quiet
      ↓
A interprets distance as rejection
      ↓
A becomes anxious
      ↓
A asks repeatedly what is wrong
      ↓
B experiences pressure
      ↓
B becomes irritated
      ↓
B withdraws further
      ↓
A experiences stronger evidence of rejection
      ↓
A pursues harder
```

The cycle is an emergent process.

It is not a diagnosis.

It is not proof that either partner has a stable attachment style.

It is not a statement of equal responsibility.

---

# G. WHAT BROAD RELATIONSHIP-SCIENCE MECHANISMS FIT?

Before using deeper clinical theories, consider broad relationship processes.

Finkel, Simpson and Eastwick organise relationship science around principles including uniqueness, interdependence, trajectory, evaluation, responsiveness, resolution, maintenance, predispositions, goals and needs, standards, diagnostic situations, alternatives, stress and culture.

Ask:

### Uniqueness
Is this interaction specific to this pairing rather than simply a trait of either person?

### Interdependence
How do the partners’ choices alter each other’s incentives, emotions or options?

### Trajectory
Has the meaning of similar interactions changed over time?

### Evaluation
What positive and negative judgments about the relationship are becoming active?

### Responsiveness
Does each person experience the other as understanding, caring and responsive?

### Resolution
What happens when difficult events are discussed?

### Maintenance
What everyday actions sustain or weaken the bond?

### Predispositions
What individual tendencies are relevant without treating them as destiny?

### Instrumentality
What needs or goals is each partner trying to pursue?

### Standards
What does each believe a partner or relationship should provide?

### Diagnosticity
Does this situation genuinely reveal something important, or is it ambiguous?

### Alternatives
Are other relationships, lifestyles or being single psychologically salient?

### Stress
Are external demands exceeding available coping resources?

### Culture
What social, family, gender, religious or cultural expectations shape the issue?

Large multi-study research also supports epistemic restraint. Relationship-specific self-reports can explain substantial variation in current relationship quality, but change in relationship quality over time proved much harder to predict. Partner reports and individual differences added much less predictive information once a person’s own relationship-specific experience was considered.

Therefore:

> A current pattern can be formulated. A future relationship trajectory should not be confidently forecast from it.

---

# H. WHAT ATTACHMENT, REGULATION OR MENTALIZATION MECHANISMS FIT?

Use these frameworks when the evidence supports them.

### Attachment questions

- Did the event involve availability, responsiveness or dependability?
- Did separation, uncertainty or vulnerability activate disproportionate alarm?
- Did the person intensify proximity-seeking?
- Did the person suppress need or distance from dependence?
- Is this response recurrent?
- Does it occur specifically in attachment-relevant contexts?
- Does it change when reliability increases?

Prefer dimensional language:

- attachment anxiety;
- attachment avoidance;
- hyperactivating strategy;
- deactivating strategy;
- security-related regulation.

Do not convert one behaviour into a fixed attachment identity.

---

### Regulation questions

- Was either person physiologically overwhelmed?
- Was the behaviour an attempt to reduce arousal?
- Did anger amplify a more vulnerable emotion?
- Did reassurance briefly reduce distress but increase future reassurance seeking?
- Did withdrawal successfully terminate uncomfortable interaction and therefore become reinforced?
- Could self-soothing have altered the sequence?
- Could co-regulation have altered it?

---

### Mentalization questions

- Can I distinguish what happened from what I think it meant?
- Can I imagine more than one explanation for the other person’s behaviour?
- Can I revise my interpretation when new evidence arrives?
- Am I treating my fear as direct knowledge?
- Am I treating the other person’s statement as the whole truth or as one piece of evidence?
- Does high arousal make certainty increase while curiosity decreases?
- Can I still perceive the other person as having a mind separate from mine?

A useful mentalizing statement is:

> “My strongest fear is that this means X. I do not yet know that X is true.”

---

# I. WHAT IDENTITY, POWER OR RECOGNITION PROCESSES FIT?

Attachment is central but not total.

Ask:

- Does the conflict threaten connection?
- Does it threaten identity or dignity?
- Does one person feel unseen, diminished or mischaracterised?
- Is the issue partly about influence or control?
- Whose definition of reality dominates?
- Can both partners disagree without one losing legitimacy?
- Who is expected to adapt?
- Who receives influence?
- Is practical or emotional labour distributed in a way that matters to the conflict?
- Is autonomy treated as rejection?
- Is closeness treated as entitlement?
- Can each person remain a separate subject?

These concepts should remain clinical or interpretive lenses, not presumed facts about a case.

---

# J. WHAT ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS FIT?

For important ambiguous behaviour, generate at least two or three plausible formulations.

Use the following table.

| Hypothesis | Supporting evidence | Contradicting evidence | Missing evidence | Confidence | What would discriminate it? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 |  |  |  |  |  |
| H2 |  |  |  |  |  |
| H3 |  |  |  |  |  |

Alternatives should be genuinely different.

Poor alternatives:

- “attachment anxiety”;
- “fear of abandonment”;
- “hyperactivation”.

These largely restate one mechanism.

Better alternatives for repeated reassurance seeking:

1. attachment-related hyperactivation;
2. realistic response to a genuinely unreliable partner;
3. anxiety amplified by recent betrayal;
4. learned conflict strategy that intermittently succeeds;
5. temporary stress reducing uncertainty tolerance.

A formulation should not pathologise a reaction that may be proportionate to the actual environment.

---

# K. WHAT DEPTH-PSYCHOLOGICAL LENSES MIGHT ADD?

This step is optional.

Do not use depth psychology merely because it is available.

### Jungian lens

Possible questions:

- Does the intensity suggest a recurring complex?
- Is the present partner carrying meanings accumulated from earlier relationships?
- Could projection be contributing to idealisation, demonisation or misrecognition?
- What part of the person’s own psyche is difficult to recognise directly?
- Does the situation expose a conflict between adaptation and individuation?
- Does a dream compensate for a narrow waking attitude?

Rules:

- complex ≠ attachment internal working model;
- projection ≠ proof that the other person is innocent;
- shadow ≠ bad behaviour;
- individuation ≠ detachment;
- dream symbolism ≠ external evidence.

---

### Relational psychoanalytic lens

Possible questions:

- What self-state becomes dominant?
- What version of the other person becomes expected?
- Is the couple repeatedly enacting complementary positions?
- Does each person help evoke the state they fear in the other?
- Can mutual recognition survive conflict?
- What cannot yet be jointly thought about?

Keep enactment as a relational hypothesis, not an excuse.

---

### Object-relations / Tavistock lens

Possible questions:

- Is the couple organising experience through shared unconscious assumptions?
- Are unwanted states being located predominantly in one partner?
- Is the relationship being used to contain something difficult for one or both people?
- Has the couple lost the ability to observe the relationship as a shared object of reflection?

Projective identification requires more than saying:

> “I projected something onto my partner.”

Do not collapse projection and projective identification.

---

### Lacanian lens

Possible questions:

- What demand is being made through the explicit request?
- What desire remains unsatisfied even if the request is met?
- What repetitive scenario gives the conflict its particular form?
- What position does the person repeatedly occupy in relation to the Other?
- What form of enjoyment or suffering is being repeated?
- What fantasy makes the partner’s behaviour legible in a particular way?

Rules:

- demand ≠ ordinary request;
- desire ≠ preference;
- fantasy ≠ daydream;
- jouissance ≠ pleasure;
- Lacanian interpretation must not be presented as empirical fact.

---

# L. WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN?

Create an uncertainty register.

Possible categories:

| Unknown | Why it matters | How it might become clearer |
|---|---|---|
| Partner’s actual intention | Several hypotheses depend on it | direct conversation, repeated behaviour |
| Whether withdrawal was deliberate punishment | affects responsibility formulation | pattern over time, explicit statements |
| Whether behaviour occurs across relationships | distinguishes relational specificity from broad tendency | longitudinal history |
| Whether work stress was unusually high | may support contextual explanation | external timeline |
| Whether agreement was mutually understood | affects boundary/betrayal interpretation | reconstruct prior conversation |

Unknown is not a defect in the formulation.

Unknown is part of the formulation.

---

# M. WHAT EVIDENCE WOULD DISTINGUISH THE HYPOTHESES?

A good formulation produces possible discriminators.

Examples:

### Hypothesis
Withdrawal mainly reflects physiological overload.

### Discriminating evidence
Does withdrawal decrease when the conversation occurs at a lower-arousal time with a structured pause?

---

### Hypothesis
Withdrawal mainly reflects low willingness to engage with the relationship.

### Discriminating evidence
Does disengagement remain high even when timing, arousal and communication conditions improve?

---

### Hypothesis
Reassurance seeking mainly reflects uncertainty about contact.

### Discriminating evidence
Does a predictable communication agreement substantially reduce it?

---

### Hypothesis
Reassurance seeking reflects a broader attachment-related pattern.

### Discriminating evidence
Does the same alarm repeatedly appear despite reliable contact and across several attachment-relevant situations?

---

# N. WHAT COULD BE TRIED?

Move from interpretation to controllable action.

Possible categories:

- observation;
- self-regulation;
- direct request;
- attribution check;
- timing change;
- behavioural agreement;
- boundary clarification;
- repair attempt;
- trust-building behaviour;
- acceptance of a persistent difference;
- professional support;
- postponing a conclusion until more evidence exists.

The action should fit the formulation.

Do not prescribe emotional exposure merely because vulnerability is theoretically desirable.

---

# O. WHAT HAPPENED AFTERWARD?

This step converts reflection into learning.

Record:

- what was tried;
- what each person actually did;
- what changed;
- what did not change;
- whether arousal changed;
- whether the cycle changed;
- whether trust changed;
- whether the original hypothesis gained or lost support;
- whether a new problem appeared;
- whether the action was sustainable.

One successful interaction does not prove permanent change.

One failed attempt does not prove impossibility.

Look for pattern.

## 12. Couple-cycle mapping

A cycle should be written as a process, not as two personality labels.

Prefer:

> When A experiences distance as danger, A intensifies contact. B experiences the intensified contact as pressure and retreats. The retreat increases A’s alarm.

Avoid:

> A is anxious and B is avoidant.

The first formulation describes a potentially changeable interaction.

The second risks turning provisional mechanisms into fixed identities.

---

## 13. Cycle-map format

| Step | Observable cue | Meaning | Emotion/body | Behaviour | Effect on partner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | B becomes quiet | A: “Something is wrong” | anxiety | asks repeatedly | B feels more pressure |
| 2 | A asks repeatedly | B: “I cannot get space” | tension | withdraws | A sees more distance |
| 3 | B withdraws | A: “I am being rejected” | stronger alarm | pursues | B withdraws further |
| 4 | escalation | both narrow attention | high arousal | attack/defend | cycle consolidates |

The purpose is not to decide whose feelings are valid.

The purpose is to identify how the sequence maintains itself.

---

## 14. Common cycle forms

These are descriptive patterns, not diagnoses.

### Pursue → withdraw

```text
distance
→ alarm
→ pursuit
→ pressure
→ withdrawal
→ stronger alarm
```

### Attack → defend

```text
complaint
→ perceived accusation
→ defence
→ perceived invalidation
→ stronger attack
```

### Protest → reassure → doubt → protest

```text
uncertainty
→ protest
→ reassurance
→ short relief
→ renewed uncertainty
→ stronger reassurance seeking
```

### Withdraw → pursue → shutdown

```text
stress
→ retreat
→ pursuit
→ overload
→ shutdown
→ panic / anger
```

### Injury → protest → defensiveness → re-injury

```text
old rupture recalled
→ pain / accusation
→ defensive explanation
→ impact feels unrecognised
→ injury becomes more salient
```

### Control → resistance → stronger control

```text
fear of disorder / loss
→ controlling action
→ resistance
→ perceived unreliability
→ stronger control
```

### Mutual avoidance

```text
difficult issue
→ both anticipate conflict
→ neither raises it
→ short-term calm
→ unresolved issue grows
→ greater future threat
```

## 15. A cycle is not necessarily the problem

Sometimes the central problem is substantive.

Examples:

- one wants children and the other does not;
- one wants monogamy and the other does not;
- incompatible financial risk tolerance;
- incompatible geographic plans;
- incompatible expectations of family involvement;
- incompatible sexual preferences or frequency expectations;
- fundamentally different views about privacy;
- repeated unwillingness to honour an agreed boundary.

Better communication may clarify an incompatibility without eliminating it.

Do not convert every conflict into a dysfunctional cycle that therapy should dissolve.

## 16. The competing-hypothesis engine

For significant ambiguous behaviour, require at least two or three serious hypotheses.

### Minimum standard

Each hypothesis must contain:

1. supporting evidence;
2. contradicting evidence;
3. missing evidence;
4. confidence;
5. discriminating evidence;
6. different implications for action.

### Example: repeated emotional withdrawal

| | Hypothesis 1 | Hypothesis 2 | Hypothesis 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Formulation** | flooding / overload | attachment deactivation | low engagement or unresolved resentment |
| **Support** | withdrawal follows high arousal | withdrawal follows intimacy demands repeatedly | withdrawal occurs across many relational contexts |
| **Contradiction** | sometimes occurs before conflict | person sometimes seeks closeness | person still invests strongly elsewhere in relationship |
| **Missing** | physiological/arousal data | broader attachment pattern | direct account of motivation |
| **Confidence** | MODERATE | LOW-MODERATE | LOW |
| **Discriminator** | structured low-arousal discussion | repeated response to closeness across episodes | engagement when conflict pressure is removed |
| **Action implication** | regulate and pause | increase safe direct connection | clarify willingness and unresolved grievance |

The point is not to create artificial uncertainty.

If a fact is clear, state it.

Competing hypotheses are most important for **meaning and motive**.

---

## 17. Do not explain away evidence

Alternative explanations should not become a method for avoiding uncomfortable conclusions.

Example:

If one partner repeatedly:

- promises to stop a behaviour;
- violates the agreement;
- conceals the violation;
- minimises the impact;
- refuses accountability;

it is unnecessary to generate elaborate alternative explanations for whether the behaviour happened.

The ambiguity may concern:

- motive;
- capacity to change;
- emotional meaning;
- future trajectory.

It does not concern a well-established behavioural record.

## 18. Relationship-science mechanism router

Use this before depth psychology.

### 18.1 Responsiveness

Ask:

- Did the person feel understood?
- Did the partner acknowledge the concern?
- Did behaviour convey care?
- Was the response proportionate to the need?
- Was responsiveness expected but unavailable?

---

### 18.2 Conflict resolution

Ask:

- Was the issue addressed or merely ended?
- Did either person become defensive?
- Did the conversation stay specific?
- Was there a usable compromise?
- Did the conflict produce new information?
- Was an agreement actually implemented?

---

### 18.3 Maintenance

Look for ordinary repeated behaviours:

- attention;
- reliability;
- practical support;
- affection;
- humour;
- companionship;
- shared time;
- household labour;
- sexual connection;
- everyday repair.

Do not assume that the psychologically deepest explanation is the most consequential one.

---

### 18.4 Standards

Ask:

- What counts as “enough” contact?
- What does loyalty mean?
- What does privacy mean?
- What is expected around sex?
- What does a fair division of labour look like?
- What does a committed partner “should” do?
- Were these standards explicitly negotiated or silently assumed?

Conflicting standards can produce distress without either person having a disorder.

---

### 18.5 Stress

Ask:

- Has work intensified?
- Is sleep poor?
- Is there financial strain?
- Is there caregiving burden?
- Are children, illness, relocation or family pressures relevant?
- Has available time for intimacy collapsed?
- Has the couple lost previously effective routines?

External stress should be tested before a one-off episode is attributed to deep personality structure.

---

### 18.6 Trajectory

Ask:

- Is this new?
- Is it becoming more frequent?
- Is intensity increasing?
- Is repair becoming easier or harder?
- Has trust changed?
- Are positive interactions disappearing?
- Are the same arguments acquiring new meanings?

Trajectory is more informative than one snapshot.

## 19. Attachment router

Use attachment when the episode concerns:

- access to the partner;
- availability;
- responsiveness;
- dependence;
- comfort;
- separation;
- vulnerability;
- trust at moments of need.

### Possible hyperactivation markers

These may include:

- escalating bids for contact;
- repeated reassurance seeking;
- intensified protest;
- preoccupation with ambiguous signs;
- difficulty disengaging from attachment threat.

These behaviours do not automatically prove attachment anxiety.

They may also be realistic reactions to inconsistency or betrayal.

---

### Possible deactivation markers

These may include:

- suppressing attachment needs;
- distancing under dependency pressure;
- dismissing bids for closeness;
- turning attention away from vulnerability;
- emphasising self-sufficiency during threat.

These behaviours do not automatically prove attachment avoidance.

They may also reflect:

- exhaustion;
- anger;
- poor timing;
- fear of escalation;
- ordinary preference for space.

---

## 20. Regulation router

Ask:

```text
What arousal state was present?
        |
        +-- manageable → reflection remains possible
        |
        +-- high → narrowing, impulsive action, attack or pursuit
        |
        +-- overwhelming → shutdown, escape, numbness or dissociation-like experience
```

Then ask:

- What behaviour reduced distress immediately?
- Did that behaviour worsen the relationship later?
- What behaviour from the partner increased arousal?
- What behaviour helped?
- Did reassurance produce durable security or only brief relief?
- Did withdrawal regulate one person by dysregulating the other?
- Did the interaction require self-regulation, co-regulation, or both?

---

## 21. Mentalization router

Use a seven-question test.

1. What did I observe?
2. What did I feel?
3. What did I infer?
4. What did the other person actually say?
5. What alternative interpretation fits?
6. What am I uncertain about?
7. What evidence could change my mind?

### Signs of better mentalization

- curiosity;
- revisability;
- tolerance of ambiguity;
- distinction between feeling and fact;
- recognition that minds can contain mixed motives.

### Signs of reduced mentalization

- certainty without evidence;
- “I know exactly why you did that”;
- converting one behaviour into a total character judgment;
- assuming a partner’s internal state from one cue;
- refusing disconfirming evidence;
- treating one’s own fear as transparent knowledge.

---

## 22. Identity, recognition and power router

Ask four groups of questions.

### Identity

- What self-definition feels threatened?
- Is the person being treated as incompetent, selfish, needy, cold, weak, controlling, irresponsible or undesirable?
- Is shame active?
- Is disagreement experienced as personal diminishment?

### Recognition

- Can each person represent the other’s position fairly?
- Can each acknowledge the other as a separate subject?
- Is validation possible without agreement?
- Does one person have to disappear psychologically for the other to feel secure?

### Influence

- Can either partner change the other’s mind?
- Is influence reciprocal?
- Does one person routinely concede?
- Does one person define the terms of every difficult discussion?

### Power

- Who controls important resources?
- Who controls timing?
- Who decides when a conversation is over?
- Who defines what counts as a legitimate need?
- Can refusal occur without punishment?
- Can boundaries be asserted safely?

---

## 23. The action layer

Every applied formulation should end by separating five domains.

| Domain | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **I CAN CONTROL** | my behaviour, pacing, boundaries, wording, decisions, self-regulation, whether I participate |
| **I CAN INFLUENCE** | conditions for conversation, clarity, invitations, reinforcement, timing, relational climate |
| **I CAN ASK ABOUT** | partner’s experience, intention, needs, willingness, interpretation |
| **I CANNOT KNOW YET** | unreported motives, future behaviour, whether a new pattern will persist |
| **I CANNOT CONTROL** | another adult’s choices, feelings, honesty, commitment, willingness to change |

This map protects agency from two common errors:

1. trying to control another person by perfecting one’s own psychology;
2. treating oneself as powerless because another person’s behaviour cannot be controlled.

---

## 24. Repair experiments

These are possible low-risk experiments, not universal prescriptions.

| Experiment | Hypothesis being tested | Observable result |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct request instead of protest** | protest form contributes to defensiveness | does the partner respond differently to a clear request? |
| **Structured time-out plus return time** | flooding maintains escalation | does reliable return reduce pursuit and shutdown? |
| **Validate impact before intention** | explanation is being heard as invalidation | does acknowledging impact reduce defensive repetition? |
| **Check an attribution** | mind-reading is escalating the cycle | does asking clarify the meaning? |
| **Name primary emotion** | reactive anger obscures the central concern | does clearer vulnerability change the interaction? |
| **Slow physiological arousal** | high activation is reducing flexibility | does lower arousal improve thinking and listening? |
| **Boundary clarification** | expectations are ambiguous | does explicit agreement reduce recurrence? |
| **Observable behavioural commitment** | verbal repair lacks behavioural specificity | is the promised behaviour actually implemented? |
| **Repeated trust-building action** | trust requires reliability over time | does consistent follow-through gradually change expectations? |

A test is useful when the outcome can update the formulation.

---

## 25. How to interpret an experiment

Do not reason:

> “I used perfect communication and my partner still reacted badly, therefore the partner is pathological.”

Instead ask:

- Was the experiment implemented as intended?
- Was the setting appropriate?
- Did the partner understand it?
- Was there enough repetition?
- Did anything improve?
- Did the result challenge the hypothesis?
- Does the partner show willingness but limited skill?
- Or is willingness itself absent?
- Did the experiment reveal a substantive incompatibility rather than a communication failure?

Behavioural testing should increase information, not become a covert test that the other person is expected to fail.

## 28. REUSABLE LAB TEMPLATE 3: PURSUIT / WITHDRAWAL

- silence;
- leaving;
- delaying discussion;
- changing subject;
- shutting down;
- physical distance.

### Formulate both sides

### Pursuer
Possible meanings:

- “Distance means danger.”
- “If I stop, the issue will disappear without resolution.”
- “I need proof that we are okay.”

Possible meanings:

- “Contact now means escalation.”
- “I cannot think under this pressure.”
- “Anything I say will make this worse.”

- attachment hyperactivation;

- flooding;
- deactivation;

- strategic avoidance;
- punitive silence;
- need for ordinary solitude.

## 30. REUSABLE LAB TEMPLATE 5: ATTACHMENT INJURY

Use this term narrowly.

An attachment injury refers to perceived abandonment, betrayal or breach of trust at a critical moment of need that becomes a recurring relational theme and impasse.

### Ask

1. Was there a specific event?
2. Was one partner particularly vulnerable or in need?
3. Was support expected from the partner?
4. Was that support experienced as unavailable or betraying?
5. Does the event repeatedly organise current conflict?
6. Has the event become a standard for judging present dependability?

If not, prefer a less specific term such as:

- rupture;
- disappointment;
- trust problem;
- unresolved conflict.

### Repair markers

Process research on attachment-injury resolution describes movement from a live, emotionally charged injury and defensive response toward clearer articulation of impact, emotional engagement, responsibility, empathy and eventually a new caring response.

Do not infer that every injury can or should be reconciled.

## 31. REUSABLE LAB TEMPLATE 6: AFFAIR / BETRAYAL

### First establish facts

- What happened?
- What agreement existed?
- What was concealed?
- For how long?
- Is the account agreed?
- Are there unresolved factual discrepancies?
- Is contact with the third party continuing?
- Have new disclosures emerged?

### Separate four questions

### 1. What happened?
Facts.

### 2. What did it mean?
Different meanings may exist.

### 3. What responsibility exists?
Do not diffuse responsibility through couple-system language.

### 4. What happens now?
Repair, renegotiation, uncertainty and ending are separate possibilities.

### Do not assume

- the affair proves the relationship was already dead;
- relationship dissatisfaction caused the affair;
- the injured partner caused the betrayal;
- forgiveness is required;
- reconciliation is required;
- ending the relationship means repair failed.

### Evidence to track

- honesty;
- accountability;
- empathy;
- cessation of violating behaviour;
- transparency;
- tolerance of legitimate questions;
- reduced defensiveness;
- repeated reliability;
- respect for boundaries.

## 33. REUSABLE LAB TEMPLATE 8: EMOTIONAL WITHDRAWAL

- timing;
- antecedent;

### Possible mechanisms

## 42. REUSABLE LAB TEMPLATE 17: ACTIVE-IMAGINATION MATERIAL

### Purpose

Explore an image, affect, figure or inner dialogue without treating it as literal external information.

### Protocol

1. identify the image or figure;
2. note bodily and emotional response;
3. let the image develop without deliberately scripting the desired answer;
4. record what occurs;
5. maintain awareness that the material is imaginal;
6. ask what it expresses psychologically;
7. compare with waking concerns;
8. consider several interpretations;
9. integrate through reflection or modest action.

### Required distinctions

- imaginal figure ≠ external person;
- imaginal statement ≠ factual revelation;
- symbolic encounter ≠ instruction;
- emotional intensity ≠ truth value.

Stop if the exercise substantially erodes ordinary reality testing or becomes destabilising.

## 45. Updating confidence over time

```text

   |

   |
```

## 47. Apology analysis

Analyse an apology through components.

| Component | Question |
|---|---|
| **Specificity** | Is the behaviour named? |
| **Impact** | Is the other person’s experience recognised? |
| **Responsibility** | Is responsibility accepted without displacement? |
| **Remorse** | Is regret expressed? |
| **Explanation** | Is context offered without turning it into an excuse? |
| **Change** | What will be different? |
| **Restitution** | Is any practical repair required? |
| **Follow-through** | Does later behaviour support the words? |

Distinguish:

- apology ≠ repair;
- remorse ≠ behavioural change;
- explanation ≠ excuse;
- forgiveness ≠ reconciliation.

## 48. Failed repair

A repair attempt may fail for different reasons.

### Type 1: timing failure

The content may be reasonable but offered while arousal is still too high.

### Type 2: recognition failure

The response addresses intention rather than impact.

> “I did not mean it that way.”

may be true but insufficient.

### Type 3: responsibility failure

The person apologises while shifting causation back to the injured partner.

> “I am sorry, but you made me…”

### Type 4: behavioural failure

Words change, behaviour does not.

### Type 5: trust-time failure

The repair may be genuine but insufficiently repeated for expectations to update.

### Type 6: incompatibility

The “repair problem” is actually a continuing disagreement that cannot be solved by apology.

### Type 7: safety problem

The behaviour is coercive or abusive, making ordinary repair framing inappropriate.

## 49. Trust as a longitudinal variable

Do not ask only:

> “Do I trust the person?”

Break trust into domains.

- truthfulness;
- reliability;
- emotional availability;
- sexual fidelity where relevant;
- financial reliability;
- respect for privacy;
- respect for boundaries;
- dependability during vulnerability;
- consistency between words and behaviour.

Trust can recover unevenly.

A person may trust a partner:

- practically;
- but not emotionally;
- financially;
- but not sexually;
- in ordinary circumstances;
- but not during conflict.

Use behavioural specificity.

## 50. Relationship decision support

The laboratory does not calculate a relationship “score”.

It maps evidence.

### 50.1 Safety

Ask:

- Is disagreement safe?
- Can either partner say no?
- Is there intimidation?
- Is there retaliation?
- Is disclosure safe?

Safety has priority over ordinary cycle work.

---

### 50.2 Reciprocity

Ask:

- Do both partners accommodate?
- Do both initiate care?
- Can both receive support?
- Is sacrifice persistently one-directional?

---

### 50.3 Accountability

Ask:

- Can each person name their own behaviour?
- Is responsibility acknowledged without immediate counterattack?
- Are repeated harms explained away?

---

### 50.4 Demonstrated behaviour change

Ask:

- What changed in observable behaviour?
- How long has it persisted?
- Does change survive stress?
- Is change voluntary or dependent on surveillance?

---

### 50.5 Trust

Ask:

- Is dependability increasing?
- Are new violations occurring?
- Are facts becoming clearer or more confusing?

---

### 50.6 Repair capacity

Ask:

- Can difficult events be discussed?
- Can impact be acknowledged?
- Does the couple return after conflict?
- Are repair attempts recognised?
- Does resentment accumulate despite discussion?

---

### 50.7 Boundary respect

Ask:

- Are boundaries understood?
- Are they respected when inconvenient?
- Is refusal accepted?

---

### 50.8 Compatibility

Ask:

- Are major goals aligned enough?
- Which differences are negotiable?
- Which require one partner to abandon a core value?

---

### 50.9 Willingness to work

Skill and willingness are different.

Someone may:

- want change but lack skill;
- have skill but lack willingness;
- agree verbally but not behaviourally;
- sincerely try and still be unable to meet a partner’s needs.

---

### 50.10 Impact on agency

Ask:

> “Who do I become in this relationship?”

Indicators of agency include:

- capacity to think;
- ability to say no;
- ability to tolerate disagreement;
- freedom to maintain relationships outside the couple;
- ability to act consistently with values;
- ability to make decisions without fear;
- capacity to distinguish desire from compliance.

## 53. Dream-analysis sequence

### Step 1: DREAM

Record the dream before interpretation.

### Step 2: EMOTION

Identify:

- dream emotion;
- waking emotion;
- strongest affective moment;
- bodily response.

### Step 3: ASSOCIATION

Use the dreamer’s own associations first.

Ask:

- What does this person remind me of?
- What is personal about this place?
- What does this object evoke?
- Where else have I felt this?

### Step 4: WAKING CONTEXT

Ask:

- What occurred in the previous day?
- What current concern resembles the dream?
- What unresolved issue is emotionally active?
- What relationship is salient?

### Step 5: CONTINUITY

Look for continuity of:

- emotion;
- concern;
- relationship;
- self-state;
- conflict;
- memory;
- role;
- expectation.

### Step 6: COMPETING MEANINGS

Generate several readings.

### Continuity hypothesis
The dream may dramatise current waking concerns.

### Emotional formulation
The dream may organise or intensify affectively salient material.

### Cognitive-metaphoric formulation
The dream may express a problem through figurative construction.

### Jungian formulation
The dream may compensate for a narrow conscious position or constellate symbolic material.

### Lacanian formulation
The dream may be explored through signifiers, desire, fantasy and associative links.

No one interpretation is privileged automatically.

### Step 7: ACTION / REFLECTION

Ask:

- What does the dream make easier to notice?
- What waking issue deserves attention?
- Is there a modest behavioural implication?
- Does the dream alter a question rather than answer it?

## 54. Dream partner-opacity rule

A dream about:

- betrayal;
- infidelity;
- abandonment;
- secret communication;
- aggression;
- another person;

is evidence that the dreamer dreamed that material.

It is not evidence that the external person actually:

- betrayed;
- cheated;
- lied;
- intends to leave;
- secretly desires someone;
- harbours a hidden motive.

A dream may reveal the dreamer’s:

- fear;
- concern;
- expectation;
- memory;
- fantasy;
- conflict;
- symbolic association.

External claims require external evidence.

## 56. Anti-rumination rule

Self-analysis becomes less useful when it produces:

- endless reinterpretation;
- ever more elaborate motives;
- compulsive rereading of messages;
- attempts to achieve certainty about another person’s mind;
- repeated theory-switching without new evidence;
- symbolic interpretation that replaces action;
- retrospective reconstruction unsupported by data.

Use the following stop rule:

```text
HAVE I IDENTIFIED A NEW OBSERVATION?
        |
       NO
        ↓
AM I GENERATING A TESTABLE DISTINCTION?
        |
       NO
        ↓
AM I IDENTIFYING A CONTROLLABLE ACTION?
        |
       NO
        ↓
STOP ADDING THEORY
```

Reflection should eventually return to life.

## 57. Multi-lens discipline

When several theories apply, present them in layers.

### Correct format

### Observation
B left the room during the argument.

### Subjective experience
A experienced the departure as abandonment.

### Relationship pattern
Similar arguments often become pursuit followed by withdrawal.

### Empirical / clinical formulation
High arousal, reassurance seeking and withdrawal may be mutually reinforcing.

### Theory-specific interpretation
A Jungian formulation might consider whether abandonment-related complex material is being constellated.

This preserves levels.

---

## 58. Theory soup is prohibited

Do not write:

> “Her anxious attachment activated her abandonment complex, producing projective identification and a Lacanian demand for the Other.”

This collapses frameworks.

Instead write:

> **Attachment formulation:** uncertainty appears to intensify proximity-seeking.  
> **Jungian formulation:** if similar affective constellations recur across situations, a complex formulation might be useful.  
> **Relational psychoanalytic formulation:** the interaction may be organising complementary states of pursuing and distancing.  
> **Lacanian formulation:** a separate analysis could ask what demand and desire are organised through the repeated appeal to the partner.

Different theories may describe overlapping phenomena without meaning the same thing.

## 59. Same behaviour, different mechanisms

Behaviour does not identify mechanism by itself.

| Behaviour | Possible mechanisms |
|---|---|
| repeated texting | reassurance seeking, practical urgency, realistic concern, anger, monitoring, habit |
| silence | flooding, reflection, anger, punishment, fatigue, avoidance |
| apology | remorse, conflict termination, appeasement, strategic impression management |
| jealousy | realistic threat, attachment alarm, identity threat, projection, possessiveness |
| sex avoidance | low desire, resentment, stress, pain, fear, attraction change, autonomy protection |
| pursuit | attachment alarm, accountability seeking, intolerance of unresolved issues, realistic mistrust |
| independence | secure autonomy, defensive detachment, practical necessity, value preference |

Mechanism requires context and pattern.

## 60. WORKED GENERIC CASE 1: SAME WITHDRAWAL, DIFFERENT MECHANISMS

### Case A

A person returns from a week of 12-hour shifts, eats little, says they are exhausted and falls asleep.

**HIGH CONFIDENCE:** marked fatigue is present.

**Parsimonious formulation:** external stress and exhaustion.

Attachment deactivation adds little unless withdrawal also appears systematically around closeness or conflict.

---

### Case B

A person is warm throughout the week but becomes distant every time their partner asks for emotional reassurance.

**MODERATE:** the withdrawal is specifically linked to attachment-relevant discussion.

Possible formulations:

1. overload during emotional conflict;
2. discomfort with dependency or vulnerability;
3. resentment about the style of reassurance seeking.

More evidence is needed.

---

### Case C

A person refuses conversation for days after disagreements and explicitly says the silence is intended to “teach you not to push me.”

The behaviour has a different functional meaning.

A general “withdrawer” label would hide the evidence that withdrawal is being used deliberately as interpersonal leverage.

---

## 61. WORKED GENERIC CASE 2: RECIPROCAL CYCLE WITHOUT EQUAL RESPONSIBILITY

One partner secretly incurs substantial shared debt.

The other discovers it and begins repeatedly questioning financial transactions.

The first partner feels monitored and withdraws from financial conversations.

The second becomes more suspicious and checks more frequently.

### Cycle

```text
concealment
→ discovery
→ mistrust
→ checking
→ withdrawal
→ more mistrust
```

The checking-withdrawal sequence is reciprocal.

Responsibility for the original deception is not reciprocal.

A useful formulation therefore holds both:

- the current cycle may intensify distress;
- the person who concealed the debt remains responsible for the concealment.

Reducing checking may eventually become relevant to recovery.

It should not be demanded as though mistrust appeared without cause.

---

## 62. WORKED GENERIC CASE 3: ATTACHMENT EXPLANATION VERSUS EXTERNAL STRESS

One partner starts responding less frequently to daytime messages.

The other experiences the change as evidence of declining commitment.

Two weeks earlier, the first partner had moved into a role requiring long periods without phone access.

### Hypothesis 1
Attachment-related distancing.

### Hypothesis 2
Work constraints account for the change.

### Test
The couple agrees on a predictable message before the phone-free period and a call afterward.

### Outcome
Reassurance-seeking drops sharply.

### Updated formulation
External unpredictability was probably contributing substantially.

This does not prove that attachment processes are absent.

It shows that the simpler contextual explanation has acquired stronger support.

## 63. WORKED GENERIC CASE 4: PARTNER OPACITY

A partner says:

> “I need some space this weekend.”

Possible meanings include:

- fatigue;
- desire for solitude;
- conflict avoidance;
- uncertainty about the relationship;
- wish to see friends;
- anger;
- need to think.

The statement does not establish:

> “They are emotionally avoidant and preparing to leave.”

A better next step is to ask what “space” means behaviourally:

- less messaging?
- no contact?
- separate activities?
- a pause in the argument?
- uncertainty about the relationship?

Clarification beats mind-reading.

## 64. WORKED GENERIC CASE 5: PROJECTION AS HYPOTHESIS

A person becomes convinced that their partner views them as incompetent whenever the partner offers practical advice.

The partner denies intending criticism.

The person reports having felt chronically treated as incompetent in previous environments.

### Observation
Advice repeatedly evokes shame and anger.

### Moderate formulation
The current communication style may be insufficiently attuned to a known sensitivity.

### Theory-specific Jungian hypothesis
Older material may be contributing to projection onto the partner.

### Important qualification
Projection would not establish that the partner’s behaviour is neutral.

The partner may genuinely communicate in a patronising way.

The external interaction and internal projection must both remain open to investigation.

## 65. WORKED GENERIC CASE 6: SUCCESSFUL BEHAVIOURAL TEST

A couple repeatedly escalates late at night.

One partner pursues immediate resolution.

The other eventually shuts down.

### Hypothesis
Physiological and cognitive overload are maintaining the cycle.

### Test
Either person may call a 45-minute pause, but must specify the return time.

### Outcome
Across four later conflicts:

- return occurs each time;
- pursuit decreases;
- neither partner leaves the conversation permanently;
- arguments become shorter.

### Interpretation
The result increases confidence that reliable time-outs reduce a major maintaining mechanism.

It does not prove the underlying disagreements are resolved.

---

## 66. WORKED GENERIC CASE 7: FAILED REPAIR

After insulting their partner during arguments, a person apologises the next morning.

The behaviour recurs every week.

The apologies sound sincere.

### Facts
Apology is repeated.

Behavioural change is not.

### Formulation
The couple has verbal repair without effective behavioural repair.

Possible next questions:

- Is arousal control inadequate?
- Are there no consequences for recurrence?
- Is the person willing but unable to change without additional help?
- Is accountability becoming ritualised without implementation?

The sincerity of the apology and insufficiency of the repair can both be true.

---

## 67. WORKED GENERIC CASE 8: DREAM WITH MULTIPLE HYPOTHESES

A person dreams that their partner hides a second phone.

They wake anxious.

There is no known evidence of infidelity.

### Dream affect
Suspicion and fear.

### Waking context
The couple recently argued about privacy.

### Hypothesis 1: continuity
The dream dramatises the waking privacy conflict.

### Hypothesis 2: broader self-pattern
The hidden phone symbolises uncertainty and lack of access to another person’s mind.

### Hypothesis 3: Jungian
The image may compensate for a conscious attempt to dismiss distrust too quickly.

### Optional Lacanian lens
The hidden object may be explored through what the dreamer imagines the partner possesses or desires outside the dreamer’s knowledge.

### What the dream does not establish
That a second phone exists.

## 68. WORKED GENERIC CASE 9: SUBSTANTIVE INCOMPATIBILITY

Two partners communicate respectfully.

They can regulate conflict.

Both understand the other accurately.

One wants children.

The other firmly does not.

Repeated conversations increase clarity but do not produce convergence.

### Formulation

The core problem is not necessarily:

- insecure attachment;
- poor communication;
- insufficient vulnerability;
- fear of intimacy;
- pathological rigidity.

It may be a substantive incompatibility involving major life goals.

Therapeutic or reflective work may clarify the consequences.

It cannot guarantee a compromise where no mutually acceptable middle position exists.

## 78. Analysis of text-message exchanges

Text exchanges invite overinterpretation because the data look precise.

Use precision carefully.

### Extract

- timestamp;
- sender;
- exact text;
- questions;
- requests;
- accusations;
- reassurance;
- response latency;
- topic shifts;
- repair attempts.

### Do not infer automatically

- sarcasm;
- emotional coldness;
- rejection;
- attachment strategy;
- hidden anger;
- commitment level;

```text
   ↓
SEMANTIC CONTENT
   ↓
PLAUSIBLE TONE
   ↓
USER'S EXPERIENCED TONE
   ↓
HYPOTHESISED INTENTION
```

## 79. Analysis of attraction and intimacy

Attraction should remain distinct from attachment and identity.

Ask:

- Is attraction present?
- Has it changed?
- Is positive anticipation present?
- Is novelty absent?
- Is resentment suppressing desire?
- Does closeness create pressure?
- Is sex being used as reassurance?
- Is sexual refusal experienced as identity rejection?
- Are different desire levels being moralised?
- Are there practical conditions suppressing intimacy?

Do not assume:

> “If attachment improves, sexual desire must return.”

These domains interact but are not identical.

## 80. Analysis of relationship uncertainty

Uncertainty can arise from several sources.

### Information uncertainty

> “I do not know what the other person wants.”

### Trust uncertainty

> “I do not know whether the person will do what they say.”

### Compatibility uncertainty

> “I know what we both want, but I do not know whether both can coexist.”

### Self uncertainty

> “I do not know what I want.”

### Outcome uncertainty

> “I know what I want, but I cannot know what will happen.”

Do not solve all four with more interpretation.

Different uncertainties require different evidence.

---

## 81. What counts as progress?

Progress depends on the problem.

Possible indicators include:

- lower escalation;
- faster recovery;
- clearer direct requests;
- improved ability to pause;
- greater behavioural reliability;
- reduced mind-reading;
- better partner responsiveness;
- clearer boundaries;
- more accurate understanding;
- increased ability to tolerate disagreement;
- improved agency;
- greater clarity about incompatibility;
- successful repair;
- safer separation;
- less need to repeat the same argument.

Progress is not limited to preserving the relationship.

---

## 82. Common reasoning errors

### Error 1: Attachment totalisation

> “Everything is attachment.”

Correction:

Consider identity, attraction, power, standards, conflict skill, practical incompatibility and external stress.

---

### Error 2: Mind-reading disguised as empathy

> “He withdraws because closeness terrifies him.”

Correction:

That is a hypothesis unless directly supported.

---

### Error 3: Depth inflation

A one-off delay becomes:

> abandonment complex + shadow projection + projective identification.

Correction:

Return to context and repetition.

---

### Error 4: Equal-blame systemic thinking

> “Both created the betrayal cycle.”

Correction:

Mutual influence does not make unilateral deception mutually caused.

---

### Error 5: Intention erases impact

> “She did not mean to hurt him, so the reaction is excessive.”

Correction:

Intention and impact are separate variables.

---

### Error 6: Impact proves intention

> “I felt controlled, therefore the person intended to control me.”

Correction:

Impact requires attention. Intention requires separate evidence.

---

### Error 7: Explanation becomes excuse

> “He behaves this way because of attachment wounds.”

Correction:

Mechanism does not eliminate responsibility.

---

### Error 8: Apology becomes evidence of repair

Correction:

Track behaviour after the apology.

---

### Error 9: Symbol becomes fact

> “I dreamed of betrayal, so something must be happening.”

Correction:

Use the dream as psychological material, not external evidence.

---

### Error 10: Current distress becomes prediction

> “This pattern means the relationship will fail.”

Correction:

Do not forecast relationship trajectory confidently from current formulation alone.

---

### Error 11: Better communication is treated as universal solution

Correction:

Communication cannot reconcile every incompatible goal or value.

---

### Error 12: Therapy model becomes ontology

Correction:

A therapy can be effective without proving that its theoretical account explains every relationship.

## 83. The asymmetry test

Whenever a cycle is identified, run this check.

```markdown
### ASYMMETRY TEST

Is there:
- deception?
- broken explicit agreement?
- coercion?
- retaliation?
- intimidation?
- violence?
- repeated boundary violation?
- unilateral financial control?
- deliberate punishment?
- persistent refusal of accountability?

If yes:
1. identify the behaviour explicitly;
2. locate responsibility;
3. do not dilute it through circularity;
4. assess safety;
5. only then describe any secondary reciprocal cycle.
```

## 84. The partner-opacity test

Before making any claim about a partner, ask:

```text
DID THE PARTNER SAY THIS DIRECTLY?
          |
       YES / NO
          |
   if NO, is there repeated
   behavioural evidence?
          |
       YES / NO
          |
 if still uncertain:
 LABEL AS HYPOTHESIS
```

Preferred phrasing:

- “The behaviour is consistent with…”
- “One possible explanation is…”
- “Another possibility is…”
- “The available evidence does not distinguish these.”
- “The partner’s internal state remains unknown.”

---

## 85. The parsimony test

Before introducing a complex concept ask:

1. Can context explain it?
2. Can ordinary communication differences explain it?
3. Can a known practical disagreement explain it?
4. Is the behaviour repeated?
5. Does the complex theory explain something additional?
6. Can that additional claim be tested?

If the first three are sufficient, stop.

---

## 86. The projection test

Before using the word “projection”, distinguish:

### Misinterpretation
I got the meaning wrong.

### Expectancy effect
I expected rejection and interpreted ambiguous cues accordingly.

### Attribution
I inferred a motive.

### Projection, theory-specific
I may be locating something psychologically significant in the other that is also related to material difficult to recognise in myself.

### Projective identification
A more specific object-relations process involving interpersonal enactment and induced relational positioning.

Do not use these terms synonymously.

---

## 87. The attachment-injury test

Before calling an event an attachment injury ask:

- Was there a distinct incident?
- Was the person vulnerable or in need?
- Was the partner expected to provide support?
- Was the response experienced as abandonment, betrayal or breach of trust?
- Does the event remain alive in current conflict?
- Has it become a reference point for dependability?

If several answers are no, use a broader rupture label.

---

## 88. The repair-readiness test

Before recommending deeper vulnerability ask:

- Is the interaction safe?
- Can both remain within tolerable arousal?
- Is there basic willingness to listen?
- Is the harmful behaviour still occurring?
- Is factual reality sufficiently established?
- Is responsibility being denied?
- Would disclosure create additional vulnerability without protection?

Vulnerability is not automatically therapeutic.

Conditions matter.

---

## 89. The behavioural-change test

When someone says they have changed, ask:

- What behaviour changed?
- When did it change?
- How often?
- What happens under stress?
- Is the change independently observable?
- Has recurrence reduced?
- Does the other person no longer need to police the behaviour?
- Has trust begun to update?

A sincere intention to change and demonstrated change are different evidence classes.

---

## 90. The ordinary-life test

Before assuming the relationship requires a deep psychological explanation, ask:

- Are the partners sleeping enough?
- Do they have time together?
- Are chores chronically unequal?
- Is childcare overwhelming?
- Is money creating continuous strain?
- Has work consumed evenings?
- Is there physical affection?
- Is there enjoyment?
- Do they laugh together?
- Is privacy available?
- Is there room for friendship and independent life?

Mundane conditions can create profound relationship effects.

---

## 91. The self-agency test

After any analysis ask:

### Perception
Can I distinguish evidence from fear?

### Reflection
Can I consider alternatives?

### Choice
Can I pause before acting?

### Boundary
Can I say no?

### Connection
Can I remain connected without abandoning myself?

### Separation
Can I tolerate another person being psychologically separate?

### Values
Can I act consistently with what matters to me?

### Responsibility
Can I own my behaviour without owning what is not mine?

If an interpretation increases helplessness while adding no useful information, reconsider it.

## 93. Evidence-updating language

Use:

### Strong update

> “This repeated observation materially increases support for…”

### Weak update

> “This is consistent with the hypothesis but does not distinguish it from…”

### Contradiction

> “This episode does not fit the previous formulation because…”

### No update

> “The event is too ambiguous to change confidence.”

### Revision

> “The earlier attachment formulation now appears less necessary because predictable contact reduced the pattern substantially.”

The model should be able to change its mind.

---

## 94. Relationship-outcome neutrality protocol

Never build the formulation around the assumption:

> “How can this relationship be saved?”

Use:

> “What is happening, what can change, what may not change, and what do the observed patterns imply for the person’s own decision?”

Possible outcomes include:

- repair;
- continuation with clearer expectations;
- acceptance of a persistent difference;
- renegotiation;
- stronger boundaries;
- trial separation;
- separation;
- safe ending.

Do not turn reconciliation into the hidden success metric.

---

## 95. Safety override protocol

If there is credible coercion, violence, intimidation or retaliation:

```text
STOP
ordinary reciprocal-cycle formulation
        ↓
DESCRIBE
specific behaviour and asymmetry
        ↓
ASSESS
whether disclosure or confrontation is safe
        ↓
PRIORITISE
safety and specialist support
        ↓
DO NOT PRESCRIBE
vulnerable conjoint disclosure as default
```

This override outranks:

- communication practice;
- attachment exploration;
- dream interpretation;
- mutual vulnerability;
- couple-cycle symmetry.

## 97. Evidence anchors for dream work

The laboratory uses several distinct forms of evidence.

### Hill
Provides a collaborative exploration → insight → action architecture rather than an expert-decoding model.

### Pesant and Zadra
Provide a cross-theoretical clinical review emphasising collaboration, description, association, interpretation levels and movement toward waking-life change.

### Domhoff
Provides empirical continuity and content-analysis guardrails while explicitly criticising unsupported traditional claims.

### Contemporary dream science
Supports continuing investigation of links between waking experience and dreaming while preserving major uncertainties and methodological limits.

### Jungian material
May be used as an explicitly theory-specific symbolic lens, not as privileged empirical truth.

## 98. Evidence anchors for rupture and repair

Attachment-injury research offers a process model in which successful resolution involves more than talking about the original event.

The sequence includes:

- articulation of the injury;
- movement beyond defensive minimisation;
- deeper contact with its emotional significance;
- responsibility;
- empathy and remorse;
- renewed request for care;
- a responsive corrective interaction.

The laboratory generalises one principle from this cautiously:

> **Repair requires evidence at the level of interaction and behaviour, not interpretation alone.**

This does not mean every rupture is an attachment injury or that every relationship should reconcile.

## 102. When not to interpret further

Stop interpretation when:

- the relevant fact is already clear;
- the problem is behavioural rather than explanatory;
- safety requires action;
- the partner has already stated their position clearly;
- the issue is substantive incompatibility;
- no new evidence is emerging;
- deeper theory does not change action;
- analysis is feeding rumination;
- the next useful step is simply to observe what happens.

Sometimes the most accurate formulation is:

> “I understand enough. The remaining issue is whether the behaviour changes.”

---

## 103. When more interpretation may be useful

Continue when:

- the same pattern recurs despite conscious intentions;
- emotional intensity is disproportionate to the immediate cue;
- several apparently different conflicts have the same structure;
- self-agency repeatedly collapses in one relational configuration;
- ordinary explanations do not account for repetition;
- behaviour changes only briefly;
- a dream series repeatedly converges on the same waking concern;
- the person is stuck between contradictory motives.

Even then, theory remains subordinate to evidence.

## 105. Self-protection versus self-defeat

A behaviour can make sense locally while becoming costly globally.

Examples:

### Pursuit
Short-term function: reduce uncertainty.  
Possible long-term cost: increase partner pressure.

### Withdrawal
Short-term function: reduce arousal.  
Possible long-term cost: amplify partner alarm.

### Hypervigilance
Short-term function: detect betrayal.  
Possible long-term cost: make neutral ambiguity intolerable.

### Appeasement
Short-term function: stop conflict.  
Possible long-term cost: erode agency.

### Control
Short-term function: reduce uncertainty.  
Possible long-term cost: provoke resistance and damage autonomy.

Understanding function is more useful than moralising the defence.

Responsibility for behaviour still remains.

## 106. Distinguishing vulnerability from permission

A person may be:

- highly triggered;
- ashamed;
- frightened;
- traumatised;
- attachment-activated;
- flooded;

and still be responsible for:

- threats;
- deception;
- aggression;
- coercion;
- boundary violations.

Use:

> “This may help explain the behaviour. It does not make the behaviour acceptable.”

---

## 107. Distinguishing validation from agreement

Validation means accurately recognising experience.

It does not require agreeing that the person’s interpretation is factually correct.

Example:

> “I can understand why the delayed call frightened you after what happened last month.”

does not require:

> “Your fear proves I was abandoning you.”

This distinction is crucial in repair.

---

## 108. Distinguishing primary emotion from automatic hidden emotion

Do not assume:

- anger always hides fear;
- withdrawal always hides shame;
- criticism always hides attachment need.

Instead ask.

A person may primarily be:

- angry;
- disgusted;
- disappointed;
- sad;
- afraid;
- ashamed;
- relieved.

Emotion-focused formulations can suggest deeper affective layers.

They should not impose a universal emotional hierarchy.

---

## 109. Distinguishing acceptance from resignation

Acceptance of an enduring difference may mean:

> “This trait is unlikely to change substantially, and I can decide what that means for me.”

Resignation means:

> “I have no meaningful agency and must tolerate whatever occurs.”

Do not confuse the two. File 07 provides the detailed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy and existential controls.

Acceptance can coexist with:

- limits;
- boundaries;
- negotiation;
- refusal;
- separation;
- values-consistent committed action.

Acceptance concerns willingness to contact internal and external reality. It is not approval of another person's conduct and does not require relationship preservation.

## 110. Substantive incompatibility test

Ask:

```text
IS THE PROBLEM CAUSED MAINLY BY
HOW THE ISSUE IS DISCUSSED?
        |
       YES
        ↓
work on process

        OR

DO BOTH UNDERSTAND EACH OTHER
AND STILL WANT INCOMPATIBLE THINGS?
        |
       YES
        ↓
work on clarity, trade-offs,
acceptance and decision
```

Do not promise that relational insight can dissolve every conflict of values. When mutual understanding is already adequate, route the remaining task to File 07: clarify values, agreements, trade-offs, reversibility, irreducible uncertainty, and the next values-consistent action without giving a verdict.

## 111. What the laboratory should never claim from sparse material

Do not say:

- “Your partner is avoidantly attached.”
- “Your partner is narcissistic.”
- “They fear intimacy.”
- “They are secretly jealous.”
- “Your dream reveals infidelity.”
- “This proves a trauma bond.”
- “This is definitely projective identification.”
- “Your relationship will fail.”
- “You should leave.”
- “You should stay.”
- “You need to forgive.”
- “The relationship can be repaired if you communicate properly.”

Replace categorical conclusions with the appropriate evidence status.

---

## 112. What the laboratory can say clearly

When evidence supports it, the model can say:

- “The behaviour violated the stated agreement.”
- “The same sequence appears across four episodes.”
- “The apology has not yet been accompanied by behavioural change.”
- “The relationship currently appears to contain a pursue-withdraw cycle.”
- “There is not enough evidence to determine the partner’s motive.”
- “External stress is a strong alternative explanation.”
- “The cycle is reciprocal, but responsibility for the deception is not.”
- “The dream reflects concern about betrayal but does not establish betrayal.”
- “The central issue appears to be incompatibility rather than misunderstanding.”
- “The available evidence does not support a confident prediction about the relationship’s future.”

Epistemic caution does not require vagueness.

## 113. Final applied checklist

Before completing any formulation, ask:

- What do I actually know?
- What am I inferring?
- What did each person directly say or report?
- What repeats?
- What does not repeat?
- What happens in my body?
- What emotions are present?
- What meaning did I assign to the event?
- What happens to my agency?
- What did I do next?
- What did the other person actually do next?
- What cycle emerges?
- What context matters?
- What ordinary relationship mechanism fits?
- What is the simplest plausible explanation?
- What other explanation fits?
- Am I using attachment because the evidence supports it or because it is available?
- Am I mentalizing or mind-reading?
- Is identity, recognition or power relevant?
- Is attraction or substantive compatibility relevant?
- What is the other person’s actual evidence?
- What remains genuinely unknown?
- Where is responsibility located?
- Is responsibility asymmetric?
- Is safety relevant?
- Am I confusing explanation with excuse?
- Am I confusing apology with repair?
- Am I confusing forgiveness with reconciliation?
- Am I treating a dream or symbolic interpretation as factual evidence?
- Would a depth interpretation add anything beyond simpler explanations?
- What evidence would distinguish the hypotheses?
- What can I control?
- What can I influence?
- What can I ask about?
- What can I not know yet?
- What can I not control?
- What could be tested safely?
- What observable change would count?
- What happened after the test?
- Did the new evidence strengthen, weaken or change the formulation?
- Am I preserving relationship-outcome neutrality?
- Is this the **least elaborate formulation that adequately explains the evidence and supports useful action**?

# 03_COUPLE_THERAPY_MODELS_MECHANISMS_AND_EVIDENCE

## 1. Purpose

This file is a comparative map of major models of couple therapy. It is designed to answer five questions:

1. What does each model think is going wrong?
2. What does each model try to change?
3. What does the therapist actually do?
4. What mechanism is supposed to produce change?
5. How strong is the evidence for the treatment, and how strong is the evidence for its proposed mechanism?

The aim is not to identify a universally superior therapy. Couple therapies overlap substantially in practice while differing in theory, language, emphasis, sequencing, and assumptions about what produces durable change. Similar interventions can also be justified by very different theories. Conversely, two therapies may use the same word, such as *emotion*, *acceptance*, *system*, or *attachment*, while meaning materially different things.

The broad outcome literature supports couple and systemic intervention for relationship distress and for several adult mental-health and health-related problems. Carr's review synthesizes meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and controlled trials across relationship distress, psychosexual problems, intimate-partner violence, anxiety and mood disorders, alcohol problems, psychosis, and adjustment to chronic illness.

Historical meta-analytic estimates generally show meaningful average effects, but the exact effect sizes should not be treated as timeless constants because the studies differ in population, comparator, outcome, treatment, follow-up, and research era.

## 2. Epistemic frame: what kind of evidence is being discussed?

A central problem in psychotherapy literature is that five different propositions are easily conflated.

**Crosswalk to File 00:** the five treatment-evidence dimensions below are orthogonal to File 00 **Axis B**. They describe *what treatment question is being asked*. The strength of evidence for that answer still depends on whether the supporting literature is, for example, a systematic review, a large body of studies, a single study, an empirically supported clinical model, or a theory/case-based tradition.

| Evidence layer | Question it answers | Typical evidence | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Model theory** | How does the model explain distress and change? | Theory, clinical formulation, conceptual papers | That the explanation is true |
| **Clinician experience** | Does the model appear clinically useful and coherent in practice? | Case material, expert observation, supervision traditions | Efficacy, comparative superiority, or causal mechanism |
| **Process research** | What changes during successful therapy, and what in-session events accompany improvement? | Session coding, task analysis, longitudinal mediation studies | That the process is the sole or necessary cause of outcome |
| **Efficacy** | Can the treatment improve outcomes under controlled research conditions? | Randomized controlled trials, controlled trials, meta-analysis | That its theory of change is correct, or that it works equally well in routine care |
| **Effectiveness** | Does it help under ordinary clinical conditions? | Practice-based studies, naturalistic cohorts, routine outcome monitoring | Causal attribution as cleanly as randomized trials |

These distinctions matter. A therapy can have good outcome evidence even if its theoretical mechanism remains uncertain. A theoretically plausible mechanism can have limited treatment-outcome evidence. A process variable can correlate with success without being the causal ingredient. A model can also help because of mechanisms that its own theory underemphasizes.

Carr privileges meta-analyses and systematic reviews where available, then controlled trials, while acknowledging the heterogeneity of systemic intervention research. The *Clinical Handbook of Couple Therapy* likewise presents a field in which controlled treatment evidence, empirically supported therapeutic relationships, process research, clinical expertise, and pluralistic integration coexist rather than reducing couple therapy to a single evidence hierarchy.

### 2.1 A useful rule

**Evidence that a treatment works is not automatically evidence that the treatment works for the reason its theory says it works.**

Examples:

- Successful Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy does not by itself prove that attachment insecurity was the sole cause of the couple's distress.
- Successful Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy does not prove that distorted cognition was the primary cause.
- A Gottman observational predictor of relationship outcome does not automatically become a universal causal mechanism.
- Successful psychodynamic therapy does not establish insight as the active ingredient.
- Successful solution-focused work does not show that historical causes were irrelevant.
- Successful Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy does not imply that acceptance alone caused improvement.
- A successful systemic intervention does not prove that responsibility for a destructive interaction was equally distributed.

## 3. Comparative map at a glance

| Model | Primary unit of analysis | Core account of distress | Main change target | Evidence profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy, Johnson tradition** | Attachment bond plus interactional cycle | Attachment threat becomes organized into rigid pursuit, withdrawal, attack, defence or disengagement | Secure emotional engagement, accessibility, responsiveness, new attachment interactions | Substantial outcome literature plus process research |
| **Emotion-Focused Couple Therapy, Greenberg and Goldman** | Emotion schemes, self-regulation, other-regulation and interaction | Maladaptive emotional schemes and failures in attachment, identity validation, power regulation and attraction | Transform emotion, increase self-soothing and other-soothing, restructure interaction | Experiential/process support, smaller distinct couple-therapy outcome literature than Johnson EFT |
| **Gottman Method / Sound Relationship House** | Observable interaction, affect, physiology, friendship, conflict, trust and meaning | Erosion of friendship/trust, destructive conflict processes, flooding, poor repair, gridlock and disengagement | Friendship, turning toward, emotion regulation, constructive conflict, repair, trust and shared meaning | Strong observational and laboratory tradition; treatment package evidence more limited than observational evidence |
| **Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy** | Individual cognition plus reciprocal behavior and dyadic interaction | Negative behavior exchanges, maladaptive appraisals, attributions, assumptions, expectancies and weak problem-solving | Behavioral change, cognitive reappraisal, communication, problem-solving and emotion regulation | Large and mature controlled-treatment tradition |
| **Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy** | Functional interaction patterns, persistent differences and emotional sensitivities | Polarization around incompatibilities, coercive efforts to change the partner, emotional vulnerabilities and external stress | Acceptance plus targeted change | Strong controlled and long-term comparative evidence |
| **Systemic approaches, umbrella** | Relationship system and recursive patterns across contexts | Problems maintained by reciprocal sequences, feedback loops, rules, structures, triangulation and context | Change interaction and system organization | Broad evidence across heterogeneous systemic interventions |
| **Bowen family systems couple coaching** | Multigenerational emotional system | Chronic anxiety, fusion, reactivity, triangles, cutoff and low differentiation | Differentiation, detriangulation, thoughtful self-definition | Strong theoretical tradition, limited direct randomized couple-treatment evidence |
| **Brief strategic couple therapy** | Present problem-maintaining interaction | Attempted solutions recursively sustain the complaint | Interrupt the attempted solution and alter feedback loops | Smaller direct couple-outcome base; mechanism evidence limited |
| **Structural couple therapy** | Couple as subsystem within wider family structure | Maladaptive boundaries, coalitions, disengagement, enmeshment and structural organization | Restructure boundaries and transactional patterns | Substantial family-therapy tradition, thinner direct couple-specific controlled evidence |
| **Narrative couple therapy** | Meaning, identity, discourse and sociocultural context | Problem-saturated stories constrain identity, agency and alternative meanings | Externalization, re-authoring, agency and alternative narratives | Rich clinical tradition; controlled couple-treatment evidence comparatively sparse |
| **Solution-Focused Brief Couple Therapy** | Desired outcomes, exceptions, resources and interaction | Problem discourse and repeated unsuccessful responses obscure existing competence and alternatives | Amplify exceptions, agency, useful behavior and preferred futures | Positive broader Solution-Focused Brief Therapy evidence; couple-specific evidence smaller |
| **Psychodynamic / object-relations couple therapy** | Internal object world plus dyadic enactment | Projection, projective identification, splitting, unresolved developmental patterns and shared defensive systems | Insight, integration, recognition, reduced projection, new relational experience | Strong clinical tradition, naturalistic effectiveness evidence; fewer controlled couple trials |
| **Relational psychoanalytic couple therapy** | Multiple subjectivities, enactment and relational field | Mutual negation, dissociated self-states, repetitive relational configurations and failed recognition | Mutual recognition, integration and negotiated relational experience | Primarily theoretical and clinical case evidence |
| **Integrative / common-factor approaches** | Couple, individuals, systems, alliance, context and multiple mechanisms | Distress is multiply determined; no single mechanism explains all couples | Tailored formulation, alliance, feedback and mechanism-specific intervention | Supported indirectly by cross-model outcome and alliance/process literature; difficult to test as one uniform package |

This table should not be read as a league table. "More evidence" can mean that a model is easier to manualize, has been researched longer, has a stronger research infrastructure, or targets populations that are easier to study. It does not automatically imply greater relevance for every couple.

## 4. Six distinctions that must not be collapsed

### 4.1 Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy is not the same as Greenberg's Emotion-Focused Couple Therapy

The two traditions share a common history and substantial experiential methodology, but their later theoretical centres differ.

**Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy**, most closely associated with Susan Johnson, increasingly made adult attachment theory its central organizing framework. Distress is understood principally in terms of threatened attachment security and the rigid interactional cycles partners use to manage that threat.

**Emotion-Focused Couple Therapy**, associated with Leslie Greenberg and Rhonda Goldman, retains attachment but places it inside a broader theory of emotion and affect regulation. Greenberg and Goldman explicitly identify three major relational motivational systems: attachment, identity validation, and attraction. They also place greater emphasis on emotion schemes, primary adaptive and maladaptive emotion, self-soothing, identity, power, and intrapsychic emotional transformation.

Therefore:

**Johnson EFT:** attachment security is the central relational organizing frame.

**Greenberg-Goldman emotion-focused work:** attachment is central but not sufficient; emotion transformation, identity, power, attraction, self-regulation, and other-regulation also organize therapy.

### 4.2 Attachment-based change is not synonymous with emotion-focused change

Attachment theory explains why proximity, responsiveness, accessibility, safe-haven functions, separation distress, and secure-base processes matter. Emotion-focused theories ask additional questions:

- What emotion is primary?
- Is it adaptive or maladaptive?
- Is a secondary emotion masking another experience?
- What learned emotion scheme has been activated?
- Can one emotion transform another?
- Can the person regulate themselves as well as use the partner for regulation?

Mikulincer and Shaver's attachment framework includes attachment-system activation, working models, hyperactivating and deactivating strategies, emotion regulation, interpersonal regulation, couple functioning, caregiving, sexuality, and psychotherapy. That is broader than a simple "attachment style" label, but it is still not identical to a general theory of emotional transformation.

### 4.3 Acceptance in Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy is not resignation

Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy uses acceptance to reduce destructive struggles around enduring differences and emotional sensitivities. It does not require approving, liking, or passively tolerating destructive conduct.

The model explicitly distinguishes tolerable differences from behavior that should not be normalized. Domestic violence, battering, dangerous compulsive behaviour, and similarly destructive conditions fall outside a simple acceptance strategy. Acceptance is therefore better understood as **reducing coercive attempts to make an unchangeable difference disappear while responding more flexibly to it**.

### 4.4 Gottman observational constructs are not universal causal laws

Criticism, defensiveness, contempt, stonewalling, physiological flooding, repair attempts, turning toward, friendship, fondness, admiration and other Gottman constructs emerged from observational and laboratory research.

Observational prediction answers:

> "What tends to co-occur with, or predict, later outcomes in studied samples?"

It does not automatically answer:

> "What single process caused this couple's outcome?"

Nor does a group-level predictor become a deterministic diagnostic test for an individual relationship.

### 4.5 Psychodynamic insight is not the same thing as a demonstrated outcome mechanism

Object-relations and psychoanalytic therapies provide sophisticated accounts of projection, projective identification, enactment, internal objects, shared unconscious fantasy and countertransference. These concepts can organize clinically meaningful work.

But three claims must remain separate:

1. clients can gain psychodynamic insight;
2. therapy can improve;
3. insight caused the improvement.

The first two do not establish the third.

### 4.6 Systemic recursion does not mean equal responsibility

Systemic therapies correctly emphasize that interaction is recursive. One person's behavior affects another's response, which feeds back into subsequent behavior.

But reciprocity is a description of interaction, not a moral equation.

If one partner assaults, coerces, threatens, stalks, sexually forces, financially controls, or terrorizes another, the existence of a relational feedback loop does not make the victim equally responsible for the abusive act. Treatment must preserve distinctions among influence, contribution to ordinary conflict, responsibility for one's own behavior, power, coercion, intentional control, and legal and moral accountability.

## 5. Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy

### 5.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Developed from experiential and systemic couple therapy, later reorganized increasingly around Bowlby-derived adult attachment theory. |
| **Unit of analysis** | The attachment bond, the recurring interactional cycle, and each partner's moment-to-moment emotional experience within that cycle. |
| **Theory of distress** | Threats to connection activate attachment alarm. Partners protect themselves through pursuit, criticism, protest, defence, withdrawal, numbing or disengagement. These protective responses alarm the other partner and consolidate a rigid negative cycle. |
| **Central mechanism** | Partners access and communicate vulnerable attachment-related emotions and needs, and experience the partner responding with greater accessibility, responsiveness and engagement. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Negative cycle; positions within it; primary and secondary emotion; accessibility and responsiveness; attachment injuries; interactional triggers; trauma; relational safety; individual symptoms insofar as they interact with the relationship. |
| **Therapist stance** | Active, empathic, process-oriented, validating and systemic. The therapist does not simply teach skills. The therapist tracks, evokes, heightens, reframes and choreographs new emotional interactions. |
| **Main interventions** | Cycle tracking, reflection and validation, evocative responding, heightening, reframing behavior in attachment terms, enactments, withdrawer re-engagement, pursuer softening, attachment-injury conversations and consolidation. |
| **Role of emotion** | Central. Secondary anger, hostility, shutdown or detachment are differentiated from more vulnerable fear, sadness, loneliness, shame and longing. |
| **Role of cognition** | Meaning is important, especially beliefs such as "you will not be there for me," but cognition is usually approached through emotional and relational experience rather than formal disputation. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Behavior is part of the interactional cycle and changes through new emotional engagement and enactment rather than primarily through behavioral contracts. |
| **Role of attachment** | Organizing theory. Attachment security provides the principal explanation of distress and recovery. |
| **Role of history** | Relevant when it clarifies sensitivities, attachment strategies, trauma, and relationship injuries, but therapy is mainly focused on present emotional process and corrective interaction. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Core. The cycle becomes the common enemy rather than either partner being defined as the problem. |
| **Role of power and context** | Culture and context can shape attachment expression and relational roles. Power differences and coercion require separate assessment because attachment reframing must not erase accountability. |
| **Mechanism of change** | De-escalation lowers threat. Vulnerable emotion becomes tolerable and communicable. Partners respond in new ways. Successful enactments generate new relational expectations and a more secure bond. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Multiple trials and reviews support improvement in relationship distress. Carr's synthesis reports an established outcome literature and also summarizes applications to depression, post-traumatic stress symptoms, sexual satisfaction and chronic illness contexts. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Many older effect-size and recovery figures come from relatively small studies and model-developer research. Treatment efficacy does not prove that attachment change fully mediates outcome. |
| **Likely strengths** | Makes intense conflict intelligible without reducing it to bad communication; strong language for pursuit-withdrawal and emotional disengagement; gives vulnerable emotion and relationship security a central role; explicit repair model for attachment injuries. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Can over-translate heterogeneous problems into attachment language; practical conflict, severe individual psychopathology, economic strain, structural inequality, coercion or concrete behavioral deficits may require additional formulations. |
| **What should not be inferred** | A negative cycle does not imply equal responsibility. Every angry partner is not necessarily anxious underneath. Every withdrawing partner is not necessarily attachment-avoidant. Treatment success does not establish attachment insecurity as the sole original cause. |

### 5.2 Theory of distress

In this model, the recurring argument is rarely only about the surface topic. Money, sex, housework, relatives, parenting, work hours, phones, or lateness can become signals about the relationship itself.

One partner may experience:

> "You are not available when I need you."

That alarm may be expressed not as fear but as criticism or protest. The other partner then experiences the criticism as danger, inadequacy, rejection or futility and withdraws. Withdrawal increases the first partner's alarm, which increases pursuit. The cycle becomes self-confirming.

The distinction between **primary vulnerability** and **secondary protection** is clinically consequential. It turns:

- "She is impossible" into "her attack is part of a protest cycle";
- "he does not care" into "his withdrawal may be a protective strategy";
- "we cannot communicate" into "we cannot remain emotionally available when threat becomes high."

Those are hypotheses, not absolutions.

### 5.3 Treatment sequence

The standard architecture contains three broad stages.

### Stage 1: de-escalation

The therapist establishes alliance with both partners, identifies the recurrent cycle, tracks triggers, emotion, interpretations and protective responses, accesses previously unarticulated primary emotions, and reframes the problem as the cycle plus underlying attachment alarm.

### Stage 2: restructuring attachment interactions

The therapist helps partners access disowned or avoided emotional experience, risk more direct expression of attachment need, re-engage a withdrawn partner, soften an attacking or pursuing position, ask rather than protest, respond rather than defend, and create new interactional events in the session.

### Stage 3: consolidation

The couple increasingly manages the relationship itself. Old practical problems can often be revisited after the emotional structure surrounding them has changed. New interactions are consolidated into a coherent account of how the relationship changed.

### 5.4 Attachment injuries

Attachment injuries are particularly important because some relationships do not remain distressed merely through generic pursuit and withdrawal. A particular event becomes a symbolic test of whether the partner can be trusted.

Makinen and Johnson define such injuries around perceived abandonment, betrayal, or breach of trust in a critical moment, with the event recurring as a reference point for partner dependability.

Their process study involved 24 couples receiving about 13 sessions on average. Fifteen were classified as having resolved the injury. Resolved couples showed more affiliative interaction, deeper experiencing, and greater improvement in relationship satisfaction and forgiveness than nonresolvers. This is valuable process evidence, but it is not a large independent efficacy trial.

The proposed repair sequence is highly specific:

1. the injured partner can describe the injury and its attachment meaning;
2. defensive minimization decreases;
3. the injured partner accesses grief, fear and vulnerability;
4. the offending partner understands the significance of what occurred;
5. responsibility, empathy, regret and remorse become possible;
6. the injured partner risks asking for the comfort that was absent;
7. the other partner responds in a new and caring manner.

A critical process finding is sequencing: trying to resolve the injury while the ordinary negative cycle is still highly escalated can be premature. The injury becomes more workable after de-escalation creates enough safety for renewed emotional risk.

### 5.5 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Highly elaborated attachment-systemic formulation. |
| **Clinician experience** | Very extensive international training and case tradition. |
| **Process research** | Substantial relative to many couple therapies, including task analysis of key change events. |
| **Efficacy** | Direct controlled evidence supports reduction of relationship distress, though exact historical effect-size claims should be interpreted cautiously. |
| **Effectiveness** | Used widely in routine care and heterogeneous clinical populations; routine-practice evidence is growing but less clean than experimental efficacy data. |

## 6. Emotion-Focused Couple Therapy: Greenberg and Goldman

### 6.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Greenberg-Goldman Emotion-Focused Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Humanistic, person-centred, gestalt, experiential and emotion theory. Shares historical roots with early Johnson-Greenberg emotionally focused couple work but developed in a different theoretical direction. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Individual emotion schemes plus dyadic regulation of emotion, identity and attachment. |
| **Theory of distress** | Conflict emerges when adaptive emotion is obscured by secondary emotion, maladaptive emotion schemes, failures of self-regulation and other-regulation, identity threat, unmet attachment needs, power struggles and loss of positive attraction. |
| **Central mechanism** | Awareness, acceptance, differentiation, expression and transformation of emotion, combined with new responsive interaction. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Primary adaptive emotion, primary maladaptive emotion, secondary emotion, instrumental emotional displays, emotion schemes, attachment, identity validation, dominance-control patterns, attraction and emotional regulation. |
| **Therapist stance** | Empathic, process-directive, emotionally attuned and experiential. |
| **Main interventions** | Empathic exploration, emotional focusing, accessing core experience, heightening, chair work where appropriate, identifying maladaptive emotion schemes, facilitating self-soothing, enactment and responsive partner interaction. |
| **Role of emotion** | The primary system of meaning, motivation, action readiness and interpersonal signalling. |
| **Role of cognition** | Cognition is integrated within emotion schemes and meaning processes rather than treated as an isolated target. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Behaviour is organized by emotional experience and can change as emotion schemes and interpersonal responses change. |
| **Role of attachment** | Major, but one motivational system among several. |
| **Role of history** | Important because maladaptive emotion schemes can be built from painful developmental experience and unmet needs. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Important, particularly pursue-distance and dominate-submit cycles, but not sufficient to explain all distress. |
| **Role of power and context** | More explicit than attachment-only formulations because identity, dominance, control, shame and invalidation are central. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Transform maladaptive emotion through access to adaptive emotion, improve self-regulation, increase empathy and partner responsiveness, and change the interactional consequences of emotion. |
| **Treatment evidence** | The broader experiential and emotion-focused literature provides support, as do early couple-process studies, but this later Greenberg-Goldman couple model has a smaller distinct outcome literature than the Johnson attachment-centred model. |
| **Evidence limitations** | The conceptual richness of emotion categories exceeds the precision with which all categories and mechanisms have been independently validated in couple-treatment research. |
| **Likely strengths** | Differentiates forms of emotion rather than treating vulnerability as one undifferentiated category; explicitly includes identity, power, shame, self-soothing and attraction. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Can become complex and therapist-dependent; social-material context and concrete behavioral negotiation may receive insufficient weight if emotional formulation dominates. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Emotion-focused does not mean "express more emotion." Intense emotion can be maladaptive, defensive, secondary or instrumental. Attachment is not the only emotional motive. |

### 6.2 Three motivational systems

Greenberg and Goldman identify three broad relational motivations:

1. **Attachment**
2. **Identity maintenance and validation**
3. **Attraction**

These motives organize different emotional families. Attachment threats commonly evoke fear, anxiety and sadness. Identity threats can evoke shame, powerlessness, anger and fear of losing control. Attraction depends more on interest, pleasure, excitement and positive engagement.

This matters because two outwardly similar arguments may be psychologically different.

A demand for reassurance may arise from fear of abandonment, shame at not feeling valued, anger at being controlled, fear of losing status, sexual rejection, jealousy, loss of attraction, or an old maladaptive emotion scheme.

### 6.3 Emotion categories

The Greenberg tradition distinguishes among:

- **primary adaptive emotion**, which provides useful information and action readiness;
- **primary maladaptive emotion**, which is authentic but organized by old learning and may no longer fit the current situation;
- **secondary emotion**, which is a reaction to another emotion, such as anger covering shame or fear;
- **instrumental emotion**, which has acquired an interpersonal function, consciously or otherwise.

The model views emotion as more than arousal. Emotion organizes self-experience, perception, motivation, action and communication. Change therefore requires more than "getting underneath anger." Sometimes the primary emotion itself is maladaptive and must be transformed by another, more adaptive emotional experience.

### 6.4 Five-stage structure

### Stage 1: create safety and alliance

Validate each partner's experience without validating destructive behaviour. Establish a sufficiently secure therapeutic environment for emotional exploration.

### Stage 2: identify and de-escalate the cycle

Map cycles such as pursuit-distance or dominance-submission. Identify the emotions, identity sensitivities and attachment concerns that organize them.

### Stage 3: access underlying emotion and need

Deepen emotional processing. Differentiate current adaptive emotion from historically organized maladaptive schemes. Work with blocked needs, shame, fear, sadness, anger and longing.

### Stage 4: restructure self and interaction

Partners develop new ways to regulate themselves and respond to each other. A withdrawn partner may engage; a pursuing partner may express underlying need more directly; a dominating partner may relinquish control; a habitually submissive partner may assert themselves.

This model places notable emphasis on **self-soothing as well as other-soothing**.

### Stage 5: consolidate new interaction and problem-solving

The couple integrates emotional change into practical interaction and a revised relational narrative.

### 6.5 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Highly developed theory of emotion, affect regulation and interpersonal motivation. |
| **Clinician experience** | Strong experiential clinical tradition. |
| **Process research** | Emotion-access and experiencing research supports parts of the proposed sequence. |
| **Efficacy** | Evidence exists, but the distinct later Greenberg-Goldman couple model has a smaller direct controlled literature than Johnson's attachment-centred EFT. |
| **Effectiveness** | Less extensively documented as a separate routine-practice brand of couple therapy. |

## 7. Gottman Method and the Sound Relationship House

### 7.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Gottman Method |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Longitudinal observational research, laboratory studies of couple interaction, psychophysiology, affect coding and later intervention development. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Observable interaction, affect, physiology, friendship, conflict regulation, turning toward or away, trust, commitment and shared meaning. |
| **Theory of distress** | Relationship deterioration involves negative sentiment, loss of friendship and responsiveness, destructive escalation, physiological flooding, weak repair, gridlocked perpetual conflict, disengagement and erosion of trust. |
| **Central mechanism** | Improve friendship and positive affect, reduce destructive conflict processes, increase self-soothing and repair, strengthen turning toward and trust, and build shared meaning. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Relationship history, friendship, conflict, Four Horsemen, flooding, repair, positive-to-negative interaction, bids and turning, perpetual versus solvable problems, trust, commitment, sex and meaning. |
| **Therapist stance** | Structured, educational, experiential and observational, but ideally responsive to the live interaction rather than mechanically following a sequence. |
| **Main intervention types** | Love maps, fondness and admiration, turning toward, soft start-up, accepting influence, self-soothing, compromise, repair, dreams-within-conflict work, rituals, trust-building and shared meaning. |
| **Role of emotion** | Strong. Positive affect, negative escalation, flooding, soothing, affection, humor and emotional attunement are central. |
| **Role of cognition** | Sentiment override, attribution, meaning and assumptions about the partner matter, but formal cognitive restructuring is not the central technique. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Very strong. Observable interaction provides much of the model's empirical foundation. |
| **Role of attachment** | Increasingly acknowledged, especially through trust and attunement, but the model did not originate as an attachment therapy. |
| **Role of history** | Relationship history and learned interaction matter, but childhood reconstruction is not generally central. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Strong. Escalation, withdrawal, repair and bid-response sequences are closely tracked. |
| **Role of power/context** | Accepting influence is explicitly important. Cultural and structural context require additional assessment because the main observational constructs do not exhaust social power. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Improved friendship and responsiveness reduce negative sentiment; lower arousal increases regulatory capacity; repair becomes more effective; perpetual conflicts become dialogued rather than gridlocked; trust accumulates through repeated interactions. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Extensive observational and laboratory evidence informs the model. Intervention studies exist, but the quantity and independence of evidence for the complete clinical package are more limited than the observational research base. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Prediction is not causation. Some famous findings derive from selected laboratory samples and should not be converted into deterministic individual-level rules. |
| **Likely strengths** | Concrete behavioral language; strong attention to physiology, repair, ordinary daily responsiveness, friendship and enduring differences; easy connection between assessment and observable intervention. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Can appear overly rule-based when popularized; deeper developmental meaning, dissociation, severe attachment injury, coercion and structural context may require broader formulation. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Presence of a "Horseman" does not diagnose a doomed relationship. A numerical ratio is not a universal clinical law. Most conflict being perpetual does not mean all conflict should simply be tolerated. |

### 7.2 The Sound Relationship House

The framework is typically organized into seven levels:

1. **Build Love Maps**
2. **Share Fondness and Admiration**
3. **Turn Toward Instead of Away**
4. **Maintain a Positive Perspective**
5. **Manage Conflict**
6. **Make Life Dreams Come True**
7. **Create Shared Meaning**

Later versions place **trust** and **commitment** as the walls supporting the house.

This structure is important because Gottman therapy is not simply "conflict-management training." It treats friendship, responsiveness and ordinary positive interaction as the infrastructure within which conflict repair becomes possible.

### 7.3 Perpetual conflict and gridlock

A major Gottman observation is that many recurring disagreements reflect durable differences rather than solvable technical problems.

That does not mean they are irrelevant. The distinction is between **perpetual but dialogued** and **perpetual and gridlocked** conflict.

Gridlock is associated with escalation, rigid positions and underlying meanings or life dreams becoming compressed into positional conflict.

The clinical task shifts from "Who is right?" to "What does this position mean to each of you, and can the relationship make room for both people?"

### 7.4 The Four Horsemen

The well-known pattern includes criticism, defensiveness, contempt, and stonewalling.

Their value is primarily as an interactional map. The important clinical sequence is often harsh start, defensive counterresponse, escalating negative affect, physiological overload, withdrawal or stonewalling, failed repair, and negative interpretation of subsequent interactions.

The model therefore combines behavior and physiology. Once arousal becomes high, asking for more sophisticated communication may be futile until self-soothing occurs.

### 7.5 Trust and attunement

In *The Science of Trust*, Gottman places greater emphasis on how trust is built or eroded through repeated moments of attunement.

Trust is not treated only as a belief. It is built through repeated choices in which partners encounter moments of need and either turn toward, turn away, or turn against.

This creates a cumulative relational history. Small interactions therefore matter because they can alter the expected probability that the partner will respond cooperatively when vulnerability or conflicting interests arise.

### 7.6 Evidence caution

Gottman's observational science is among the most distinctive empirical contributions to couple research, but its clinical interpretation requires restraint.

Three levels must be distinguished:

1. **observational association or prediction**;
2. **experimental manipulation of a process**;
3. **outcome evidence for the complete treatment package**.

The fact that stable and distressed couples differ on an interactional variable supports clinical attention to that variable. It does not prove that changing the variable alone will reproduce the observed long-term difference.

Similarly, laboratory classification accuracy in a research sample is not equivalent to a general-purpose diagnostic tool.

### 7.7 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Grounded more directly in observed couple interaction than most models. |
| **Clinician experience** | Large clinical and training infrastructure. |
| **Process research** | Strong for many interactional and physiological constructs. |
| **Efficacy** | Intervention evidence exists, but the full branded method has a smaller independent controlled base than the observational literature might imply. |
| **Effectiveness** | Widely used in practice, although routine outcome evidence should be distinguished from popularity and training dissemination. |

## 8. Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy

### 8.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Behavioral exchange, operant learning, social learning, social exchange theory, later cognitive therapy and broader contextual cognitive-behavioral models. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Individual cognitions plus reciprocal behavior and dyadic interaction. |
| **Theory of distress** | Low reinforcement, aversive exchange, ineffective communication and problem-solving interact with biased attention, maladaptive attributions, expectations, assumptions and standards. |
| **Central mechanism** | Partners change what they do, how they interpret each other, and how they solve problems. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Presenting problems, relationship history, strengths, interaction, communication, behavior frequencies, reinforcement, attributions, beliefs, expectations, standards, individual psychopathology and context. |
| **Therapist stance** | Collaborative but active, structured and often educational. |
| **Main intervention types** | Behavioral exchange, communication training, problem-solving, negotiation, cognitive restructuring, attribution work, coping strategies, emotion-regulation interventions and homework. |
| **Role of emotion** | Important but historically less central than behavior and cognition. Contemporary variants integrate emotion more explicitly. |
| **Role of cognition** | Central. Interpretations of partner behaviour influence affect and response. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Central. Behavior both shapes and is shaped by partner responses. |
| **Role of attachment** | Can be incorporated into formulation, but it is not the defining theory. |
| **Role of history** | Relevant when it shaped enduring schemas, standards and vulnerabilities; present maintaining processes remain the main intervention focus. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Strong. Reciprocal reinforcement, demand-withdrawal, coercive patterns and attribution-response sequences are commonly formulated. |
| **Role of power/context** | Modern contextual Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy incorporates culture, stress, individual differences and resources more than early behavioral exchange models did. |
| **Mechanism of change** | More rewarding interaction, less aversive exchange, more accurate and benign interpretation, better emotional regulation, and more effective communication/problem-solving. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Extensive controlled research. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Demonstrating treatment efficacy does not isolate whether behavioral, cognitive, emotional, alliance or expectancy changes caused improvement. |
| **Likely strengths** | Clear case formulation; observable targets; practical skills; well suited to concrete repeated conflicts; adaptable to depression, alcohol problems and health-related stress. |
| **Likely blind spots** | A rigid skills-and-beliefs approach can underread attachment injury, shame, dissociation, implicit emotion, power and meaning. Contemporary forms partly correct this. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Distressed couples are not simply irrational thinkers or deficient communicators. Accurate negative beliefs can exist. Teaching skills is not sufficient when emotional safety or motivation is absent. |

### 8.2 Behavioral roots

Traditional Behavioral Couple Therapy conceptualized distress partly as an unfavorable exchange in which partners no longer provide enough reinforcing behavior and increasingly respond aversively.

Interventions therefore targeted increasing desired positive behavior, reducing aversive exchanges, specifying requests, reinforcing partner change, communication, problem-solving, and negotiation.

### 8.3 The cognitive expansion

Cognitive approaches added the recognition that the same partner behavior can produce radically different responses depending on how it is construed.

Relevant cognitive processes include selective attention, attribution of cause, attribution of intention, expectations, assumptions, standards for relationships, beliefs about change, beliefs about conflict, and interpretations of emotional states.

For example, the same event, a partner arriving home late, may be interpreted as "work held her up," "I do not matter," "she is irresponsible," "he is controlling," or "this proves I cannot rely on anyone." The event is identical. The emotional and behavioral consequences are not.

### 8.4 Modern CBCT is broader than communication-skills training

Contemporary Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy can include emotional vulnerability, chronic external stress, psychopathology, family-of-origin beliefs, cultural standards, sexual problems, partner support, coping with illness, behavioral activation, cognitive reappraisal, and problem-specific protocols.

### 8.5 Evidence and mechanism

Treatment success does not identify a single mediator. Possible mechanisms include more positive behavior, fewer aversive exchanges, improved attribution, increased behavioral flexibility, greater perceived partner responsiveness, successful joint problem-solving, reduction of hopelessness, stronger alliance, and increased emotion regulation.

Different couples may improve through different paths.

### 8.6 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Strongly specified and testable. |
| **Clinician experience** | Mature manuals and broad adaptations. |
| **Process research** | Substantial but does not reduce outcome to one mechanism. |
| **Efficacy** | One of the strongest direct controlled literatures in couple therapy. |
| **Effectiveness** | Adapted and delivered in multiple routine settings and problem-specific protocols. |

## 9. Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy

### 9.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Developed by Andrew Christensen and Neil Jacobson from Traditional Behavioral Couple Therapy, integrating behavioral change with contextual and acceptance-based work. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Persistent partner differences, emotional sensitivities, external stress and the interactional pattern through which partners respond to them. |
| **Theory of distress** | Differences become polarized when each partner attempts to force the other to change. Coercive change efforts create reciprocal traps and make vulnerabilities increasingly difficult to disclose. |
| **Central mechanism** | Balance acceptance and change. Reduce polarization, increase compassionate understanding, and change behavior where change is feasible and valuable. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | The DEEP formulation: Differences, Emotional sensitivities, External stressors and Patterns of interaction. |
| **Therapist stance** | Collaborative, functional and nonmoralizing. |
| **Main intervention types** | Empathic joining, unified detachment, tolerance building, behavioral exchange, communication and problem-solving. |
| **Role of emotion** | Important, particularly soft disclosure, emotional sensitivities and empathic joining. |
| **Role of cognition** | Meaning and attribution matter, but cognitive disputation is less central than in classic cognitive therapy. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Central, both as change target and as functional component of the interactional trap. |
| **Role of attachment** | Compatible but not theoretically necessary. |
| **Role of history** | Used to understand emotional sensitivities and recurring differences, not as the sole explanation. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Core. Polarization and mutual coercion maintain distress. |
| **Role of power/context** | External stress is explicitly included. Acceptance must not normalize violence or coercion. |
| **Mechanism of change** | When partners stop treating every difference as a defect requiring correction, defensiveness decreases and empathy, flexibility and voluntary behavior change become more possible. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Strong randomized and longer-term comparative evidence. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Acceptance and behavior change covary with many other processes. Long-term relapse and separation remain possible. |
| **Likely strengths** | Chronic resentment, entrenched differences, polarization, repeated failed change campaigns and conflicts where both acceptance and concrete change are needed. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Severe attachment trauma, dissociation or coercive control can exceed a difference-and-polarization formulation. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Acceptance does not mean resignation, forgiveness on demand, tolerance of abuse, or remaining in a relationship. |

### 9.2 The DEEP formulation

A compact Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy formulation asks about **Differences**, **Emotional sensitivities**, **External stressors**, and **Patterns of interaction**.

Differences may include temperament, preferences, sex drive, sociability, spending, cleanliness, parenting, emotional expression, ambition, routines, and risk tolerance.

Emotional sensitivities can include rejection, criticism, abandonment, inadequacy, control, shame, failure, and invisibility.

External stressors include work, money, illness, children, caregiving, migration, discrimination, sleep loss and family demands.

The interactional question is: how does each person's attempt to solve the problem become part of the problem?

### 9.3 Acceptance is an active process

Acceptance commonly uses two strategies.

### Empathic joining

Partners describe painful experience beneath accusation. The objective is not merely catharsis. The disclosure should change the interpersonal function of the conversation.

### Unified detachment

The couple steps back and observes the pattern as a shared object: "Here is the sequence we repeatedly get pulled into."

Tolerance work may also reduce unnecessary struggle around irritating but non-destructive behavior.

A crucial boundary is explicit: destructive or dangerous behavior is not an appropriate target for simple acceptance. The model distinguishes tolerating enduring differences from tolerating violence, battering, dangerous addictions or other conduct that jeopardizes safety.

### 9.4 Change remains part of IBCT

IBCT integrates **acceptance + behavior change**. As polarization decreases, direct change may become easier because requests are no longer experienced as coercive verdicts on character.

### 9.5 Long-term evidence

Long-term comparative studies of traditional and integrative behavioral couple therapy show meaningful improvement for many couples while also showing substantial relapse, continued distress and separation. Successful couple therapy does not guarantee permanent immunity from future relationship distress.

### 9.6 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Clear, functional and testable. |
| **Clinician experience** | Mature manualized tradition. |
| **Process research** | Supports roles for both behavior change and acceptance, but neither is an exclusive mechanism. |
| **Efficacy** | Strong controlled evidence. |
| **Effectiveness** | Includes in-person and lower-intensity dissemination formats. |

## 10. Systemic approaches as an umbrella

### 10.1 "Systemic therapy" is not one treatment

The word *systemic* covers several historically distinct traditions: structural, strategic, Bowen family systems, Milan-derived traditions, solution-focused, narrative and social-constructionist approaches, integrative systemic therapies, and problem-specific family and couple interventions.

They share a broad shift from asking "What is wrong inside this individual?" toward asking "How does this problem operate within patterns of relationship and context?" Beyond that, their theories can differ radically.

### 10.2 Model profile

| Dimension | Systemic family of approaches |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | General systems theory, cybernetics, communication theory, feedback, family therapy and later constructivist, social-constructionist and contextual developments. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Interactional systems and the contexts within which they are embedded. |
| **Theory of distress** | Problems can be organized and maintained by reciprocal feedback, relational rules, boundaries, triangulation, attempted solutions, meaning systems and contextual pressures. |
| **Central mechanism** | Alter the organization, meaning or recursive pattern that sustains the problem. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Sequences rather than isolated acts, family and couple organization, resources, feedback loops, alliances, context, intergenerational relations and external systems. |
| **Therapist stance** | Varies enormously from expert and strategic to collaborative and "not-knowing." |
| **Main intervention types** | Reframing, enactment, boundary work, directives, pattern interruption, circular questions, genograms, externalization, solution-building and multilevel intervention. |
| **Role of emotion** | Varies. Central in some contemporary systemic models, secondary in older strategic forms. |
| **Role of cognition** | Meaning and attribution are often relationally framed. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Strong in structural and strategic traditions. |
| **Role of attachment** | Compatible with systemic formulations but not intrinsic to all systemic schools. |
| **Role of history** | Varies from deliberately present-focused strategic therapy to multigenerational Bowen work. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Defining feature across many systemic traditions. |
| **Role of power/context** | Increasingly central, particularly in feminist, narrative and multicultural revisions. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Disrupt recursive maintenance, introduce new relational organization and meaning, mobilize resources, alter communication and increase systemic flexibility. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Broad evidence exists for systemic interventions across relationship and adult clinical problems. |
| **Evidence limitations** | "Systemic" evidence pools heterogeneous treatments, populations and targets. Broad effectiveness does not validate every systemic concept or every school. |
| **Likely strengths** | Prevents individual scapegoating; highlights context, reciprocity and feedback; useful where problems involve families, children, health, caregiving or wider systems. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Circularity can obscure unilateral harm if applied carelessly. Some older approaches gave insufficient attention to gender, coercion, culture and subjective emotional experience. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Recursive interaction does not mean symmetrical power, equal contribution or equal responsibility. |

### 10.3 Evidence base

Systemic intervention as a broad family of practices has meaningful empirical support, but that does not mean every systemic theory or technique has independently been proven.

## 11. Bowen-oriented couple work

### 11.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Bowen Family Systems Couple Coaching |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Murray Bowen's multigenerational family systems theory, with an explicit attempt to conceptualize family emotional processes as natural-system phenomena. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Multigenerational emotional system. |
| **Theory of distress** | Chronic anxiety increases fusion, emotional reactivity, triangulation, cutoff, overfunctioning-underfunctioning and loss of thoughtful self-definition. |
| **Central mechanism** | Increase differentiation of self and reduce reactive participation in triangles. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Differentiation, triangles, chronic anxiety, family-of-origin relationships, cutoff, projection process, multigenerational transmission, sibling position and nuclear family emotional processes. |
| **Therapist stance** | Coach rather than emotional rescuer; calm, observational, detriangled and oriented toward thoughtful functioning. |
| **Main intervention types** | Family mapping, process questions, coaching "I-position," direct work on family-of-origin relationships, detriangling and reduction of automatic reactivity. |
| **Role of emotion** | Emotion is important mainly as part of an automatic emotional system and chronic anxiety rather than as material to heighten experientially. |
| **Role of cognition** | Thoughtful observation and principled self-definition are used to reduce emotionally driven reactivity. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Direct relationship behavior changes as clients reduce reactive patterns. |
| **Role of attachment** | Bowen uses attachment-related ideas differently from contemporary adult attachment theory. Fusion and unresolved family attachment are framed within multigenerational emotional process. |
| **Role of history** | Central. Present couple functioning is viewed in multigenerational context. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Important, especially triangles, conflict-distance sequences and overfunctioning-underfunctioning reciprocity. |
| **Role of power/context** | Societal emotional processes are included conceptually, but modern structural power analysis is not the theory's primary language. |
| **Mechanism of change** | A partner who can stay connected while maintaining a clearer self-position reduces automatic reactivity and changes the functioning of the wider emotional system. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Direct couple-treatment randomized evidence is sparse. Some observational and longitudinal research tests Bowen-derived constructs. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Differentiation theory has more correlational and conceptual support than randomized evidence showing Bowen coaching produces superior couple outcomes. |
| **Likely strengths** | Chronic family entanglement, triangulation, in-law conflict, repeated multigenerational patterns, emotional cutoff and overfunctioning-underfunctioning. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Acute attachment injury and immediate emotional repair receive less emphasis; differentiation can be moralized if mistakenly treated as a simple maturity score. |
| **What should not be inferred** | A theoretical differentiation continuum is not a precise objective rating of a person's worth or health. Low differentiation is not an established explanation for every symptom. |

### 11.2 Differentiation

Bowen's central concept is the ability to define and maintain a self while remaining in meaningful emotional contact with important others. This is not simple independence.

High differentiation is intended to mean connection without fusion, and individuality without cutoff. Low differentiation can produce either apparent closeness or apparent independence. Emotional cutoff can look autonomous while actually being organized by unresolved reactivity.

### 11.3 Triangles

When anxiety rises in a two-person system, involving a third person can stabilize the dyad. The third may be a child, parent, in-law, friend, colleague, affair partner, symptom, or therapist.

Bowen-oriented work therefore pays close attention to how the therapist may themselves be recruited into a triangle. The coach attempts to remain connected without becoming the judge or ally of one partner.

### 11.4 Therapist as coach

Bowen deliberately preferred a coaching metaphor. The therapist's own capacity to remain calm, thoughtful and nonreactive is part of the intervention. Unlike emotionally focused models, the coach is less likely to heighten emotion. The objective is often to make emotional process more observable and less automatic.

### 11.5 Evidence

Direct randomized evidence for Bowen couple coaching is sparse. Some construct-oriented and longitudinal research examines differentiation, intergenerational fusion and nuclear-family functioning.

### 11.6 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Extensive and internally coherent. |
| **Clinician experience** | Longstanding clinical tradition. |
| **Process research** | Limited compared with behavioral and emotionally focused traditions. |
| **Efficacy** | Sparse direct randomized evidence for the couple-coaching package. |
| **Effectiveness** | Insufficiently studied relative to its clinical longevity. |

## 12. Brief strategic couple therapy

### 12.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Brief Strategic Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Palo Alto Mental Research Institute, communication theory, cybernetics and brief problem-focused therapy. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Current complaint and the interactional "attempted solutions" that maintain it. |
| **Theory of distress** | Problems persist because well-intended responses repeatedly reproduce or escalate the problem. |
| **Central mechanism** | Interrupt the maintaining response and create a discontinuity in the feedback loop. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Exactly what happens, what each person does next, what has been tried, and how attempted solutions affect the complaint. |
| **Therapist stance** | Strategic, active and pragmatic, historically more expert and directive than contemporary collaborative models. |
| **Main intervention types** | Reframing, directives, reversal of attempted solutions, paradoxical or indirect suggestions, "less of the same," prescribing or interrupting patterns. |
| **Role of emotion** | Secondary to the interactional sequence, although emotional responses may be part of the loop. |
| **Role of cognition** | Client beliefs and worldview matter primarily because interventions must be framed in a way the client can use. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Central. Current interactional behavior maintains the complaint. |
| **Role of attachment** | Not central. |
| **Role of history** | Intentionally deemphasized unless history informs present attempted solutions. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Core. |
| **Role of power/context** | Older strategic methods can become therapist-heavy and can underanalyse structural power if not modernized. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Stop or reverse the attempted solution, weakening the positive feedback loop that sustains the problem. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Some outcome evidence exists within broader systemic and problem-specific traditions; direct mechanism evidence is limited. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Case success does not prove that attempted-solution interruption alone caused change. Sequential process evidence is comparatively sparse. |
| **Likely strengths** | Highly repetitive, concrete, self-defeating interactional loops; situations in which "trying harder" has made the problem worse. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Trauma, attachment injury, identity, developmental meaning, culture and coercion can be underread. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Present maintenance does not prove that origins are irrelevant to all treatment, or that every problem is caused by attempted solutions. |

### 12.2 The attempted-solution principle

The model asks: what are you repeatedly doing to solve the problem, and how might that response be maintaining it?

Examples include one partner demanding discussion while the other withdraws, one checking for reassurance while the other becomes secretive, one reminding while the other resists, or both repeatedly analysing disagreements until every disagreement becomes a relationship crisis.

### 12.3 Mechanism evidence

The model makes a relatively specific causal prediction: change in the attempted solution should precede and relate to change in the complaint. Direct evidence for this sequential dependency is limited and heavily based on case material.

## 13. Structural couple therapy

### 13.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Structural Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Salvador Minuchin's structural family therapy. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Couple as a subsystem within the wider family system. |
| **Theory of distress** | Maladaptive organization of boundaries, coalitions, roles, hierarchy and transactions restricts adaptive functioning. |
| **Central mechanism** | Change the structure through live interaction so healthier transactional patterns become possible. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Boundaries, subsystems, hierarchy, alliances, coalitions, enmeshment, disengagement, cross-generational organization and contextual demands. |
| **Therapist stance** | Active, joining and strategically involved in reorganizing interaction. |
| **Main intervention types** | Joining, enactment, boundary making, unbalancing, reframing, intensification and restructuring. |
| **Role of emotion** | Important within interaction, but structural organization is the primary analytic lens. |
| **Role of cognition** | Secondary. Meaning is often changed through enacted relationship change rather than formal cognitive work. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Very strong. The therapist works with live interaction. |
| **Role of attachment** | Not foundational, although boundaries and responsiveness can overlap with attachment concerns. |
| **Role of history** | Less central than present structure, except where historical organization persists in current family form. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Strong, especially repeated family transactions. |
| **Role of power/context** | Hierarchy and influence are central within the system, but cultural assumptions about "appropriate" structure require caution. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Enact new transactions, reorganize boundaries and coalitions, then allow the couple/family to stabilize a more adaptive structure. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Structural family therapy has a meaningful broader family evidence base, but direct couple-specific evidence is thinner. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Much empirical support comes from family treatments involving children or young people rather than couple therapy alone. |
| **Likely strengths** | Couples embedded in family conflict, parenting coalitions, blurred generational boundaries, intrusive extended family or chronic disengagement. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Individual subjective meaning, implicit trauma and attachment can receive less depth; therapist-defined "healthy structure" can become culturally normative. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Enmeshment or disengagement is not an objective diagnosis independent of context, culture, developmental stage and the couple's own goals. |

### 13.2 Boundaries and structure

Structural theory uses the concepts of subsystems, boundaries, enmeshment, disengagement, hierarchy, and alliances. Diffuse boundaries can leave roles poorly differentiated. Rigid boundaries can isolate resources.

### 13.3 Enactment

A structural therapist often wants to see the problem rather than hear only a retrospective account of it. Live enactment can reveal who speaks for whom, who withdraws, who interrupts, whether a child is pulled into the parental subsystem, whether one partner is excluded, how decisions are made, and where boundaries become rigid or diffuse.

### 13.4 Cultural caution

Structural language can easily become normative. Questions such as how close is "too close," how autonomous is "too distant," who should lead, or what a parental hierarchy should look like cannot be answered independently of culture, class, gender, development and the couple's values.

### 13.5 Evidence

Much structural outcome research concerns family treatment rather than couple therapy specifically. Therefore it provides indirect rather than strong direct couple-specific support.

## 14. Narrative couple therapy

### 14.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Narrative Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Postmodernism, social constructionism, Foucault-inspired analysis of discourse and power, Michael White and David Epston. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Stories, identities, discourse, relational meaning and sociocultural context. |
| **Theory of distress** | Problem-saturated stories become dominant, narrow identity, obscure exceptions, and can reproduce social discourses and unequal power. |
| **Central mechanism** | Separate persons from problems, recover neglected knowledge and agency, and develop richer alternative narratives. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | How the problem is named, what story organizes it, whose account is privileged, how power operates, what exceptions exist, what values and preferred identities are hidden. |
| **Therapist stance** | Collaborative, decentered but influential, curious and reflexive about power. |
| **Main intervention types** | Externalizing conversations, mapping influence, unique outcomes, double listening, re-authoring, thickening preferred stories, therapeutic documents, outsider witnessing and definitional ceremonies. |
| **Role of emotion** | Emotion is meaningful within lived stories and relationships rather than treated as a separate intrapsychic mechanism. |
| **Role of cognition** | Meaning is central, but the model resists treating beliefs merely as distorted individual cognitions. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Actions provide evidence for preferred identities and alternative stories. |
| **Role of attachment** | Can be incorporated, but is not the master theory. |
| **Role of history** | History is actively reconstructed around neglected events, values, resistance and alternative meanings. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Can be externalized and mapped, but discourse and meaning receive more emphasis than cybernetic sequencing. |
| **Role of power/context** | Core. Social norms and power relations can shape the relationship and the stories available to partners. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Externalization reduces identity fusion with the problem; attention to exceptions and values increases agency; repeated enactment and retelling strengthen alternative relational identities. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Rich qualitative, clinical and practice-based literature; comparatively limited couple-specific randomized evidence. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Preferred qualitative epistemology makes direct comparison with manualized treatments less common. Mechanism claims are difficult to isolate experimentally. |
| **Likely strengths** | Shame, identity conflict, oppressive labels, cultural narratives, marginalized identities, power and relationships trapped in rigid stories. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Severe physiological dysregulation, attachment injury, concrete skill deficits or psychopathology can be underformulated if all problems are translated into discourse. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Narrative therapy does not claim facts are irrelevant. Externalizing a problem does not remove personal responsibility. Re-authoring is not positive thinking. |

### 14.2 Power and discourse

Narrative therapy brought an important correction to theories that describe only the internal couple system. Partners live inside broader systems of meaning concerning gender, sexuality, race, class, disability, parenthood, monogamy, masculinity and femininity, success, family, religion, and normality.

### 14.3 Externalization

The signature move is captured by the principle: the person is not identical to the problem.

Instead of "he is avoidant," "she is controlling," or "we are toxic," therapy may ask how avoidance recruits each partner, when control takes over the conversation, what distrust persuades each person to do, and when the relationship has resisted that pattern.

Externalization changes the grammar of the problem, which can reduce shame and reopen agency. Externalization must not become exoneration. "The violence is the problem" is insufficient if it obscures who committed the violent act.

### 14.4 Evidence

Narrative couple therapy has extensive clinical description but a much smaller conventional outcome literature. Clinical and qualitative credibility is not the same thing as randomized efficacy evidence.

## 15. Solution-Focused Brief Couple Therapy

### 15.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Solution-Focused Brief Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Milwaukee Brief Family Therapy Center, Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg, pragmatic and social-constructionist influences. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Client-defined goals, exceptions, existing competence, language and observable movement toward a preferred future. |
| **Theory of distress** | Detailed causal explanation is often considered unnecessary. Persistent problem talk and repeated unsuccessful responses may obscure exceptions, resources and possible alternatives. |
| **Central mechanism** | Shift attention and action toward exceptions, competence, achievable goals and preferred futures. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | What clients want, what is already working, exceptions, resources, signs of progress and client-generated definitions of success. |
| **Therapist stance** | Collaborative consultant; client treated as expert on their life and goals. |
| **Main intervention types** | Miracle question, scaling, exception questions, coping questions, compliments, future-oriented questions, small behavioral experiments and EARS: elicit, amplify, reinforce, start again. |
| **Role of emotion** | Acknowledged but usually not deeply processed as an independent mechanism. |
| **Role of cognition** | Language and attention shape perceived possibilities. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Small observable changes are strongly emphasized. |
| **Role of attachment** | Not foundational. |
| **Role of history** | Used selectively to identify previous successes or useful exceptions rather than reconstruct causes. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Relevant insofar as a different interaction can become part of the solution. |
| **Role of power/context** | Client-defined goals support pluralism, but the tradition has often been relatively apolitical unless clients introduce structural issues. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Attention to exceptions and resources increases expectancy, agency and useful behavior; small changes can generate reinforcing feedback. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Broader Solution-Focused Brief Therapy research shows small positive effects across diverse problems and, in some comparisons, outcomes similar to established treatments. Couple-specific evidence is smaller. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Broad SFBT evidence should not automatically be treated as couple-specific treatment evidence. Mechanisms such as hope, expectancy and alliance may account for some effects. |
| **Likely strengths** | Clear goals, relatively circumscribed problems, demoralization, couples with underused strengths, time-limited work and situations where lengthy causal reconstruction offers little added value. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Severe attachment trauma, coercion, complex psychopathology, entrenched shame and structural power may require more assessment and depth. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Solution-focused does not mean ignoring suffering, denying history, or forcing optimism. |

### 15.2 Problem-focused versus solution-focused brief therapy

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy diverged from the Mental Research Institute strategic tradition in a subtle but important way.

The strategic question is: "What attempted solution maintains the problem?"

The solution-focused question is: "When is the problem absent or less severe, and what useful difference is already occurring?"

### 15.3 Typical tools

### Miracle question

The couple describes observable signs that would show the problem had been resolved.

### Scaling

A 0-10 or 1-10 scale turns vague change into gradations.

### Exceptions

Search for times when the expected problem did not occur.

### EARS

Later sessions commonly **Elicit** what is better, **Amplify** it, **Reinforce** useful agency, and **Start again** by looking for the next improvement.

### 15.4 Evidence

Broader SFBT research generally finds small positive outcomes across diverse populations and sometimes similar outcomes to established treatments with less treatment time. This is not all couple-specific research, equivalence in one population does not imply equivalence for all presenting problems, and brief duration may partly reflect case selection.

## 16. Psychodynamic and object-relations couple therapy

### 16.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Psychodynamic / Object-Relations Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Psychoanalysis, British object relations, Kleinian and post-Kleinian thinking, self psychology and later couple psychoanalysis. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Each partner's internal relational world plus the couple's shared enactments and defensive organization. |
| **Theory of distress** | Early relational patterns, unresolved conflicts and internal object relations are projected into the partnership and enacted through splitting, projection, projective identification and repetitive role configurations. |
| **Central mechanism** | Make unconscious or poorly symbolized relational processes more thinkable, reduce projection, integrate split-off experience, and create a different relational experience. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Partner choice, developmental history, object relations, projection, projective identification, splitting, narcissistic vulnerability, dependency, transference, countertransference, shared unconscious fantasy and couple defensive systems. |
| **Therapist stance** | Reflective, containing, interpretive and attentive to both individual minds and the couple as a psychological system. |
| **Main intervention types** | Clarification, interpretation, linking present and past, observing enactment, working with transference and countertransference, containing unmentalized affect and developing a reflective "third" position. |
| **Role of emotion** | Central, particularly affects that are split off, projected, dissociated or enacted rather than articulated. |
| **Role of cognition** | Insight and reflective understanding are important, but not usually framed as cognitive restructuring. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Behavior is read partly as enactment carrying unconscious meaning. |
| **Role of attachment** | Related historically but not reducible to modern attachment theory. |
| **Role of history** | Central. The relationship can become a stage upon which unresolved internal relational patterns are replayed. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Important, but cycles are interpreted through projection, internal objects and enactment rather than only feedback. |
| **Role of power/context** | Contemporary psychoanalytic couple work increasingly addresses gender, culture and social context, though classical formulations often underemphasized them. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Greater capacity to think rather than enact; withdrawal of projections; integration of split representations; recognition of partner separateness; corrective relational experience. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Naturalistic effectiveness evidence exists. Direct randomized couple-treatment evidence is much thinner than for behavioral and emotionally focused therapies. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Insight, projective identification and countertransference interpretations are difficult to operationalize and test as specific causal mediators. |
| **Likely strengths** | Repetition that feels disproportionate to present events, chronic projection, polarized idealization/devaluation, narcissistic injury, dependency, enactment and emotionally loaded partner choice. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Can overinterpret history, privilege therapist inference, become slow or opaque, or underuse concrete behavior change. |
| **What should not be inferred** | A persuasive interpretation is not automatically true. Countertransference is potential clinical information, not objective evidence. Insight is not automatically the active ingredient of change. |

### 16.2 Projective identification as a couple process

Object-relations couple therapy views some intense interactions as reenactments in which a partner is pulled into a role that fits the other's prior relational world.

A simple example:

- Partner A carries an expectation of being judged.
- Ambiguous criticism activates shame.
- A becomes defensive or submissive.
- Partner B experiences the defensiveness as evasiveness.
- B becomes more prosecutorial.
- B now behaves increasingly like the judging figure A expected.
- A experiences the original expectation as confirmed.

A behavioral model can describe the same sequence without projective-identification language. The theoretical difference concerns why this particular sequence has such emotional force and why these roles are so compelling.

### 16.3 The therapist's position

The therapist slows the interaction, observes emotional shifts, asks what the exchange means, attends to their own emotional responses, links present experience with prior relational themes when warranted, and helps partners distinguish the actual present partner from projected or expected figures.

That is the model's theory of therapeutic mechanism. It should not be rewritten as already demonstrated causal fact.

### 16.4 Tavistock: the couple state of mind

Mary Morgan's Tavistock formulation adds the idea that successful couple work requires a **couple state of mind**.

The partners need to become capable of thinking about themselves, the other person, and the relationship itself.

The "third" position allows both partners to reflect on the shared relationship while retaining their individual subjective positions.

This overlaps conceptually with mentalization, metacommunication, unified detachment, systemic observation of the cycle, and the Gottman idea of jointly examining a perpetual problem. But the theories explaining that reflective stance are not identical.

### 16.5 Effectiveness evidence

Naturalistic studies provide meaningful effectiveness evidence for psychodynamic couple therapy, including improvement in mood and relationship satisfaction in routine clinical populations. This is not equivalent to randomized evidence proving treatment-specific efficacy or proving that insight caused improvement.

### 16.6 Evidence ledger

| Evidence type | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Model theory** | Very rich and clinically detailed. |
| **Clinician experience** | Deep, longstanding tradition. |
| **Process research** | More limited and difficult to operationalize than in manualized treatments. |
| **Efficacy** | Comparatively sparse randomized couple-specific evidence. |
| **Effectiveness** | Meaningful naturalistic evidence exists, including large routine-practice cohorts. |

## 17. Relational psychoanalytic couple therapy

### 17.1 Model profile

| Dimension | Relational Psychoanalytic Couple Therapy |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Contemporary relational psychoanalysis, interpersonal psychoanalysis, object relations, self psychology, intersubjectivity and developmental research. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Two interacting subjectivities plus the emergent relationship between them. |
| **Theory of distress** | Partners become organized into repetitive relational configurations in which aspects of self are dissociated, recognition collapses and each person attempts to preserve subjectivity by negating the other's. |
| **Central mechanism** | Increase mutual recognition, integrate dissociated self-states and create more negotiable relational configurations. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Subjective realities, developmental history, relational enactments, mutual recognition, self-state shifts, gender, culture, power and recurring relational configurations. |
| **Therapist stance** | Participatory, intersubjective, curious and reflexive rather than classically neutral. |
| **Main intervention types** | Attunement, exploration of enactment, interpretation, disclosure when clinically useful, negotiation of divergent realities, recognition of self-states and repair of relational impasses. |
| **Role of emotion** | Central as lived intersubjective experience. |
| **Role of cognition** | Reflective awareness matters, but meaning is co-created relationally. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Behavior is part of enactment and relational negotiation. |
| **Role of attachment** | Relevant but not the sole organizing model. |
| **Role of history** | Important because prior relational experience shapes self-states and expectations, but history is reactivated within current interaction. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Strong, understood as co-created enactment rather than only behavioral feedback. |
| **Role of power/context** | More explicit than classical psychoanalysis; gender, culture and power form part of subjectivity and relationship. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Previously dissociated experience becomes speakable; each partner becomes more able to recognize the other as a separate subject; rigid enactments become open to negotiation. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Predominantly theoretical, clinical and case-based in the supplied source. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Direct controlled couple-outcome and mediator research is limited. |
| **Likely strengths** | Complex identity issues, mutual negation, shame, dissociation, enactment, developmental repetition and couples for whom simple skills interventions feel superficial. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Can become conceptually dense, difficult to manualize, and less efficient for practical circumscribed problems. |
| **What should not be inferred** | Relational theory does not give the therapist privileged access to an objective hidden truth. Mutual influence does not imply symmetrical power or accountability. |

### 17.2 Ringstrom's three organizing themes

Ringstrom describes relational psychoanalytic couple therapy around three broad themes:

1. actualization of self-experience in intimate relationship;
2. mutual recognition versus mutual negation;
3. the relationship having a psychological life of its own.

A relational question is:

> Can I remain a subject while recognizing that you are also a subject whose reality is not reducible to mine?

That question is particularly relevant when conflict becomes organized around "If your experience is valid, mine cannot be," "If I recognize your pain, I lose my claim," "If you are separate from me, I disappear," or "If I depend on you, I surrender agency."

### 17.3 Mutual recognition versus attachment responsiveness

**Attachment responsiveness** asks whether the partner is accessible and responsive when needed.

**Mutual recognition** asks whether each partner can recognize the other's separate subjectivity without erasing their own.

A partner can be caring yet controlling. A relationship can be securely attached in some respects while still struggling with recognition, identity and autonomy.

## 18. Integrative and common-factor approaches

### 18.1 Why integration exists

Couple distress is multiply determined.

A single couple may simultaneously have an attachment injury, hostile attributions, poor conflict skills, chronic external stress, sexual avoidance, family-of-origin triangulation, shame, depressive symptoms, unresolved betrayal, different expectations about closeness, coercive attempts to change each other, and a weak therapeutic alliance.

No single level automatically deserves causal priority.

Integrative approaches therefore ask: which constraints are operating in this couple, in which system, and through which mechanism?

### 18.2 Integrative Problem-Centered Metaframeworks

The Integrative Problem-Centered Metaframeworks approach treats formulation as a changing set of hypotheses rather than a fixed diagnosis. Relevant domains can include organization, sequences, development, culture, gender, mind, biology, spirituality, family and wider systems.

The couple's problem narrative is co-created and revised as new evidence appears. This is a different form of integration from simply collecting favourite techniques.

### Model profile

| Dimension | Integrative Problem-Centered / common-factor approaches |
|---|---|
| **Historical and theoretical basis** | Psychotherapy integration, systemic metaframeworks, common-factor research, alliance research and measurement-based care. |
| **Unit of analysis** | Individuals, couple, family, therapist, wider systems and treatment process. |
| **Theory of distress** | Problems are multiply determined and maintained by constraints operating across several levels. |
| **Central mechanism** | Match intervention to the maintaining constraint while preserving alliance and monitoring response. |
| **Typical assessment focus** | Presenting problem, interaction, individuals, family, development, culture, biology, attachment, cognition, emotion, power and treatment alliance. |
| **Therapist stance** | Hypothesis-driven but pluralistic and responsive. |
| **Main intervention types** | Selected from multiple traditions based on formulation and feedback. |
| **Role of emotion** | Potentially central when formulation supports it. |
| **Role of cognition** | Potentially central. |
| **Role of behaviour** | Potentially central. |
| **Role of attachment** | One clinically important framework among several. |
| **Role of history** | Used when historically derived constraints remain active. |
| **Role of interactional cycle** | Usually important but may not be the only level of intervention. |
| **Role of power/context** | Explicitly available within multilevel formulation. |
| **Mechanism of change** | Reduce the particular constraints sustaining the problem, strengthen alliance, mobilize resources and revise treatment when outcome data suggest insufficient progress. |
| **Treatment evidence** | Supported indirectly by evidence that multiple couple treatments work and that different mechanisms may matter for different couples. |
| **Evidence limitations** | Integration can become eclectic and theoretically loose unless formulation specifies why an intervention is being used. |
| **Likely strengths** | Complex comorbidity, heterogeneous couples, multi-problem presentations and cases requiring coordination with other treatments. |
| **Likely blind spots** | Too much flexibility can reduce treatment fidelity and make mechanisms difficult to test. |
| **What should not be inferred** | "Integrative" does not mean all theories are equally supported, mutually compatible or interchangeable. |

### 18.3 Common factors

### Model-specific mechanisms

Examples include attachment enactments in Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy, attribution restructuring in Cognitive-Behavioral Couple Therapy, acceptance in Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy, attempted-solution interruption in strategic therapy, projection withdrawal in psychodynamic therapy, and externalization in narrative therapy.

### Common mechanisms

Processes potentially shared across models include therapeutic alliance, hope and expectancy, emotional safety, empathy, validation, exposure to difficult relational experience, reduced arousal, new interactional experience, changed attribution, increased agency, partner responsiveness, and practice outside therapy.

The existence of common factors does not mean specific models are theoretically redundant.

## 19. Cross-model mechanisms of change

### 19.1 Mechanism matrix

| Plausible mechanism | Models where especially explicit | What changes | Evidence caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Therapeutic alliance** | All models; integrative approaches make it especially explicit | Both partners experience therapist as credible, fair, collaborative and useful | Couple alliance is more complex than individual alliance because alliance with each partner and the within-couple alliance can diverge |
| **Emotional safety** | Emotionally Focused, emotion-focused, psychodynamic, relational | Vulnerability becomes tolerable enough to articulate | Safety is not merely subjective comfort; coercive relationships may be objectively unsafe |
| **De-escalation** | Emotionally Focused, Gottman, strategic, behavioral | Lower arousal and reduced attack-defend or pursue-withdraw escalation | De-escalation may be prerequisite rather than final mechanism |
| **Improved responsiveness** | Emotionally Focused, attachment theory, Gottman, relational | Partner becomes more accessible and responsive to expressed need | Responsiveness is trans-theoretical but theories differ on why it matters |
| **Changed attribution** | Cognitive-Behavioral, Integrative Behavioral, Gottman | Partner behavior is interpreted less globally, personally or maliciously | Benign attribution is not appropriate when evidence genuinely supports exploitation or danger |
| **New interactional experience** | Emotionally Focused, experiential, psychodynamic, relational, structural | Couple does something emotionally or behaviorally different in session | New experience must generalize beyond session |
| **Acceptance** | Integrative Behavioral, solution-focused, some experiential and mindfulness-informed work | Reduced coercive struggle with persistent differences | Acceptance must not be confused with tolerance of harm |
| **Behavioral change** | Behavioral, Cognitive-Behavioral, Integrative Behavioral, Gottman, structural | New actions alter reinforcement and partner response | Behavior change can be produced by multiple mechanisms |
| **Problem-solving** | Cognitive-Behavioral, Gottman, systemic, integrative | Couple handles solvable practical problems more effectively | Skill is less useful when arousal, coercion or attachment threat overwhelms access to the skill |
| **Self-regulation** | Greenberg-Goldman, Gottman, Cognitive-Behavioral, Bowen | Reduced flooding, impulsivity and dependency on partner regulation | Self-regulation should not be used to imply partners should become emotionally self-sufficient |
| **Empathy** | Experiential, Integrative Behavioral, psychodynamic, relational | Partner's behavior becomes understandable in context | Empathy does not remove accountability |
| **Mentalization** | Psychodynamic, relational, attachment-informed, integrative | Partners become better able to reflect on self and other minds under stress | Mentalization is a plausible trans-theoretical mechanism, not a proven explanation for all couple-treatment outcome |
| **Corrective relational experience** | Emotionally Focused, emotion-focused, psychodynamic, relational | A feared or expected relational outcome is contradicted by lived experience | Conceptually broad and difficult to isolate experimentally |
| **Agency** | Narrative, solution-focused, Bowen, relational, behavioral | Partners experience themselves as capable of intentional action | Agency should not be reduced to individual responsibility for structural constraints |
| **Meaning reconstruction** | Narrative, psychodynamic, Gottman shared meaning, relational | Couple changes the story through which events and the relationship are interpreted | Changed story can follow rather than cause improvement |
| **Reduced projection** | Psychodynamic, object relations, relational | Partner becomes more distinguishable from internal expectations | Projection is difficult to measure independently |
| **Detriangulation** | Bowen, structural, systemic | Dyad relies less on third parties or symptoms to regulate tension | Some third-party support is adaptive; "triangle" is not inherently pathological |
| **Hope and expectancy** | Solution-focused, common-factor approaches, all therapies | Change becomes imaginable and effort increases | Hope alone does not resolve severe violence, addiction or psychopathology |

### 19.2 Emotional safety

Most models require some degree of emotional safety even when they do not use that term.

Without safety, disclosure becomes dangerous, cognitive flexibility narrows, physiology becomes more reactive, listening becomes defensive, repair attempts are mistrusted, mentalization deteriorates, and new interaction cannot consolidate.

This is one reason several apparently different models first reduce escalation. Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy calls it de-escalation of the negative cycle. Gottman work emphasizes physiological soothing and lower destructive affect. Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy reduces polarization. Strategic therapy interrupts feedback. Psychodynamic work contains affect and slows enactment. Bowen work lowers reactivity.

Different theories, similar immediate regulatory requirement.

### 19.3 Responsiveness

Responsiveness can mean "you noticed me," "you understood," "you cared," "you changed your behavior," "you respected my boundary," "you took responsibility," "you helped," or "you did not retaliate." That makes responsiveness broader than reassurance.

### 19.4 Mentalization

Mentalization offers a useful bridging concept without becoming a substitute therapy model.

Under high affective arousal, people can lose the ability to hold complex representations of their own mind, the partner's mind, uncertainty, and multiple perspectives. The partner then becomes certain and simple: malicious, uncaring, controlling, selfish, impossible, dangerous, or needy.

Some couple therapies restore reflective capacity indirectly. De-escalation lowers arousal; unified detachment creates observational distance; narrative externalization loosens identity conclusions; psychodynamic interpretation connects affect with meaning; Bowen coaching reduces reactivity; Gottman self-soothing reduces physiological overload.

The overlap is meaningful, but it does not make these methods equivalent.

## 20. Attachment theory as a cross-model lens

Adult attachment theory is unusually influential because it has a large empirical literature independent of any one therapy.

Mikulincer and Shaver describe adult attachment through attachment-system activation, safe haven, secure base, working models of self and others, hyperactivation, deactivation, attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, emotion regulation, caregiving, and couple interaction.

Attachment research therefore provides a useful empirical language for relationship dynamics.

### 20.1 Attachment dimensions are not identities

"Anxious" and "avoidant" are better treated as dimensions, patterns or strategies than as fixed personality types.

A person can be more secure with one partner than another, become more anxious under particular threats, show both hyperactivation and deactivation at different times, and change over development and relationship experience.

### 20.2 Attachment does not explain every relationship motive

Partners also seek autonomy, status, identity validation, fairness, sexual satisfaction, companionship, practical cooperation, recognition, novelty, shared meaning, values, and resources.

### 20.3 Attachment security is not unlimited availability

A secure bond does not require constant reassurance, fusion, surrender of autonomy, tolerance of abuse, or unbounded emotional availability. Security should increase capacity for both connection and exploration.

## 21. Power, context and asymmetry

Couple therapies historically became more sophisticated as feminist, multicultural and social-constructionist critiques challenged the idea that the couple could be understood entirely as a closed two-person system.

Relevant context includes economic pressure, work demands, discrimination, disability, migration, social class, race and ethnicity, religion, caregiving, gender expectations, sexual orientation, family networks, housing, illness, legal status, and parenting demands.

A couple may appear to have a "communication problem" when the actual burden includes 70-hour workweeks, chronic illness, poverty, unequal domestic labor, discrimination, childcare exhaustion, an intrusive extended family, or financial dependence.

Context does not make interaction irrelevant. It changes the causal model.

### 21.1 Power is not reducible to personality

Power can arise from money, physical strength, immigration status, social legitimacy, control of housing, control of children, occupational status, cultural authority, access to information, control of sex or reproduction, and threat capacity.

A model that only asks how both partners "co-create" conflict can miss these asymmetries.

### 21.2 Systemic recursion with accountability

A safe formulation can hold two propositions simultaneously:

1. each partner's responses affect the interaction;
2. each person remains responsible for their own acts.

For ordinary conflict, mutual influence may be highly informative. For coercive abuse, moral and practical responsibility is not symmetrical simply because interaction is reciprocal.

## 23. Affairs and betrayal: what models see differently

An affair is a useful example because no single model exhausts the problem.

### Attachment formulation

The affair is a breach of availability, specialness, trust and secure bond. Relevant mechanisms include attachment injury, grief, abandonment alarm, and responsive repair.

### Behavioral formulation

Relevant questions include what boundaries changed, what reinforcement patterns supported secrecy, what behaviors are necessary for renewed reliability, and what concrete transparency or contact agreements are required.

### Cognitive formulation

Relevant processes include traumatic interpretations, global beliefs about self and partner, intrusive attribution, and catastrophic future expectations.

### Integrative Behavioral formulation

The affair may interact with enduring differences, emotional sensitivities, external stress, and polarization. Acceptance of partner differences does not mean acceptance of ongoing deception.

### Gottman-informed formulation

Trust requires repeated trustworthy choices after the betrayal, not one conversation.

### Psychodynamic formulation

The affair may also carry meanings related to self-esteem, desire, aggression, autonomy, dependency, ageing, identity, repetition, and triangulation.

### Narrative formulation

Relevant questions include what story the affair has imposed on each person's identity, what cultural meanings shape betrayal and forgiveness, whether one partner has become reduced to "betrayer" and the other to "victim," and whether accountability can be preserved while identity becomes more complex.

No framework removes the need to establish whether betrayal has actually ended.

## 24. Sexual problems

Couple sexual difficulties are particularly unsuitable for single-theory reduction.

Possible contributors include medical illness, pain, medications, hormones, trauma, desire discrepancy, resentment, attachment threat, sexual scripts, performance anxiety, shame, orientation, identity, relationship conflict, exhaustion, parenthood, ageing, body image, and coercion.

Therefore:

**sexual difficulty is not automatically a relationship symptom, and relationship distress is not automatically the cause of sexual difficulty.**

A credible couple formulation may need medical assessment, sex-therapy techniques, behavioral interventions, attachment work, emotion work, trauma treatment, practical scheduling, education, and medication review.

## 25. Individual psychopathology and the couple

A false dichotomy in older debates was: is the problem inside the individual or inside the relationship?

Contemporary evidence suggests that many problems are bidirectional.

### Depression

Depression can reduce activity and sexual interest, alter interpretation, increase irritability, burden the partner, and reduce responsiveness. Relationship distress can in turn worsen depression.

### Post-traumatic stress disorder

Trauma symptoms can increase hyperarousal, increase avoidance, reduce emotional availability, impair sexual intimacy, and create accommodation patterns. Partner responses can either reinforce avoidance or support recovery.

### Substance misuse

Substance use can destabilize trust and safety. Relationship contingencies can also influence drinking or recovery.

### Severe personality dysfunction

Intense affective instability, chronic suicidality, impulsivity, dissociation or severe personality dysfunction can overwhelm generic couple-treatment structures and may require coordinated individual treatment.

The principle is:

**a relational formulation should broaden clinical understanding, not erase the individual disorder.**

Likewise:

**an individual diagnosis should not erase the relationship system.**

## 26. Safety and the limits of conjoint couple work

Conjoint therapy assumes that speaking openly in front of the partner is sufficiently safe. That assumption must be tested, not presumed.

### 26.1 Situations requiring careful separate assessment

Potential concerns include physical assault, threats of serious violence, strangulation, weapon use or weapon threats, stalking, sexual coercion, coercive control, severe intimidation, fear of retaliation after sessions, forced financial dependence, restriction of movement, monitoring of communications, credible homicidal or suicidal threats, severe uncontrolled substance use, and inability to disagree safely.

### 26.2 Situational conflict versus coercive control

Not all couple aggression has the same structure. Some violence occurs during poorly regulated conflict where neither partner lives in sustained fear or subjugation. Specialized conjoint interventions have been studied for selected lower-risk couples.

This must not be generalized to relationships characterized by coercive control or terror.

The key clinical question is not merely whether the partners have ever pushed or hit each other. It is also: Who is afraid of whom? Can each person say no? Can either partner leave the room? Is there retaliation? Is one partner systematically controlled? Are threats credible? Are children endangered? Can confidential safety planning occur?

### 26.3 Why some standard couple interventions become unsafe

### Vulnerability work

Asking the controlled partner to disclose fears can give the coercive partner more information to exploit.

### Communication training

Telling both partners to "communicate better" can imply symmetry where there is domination.

### Systemic cycle reframing

Calling coercive abuse "the couple's cycle" can dilute responsibility.

### Acceptance

Acceptance of enduring personality differences is not acceptance of violence.

### Empathy

Understanding the history of an abusive partner does not reduce their accountability.

### Forgiveness

Forgiveness cannot be a treatment obligation or prerequisite for recovery.

### Attachment formulation

Fear of losing an attachment figure may help explain why someone behaves violently. It does not justify violence.

### 26.4 Decision principle

Where vulnerability itself is dangerous, **safety takes precedence over intimacy work**.

Possible responses include individual assessment, safety planning, specialist domestic-violence services, medical care, legal protection, addiction treatment, psychiatric assessment, and postponement or avoidance of conjoint work.

## 27. What the evidence does and does not say about "which therapy works"

Broad evidence synthesis indicates that couple therapy produces meaningful benefit for many distressed couples, with behavioral and emotionally focused approaches accounting for much of the direct relationship-distress trial literature.

### 27.1 Couple therapy as a class can work

Relationship distress is not merely an untreatable reflection of compatibility.

### 27.2 No model solves all cases

Nonresponse, dropout, relapse, separation and divorce remain substantial.

### 27.3 Several models likely share mechanisms

The fact that theoretically different therapies can produce broadly similar outcomes is consistent with common mechanisms such as alliance, hope, attention, de-escalation, increased empathy, behavioral change, emotional processing, and corrective interaction.

### 27.4 Similar outcomes do not imply identical therapy

Two interventions may both improve satisfaction while producing change through partly different pathways.

### 27.5 Model-specific theory often outruns mechanism evidence

This is especially important for attachment-security claims, psychodynamic interpretation, strategic attempted-solution claims, narrative re-authoring, differentiation, and Gottman observational constructs.

They may all be clinically useful without each theoretical proposition having been independently demonstrated as a mediator.

## 29. A mechanism-first way to think about model selection

Rather than asking "Which therapy is best?" a more defensible question is: **What appears to be maintaining distress in this couple, and which therapeutic mechanisms directly address it?**

The sequence can be represented as:

**Presenting problem**  
↓  
**Safety and contraindication assessment**  
↓  
**Maintaining mechanisms**  
↓  
**Treatment targets**  
↓  
**Intervention model or combination**  
↓  
**Monitor response**  
↓  
**Revise formulation if progress does not occur**

### 29.1 Example: severe pursuit-withdrawal

Possible formulation: attachment alarm, secondary anger, physiological flooding, threat-sensitive attributions, and withdrawal as protection.

Potential mechanisms: de-escalation, self-soothing, primary emotion access, responsive enactment, and cognitive reappraisal.

### 29.2 Example: chronic argument over money

Possible formulation: incompatible preferences, practical skill deficit, identity threat around competence, family-of-origin money beliefs, and actual unequal economic power.

Potential mechanisms: budgeting and negotiation, cognitive work, acceptance of enduring differences, emotion work around shame/control, and explicit power analysis.

Calling this only an attachment problem would be premature.

### 29.3 Example: emotionally distant relationship

Possible mechanisms include deactivated attachment, depression, exhaustion, unresolved resentment, sexual avoidance, trauma, habituation, parallel lives, fear of conflict, and normative preference for lower emotional intensity.

The topography alone does not identify the mechanism.

### 29.4 Example: recurring betrayal injury

Relevant questions include: Has the behavior actually stopped? Is there accountability? Is there genuine remorse? Is the injured partner safe enough to become vulnerable? Does the offender tolerate discussion without retaliation? Is the couple asking for repair, separation, clarification, or something else?

Only after those questions does a specific repair mechanism become meaningful.

## 30. Common clinical errors across models

### 30.1 Mistaking the model for the couple

If every distressed couple becomes an insecure attachment bond, a dysfunctional cognition, a differentiation problem, a projective-identification system, a set of Four Horsemen, or a problem-saturated narrative, then formulation has become confirmation.

### 30.2 Premature depth

A therapist can move too quickly into childhood, trauma, primary emotion, unconscious fantasy, or attachment injury when the couple first needs safety, stabilization, sleep, sobriety, behavioral boundaries, or de-escalation.

### 30.3 Premature skills

The reverse error is teaching active listening, problem-solving, soft start-up, or negotiation to partners who are too threatened, flooded, ashamed or coercively organized to use them.

### 30.4 Premature acceptance

Some differences should be accepted. Some behavior should change. Some behavior requires firm boundaries. Some relationships are unsafe. These categories cannot be collapsed.

### 30.5 Premature forgiveness

Repair may involve forgiveness, but forgiveness is an outcome or personal process, not a debt owed by the injured partner.

### 30.6 Overinterpreting history

Historical similarity can clarify present experience without proving historical causation.

### 30.7 Underestimating external stress

Not every deteriorating relationship contains an obscure intrapsychic problem. Sometimes people are exhausted, sick, poor, overworked or overwhelmed.

### 30.8 Therapist triangulation

Couple therapy creates unusual alliance complexity. Each partner may ask "Whose side are you on?" The therapist may be recruited into prosecutor, rescuer, judge, witness, ally, secret keeper, or parent. Different models conceptualize this differently, but every model must manage it.

## 31. Practical differences in therapist stance

| Model | Therapist tends to function most like |
|---|---|
| Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy | Emotional process choreographer and attachment-focused systemic therapist |
| Greenberg-Goldman Emotion-Focused | Experiential emotion coach and process facilitator |
| Gottman | Structured assessor, educator and interaction coach |
| Cognitive-Behavioral | Collaborative formulation expert, trainer and coach |
| Integrative Behavioral | Functional analyst balancing acceptance and change |
| Bowen | Detriangled coach |
| Strategic | Pragmatic strategist disrupting feedback loops |
| Structural | Active participant restructuring live interaction |
| Narrative | Collaborative conversational partner and re-authoring facilitator |
| Solution-Focused | Consultant eliciting goals, exceptions and competence |
| Psychodynamic/Object Relations | Reflective interpreter and container of enactment |
| Relational Psychoanalytic | Participatory observer of intersubjective enactment and recognition |
| Integrative | Hypothesis-driven coordinator across levels and models |

None of these stances is inherently neutral.

A therapist who acts as expert uses power differently from a therapist who acts as a curious collaborator. A "not-knowing" stance can itself conceal professional influence if presented as genuinely powerless. A relational psychoanalytic therapist is still the therapist. A Bowen coach remains influential. A narrative therapist selects questions. A behavioral therapist chooses which behavior becomes a target.

The appropriate issue is not whether therapy contains influence. It inevitably does. The issue is whether that influence is transparent, ethical, proportionate, evidence-informed, responsive to the couple's goals, and safe.

---

## 32. Relationship change versus relationship preservation

Outcome measurement often assumes:

> better relationship = staying together.

That assumption is too simple.

Possible positive outcomes include improved relationship satisfaction, reduced conflict, increased intimacy, repaired trust, safer co-parenting, clearer decision-making, consensual separation, reduced hostility after separation, protection from abuse, and individual recovery.

A couple can improve clinically and still separate. Conversely, a couple can remain together without becoming safer or more satisfying.

Therefore divorce or continuation is not a sufficient outcome measure by itself.

## 33. Final synthesis

The major couple therapies are best understood as **different maps of a genuinely multilevel system**.

They focus attention on different parts of the same broad territory:

- attachment theory asks whether the bond feels safe and responsive;
- emotion-focused theory asks what emotional process organizes self and interaction;
- Gottman research asks what couples repeatedly do and how affect and physiology unfold;
- cognitive-behavioral therapy asks how interpretations and actions reinforce distress;
- Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy asks how differences become polarized through futile change efforts;
- systemic therapy asks how recursive patterns sustain the problem;
- Bowen asks how chronic anxiety and multigenerational emotional process organize the couple;
- strategic therapy asks whether the attempted solution is maintaining the complaint;
- structural therapy asks how relational organization, boundaries and subsystems constrain functioning;
- narrative therapy asks what stories and discourses organize identity, power and possibility;
- solution-focused therapy asks what already works and how a preferred future can be built;
- object-relations therapy asks what internal relational patterns are being projected and reenacted;
- relational psychoanalysis asks whether two separate subjectivities can recognize each other without mutual negation;
- integrative approaches ask which of these mechanisms actually appears relevant in this case.

Three conclusions follow.

First, **theories should not be flattened into one generic couple therapy**. A vulnerable attachment enactment, unified detachment, cognitive restructuring, externalization, projective interpretation and solution-focused exception question are not interchangeable interventions merely because each might eventually produce empathy.

Second, **overlap is real**. Most successful couple therapies appear to create some combination of safety, attention, hope, emotional regulation, altered meaning, new behavior and new interpersonal experience. Common mechanisms matter without making specific theories redundant.

Third, **evidence belongs at the level of the claim being made**. There is broad evidence that couple therapy can help. There is strong direct outcome evidence for some model families and thinner evidence for others. There is meaningful process research for several proposed mechanisms. But outcome evidence should not be converted automatically into proof of theory, and clinical sophistication should not be converted automatically into efficacy.

The most defensible comparative stance is therefore neither "all therapies are the same" nor "one model explains couples." It is:

> Couple distress can be maintained by multiple interacting mechanisms. Different therapeutic models illuminate different mechanisms. The clinical task is to identify what is operating, preserve safety and accountability, use interventions that plausibly address those mechanisms, and revise the formulation when the couple's actual response contradicts the theory.

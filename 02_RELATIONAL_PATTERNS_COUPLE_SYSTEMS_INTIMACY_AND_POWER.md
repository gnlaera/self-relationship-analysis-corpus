# Relational Patterns, Couple Systems, Intimacy, and Power

## 1. Governing proposition

A relationship cannot be understood adequately as Person A plus Person B.

Person A has a psychology. Person B has a psychology. Each brings temperament, history, expectations, attachment-related strategies, values, vulnerabilities, goals, desires, standards, habits, and capacities for regulation. But once the two people repeatedly affect one another, something additional becomes analytically necessary: the relationship develops recurrent sequences, expectations, meanings, routines, roles, feedback loops, asymmetries, shared practices, and ways of organising closeness and distance.

Relationship science captures this point in its principle of **uniqueness**. Outcomes depend not only on each partner's qualities, but on what emerges when those qualities intersect. It also emphasises **interdependence**, **trajectory**, and the continual updating of relationship-specific perceptions through interaction.

This proposition does not require the claim that a relationship is literally a separate mind. It means that some relational phenomena cannot be predicted from either partner considered in isolation. A person who rarely pursues reassurance in other relationships may do so repeatedly with one particular partner. A person who is usually calm may become defensive in a particular recurring interaction. Two individually generous people may develop a chronically adversarial pattern around money. Two conflict-avoidant people may create a relationship in which major disagreements remain unspoken for years. Two people with considerable individual psychological insight may still reproduce the same interactional cycle.

The central question of this file is therefore:

> **What happens between partners, how does it become recurrent, what maintains or changes it, and which explanatory level is appropriate for understanding it?**

The answer requires several levels to be held simultaneously without being collapsed.

| Level | Core question | Examples | Epistemic caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual disposition | What does this person tend to bring across situations? | temperament, attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, negative affect, impulsivity | A disposition does not determine behaviour in every relationship |
| Relationship-specific perception | How does this person experience this partner and this relationship? | “You will not be there for me”; “I am not respected here”; perceived commitment | A perception is psychologically consequential without necessarily being objectively correct |
| Interaction behaviour | What did each person actually do? | criticise, reassure, leave, initiate sex, listen, interrupt, make tea, refuse a request | Behaviour should be described before motives are assigned |
| Dyadic cycle | How does A's action alter B, whose response then alters A? | pursue/withdraw, attack/defend, demand/withdraw, protest/disengage | Circularity describes influence, not equal blame |
| Relationship-level emergent pattern | What recurrent property now characterises this pair? | chronic mistrust, playfulness, mutual avoidance, reliable repair, rigid roles | An emergent pattern is not a mysterious third person |
| Context | What conditions constrain or amplify the interaction? | work stress, money, parenting, housing, culture, illness, social networks | Context must not be reduced to personality |
| Theory-specific unconscious formulation | How might a particular clinical theory interpret the pattern? | relational enactment, projective system, shared unconscious phantasy, psychoanalytic third | These are interpretive formulations, not equivalent to empirically established dyadic mechanisms |

A defensible formulation moves up these levels cautiously. It does not start at the bottom of the table and treat an unconscious interpretation as more sophisticated, and therefore more true, than ordinary observation.

## 2. What is a relationship?

A close relationship is not simply a positive feeling about another person. Relationship science has generally treated close relationships as sustained patterns of substantial interdependence across multiple domains of life. Partners repeatedly affect one another's outcomes, emotions, goals, behaviour, self-understanding, routines, and opportunities. Finkel, Simpson, and Eastwick organise relationship science around fourteen principles addressing four broad questions: what a relationship is, how it operates, what people bring to it, and how context shapes it.

Three points follow.

First, **relationship effects are relationally specific**. Knowing that A is anxious, conscientious, avoidant, extraverted, depressed, generous, conflict-averse, or highly sexual tells us something about A, but not everything about how A will function with B. B's dispositions matter, but so does the fit between them, the situations they encounter, their history together, and the behaviours they evoke from one another.

Second, **relationships become historical systems**. Yesterday's interaction changes the meaning of today's interaction. A delayed reply after ten years of dependable responsiveness may mean something different from the same delay after repeated deception. An attempt to take a break from conflict may be experienced as responsible self-regulation in a relationship with reliable return, but as abandonment in a relationship where withdrawal regularly ends discussion indefinitely.

Third, **relationships organise expectations**. Repeated experience creates predictions about what happens next. If protest is repeatedly followed by withdrawal, both partners may become sensitised to the first signs of the sequence. A mild complaint can then evoke a response appropriate to a much more severe conflict because each person is reacting not only to the present event but to the anticipated cycle.

A relationship is therefore simultaneously:

- a pattern of interdependence;
- an accumulation of shared history;
- a field of relationship-specific expectations;
- a set of ordinary practices;
- a system of reciprocal influence;
- a context in which aspects of self are expressed, constrained, amplified, or changed.

Relationship science, systemic theory, attachment theory, relational psychoanalysis, and couple psychoanalysis all recognise some version of this irreducibility. They do **not**, however, mean the same thing by it.

## 3. Four different meanings of “the relationship is more than the two people”

The phrase can conceal major theoretical differences.

| Framework | What “more than the two people” means | Main mechanism | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relationship science | Unique outcomes emerge from the intersection of partners and their interdependence | repeated interaction, partner effects, relationship-specific perception, shared goals, dependence | empirically grounded research framework |
| Systemic / interactional formulation | Behaviour is recursively organised through feedback | A affects B, B affects A, consequences reinforce or alter the next round | clinical and empirical systemic framework |
| Relational psychoanalysis | The couple co-creates patterns of subjectivity, recognition, enactment, and thirdness | mutual recognition/negation, dissociated self-states, enactment, co-created relational meanings | clinical-theoretical formulation |
| Tavistock couple psychoanalysis | The couple may organise a shared unconscious relationship with projective and phantasy dimensions | shared unconscious phantasy, projective identification, couple projective system, narcissistic relating | psychoanalytic clinical theory |

Ringstrom explicitly centres his model on self-experience in intimate relationship, mutual recognition versus mutual negation, and the idea that the relationship develops a “mind of its own.” Morgan's Tavistock model similarly develops the couple as a relationship with shared psychic properties, including shared unconscious phantasy, projective identification, narcissism, and couple development.

There is functional overlap. All four approaches can notice that a repeated A-B sequence develops properties that neither partner intended. All can notice that each person's behaviour becomes partly intelligible as a response to the other's. All can recognise that history changes the meaning of current events.

But the theories diverge sharply in what they claim explains those phenomena.

A systemic feedback loop does not require an unconscious shared phantasy. An empirically observed actor-partner interaction does not establish psychoanalytic thirdness. Relationship-specific attachment expectations are not identical to projective identification. A repeated pursue/withdraw pattern does not prove that the couple has unconsciously allocated unwanted parts of self between the partners.

This corpus therefore uses **functional comparison without theoretical equivalence**.

## 4. The fourteen relationship-science principles as the broad map

Finkel, Simpson, and Eastwick's fourteen principles provide the most useful general map before narrower clinical theories are introduced.

| Principle | Core idea | Practical relational question |
|---|---|---|
| Uniqueness | This pairing produces outcomes not reducible to either person alone | What happens specifically when A and B are together? |
| Integration | Interdependence can merge aspects of goals, identity, behaviour, and regulation | Where have “mine” and “yours” become partly “ours”? |
| Trajectory | Relationships change through continually updated experience | What has accumulated, and what is changing? |
| Evaluation | People continually evaluate partners and relationships | How does each person currently appraise the relationship? |
| Responsiveness | Responsive behaviour generally supports relationship quality | Does each feel understood, valued, and responded to? |
| Resolution | How partners handle consequential events matters | What happens when something goes wrong? |
| Maintenance | Committed partners engage in processes that preserve the relationship | What keeps the relationship going? |
| Predisposition | People bring traits and temperamental tendencies | What tendencies existed before this relationship? |
| Instrumentality | Relationships facilitate or obstruct important goals | Does the relationship help each person become able to pursue valued aims? |
| Standards | People compare relationships with expectations or ideals | What does each believe a relationship should provide? |
| Diagnosticity | Some situations reveal motives and priorities more clearly than others | Which moments genuinely test dependability, loyalty, care, or respect? |
| Alternatives | Other possible lives affect dependence and commitment | What viable alternatives does each person perceive? |
| Stress | External demands can degrade relationship functioning | What is entering the couple from outside it? |
| Culture | Networks, norms, institutions, and traditions shape intimate life | What broader scripts are organising expectations and options? |

These principles do not form a diagnostic checklist. They are a set of lenses. Different relationships may be organised around different combinations at different times.

They also prevent attachment theory from becoming totalising. A conflict may be centrally about attachment security. It may instead be about incompatible standards, goal obstruction, power, household labour, sexual preference, cultural expectations, attractive alternatives, financial stress, or a practical disagreement that does not require a deeper explanation.

## 5. Interdependence and dependence

Interdependence means that one partner's outcomes are contingent on what the other does. This becomes increasingly obvious as lives become structurally intertwined through housing, money, sex, caregiving, children, social networks, migration, work, health, and shared plans.

Interdependence has several dimensions.

### 5.1 Emotional interdependence

One person's emotional state affects the other. This can involve co-regulation, emotional contagion, reassurance, escalation, withdrawal, or mutual calming.

Emotional interdependence is not the same as emotional fusion. A relationship can involve strong mutual influence while each partner retains agency and psychological separateness.

### 5.2 Practical interdependence

Partners coordinate tasks, resources, labour, calendars, household management, parenting, and care. Practical interdependence is one reason seemingly small domestic actions acquire emotional significance. A missed task may be experienced not only as an unfinished task but as evidence about fairness, reliability, consideration, or status.

### 5.3 Goal interdependence

Partners can facilitate or obstruct one another's goals. One may absorb more household labour while the other completes professional training. One person's migration may enable the other's career but constrain their own. A partner may encourage health behaviour, provide childcare, or create conditions in which another goal becomes achievable.

Such coordination can expand both partners' capacities. It can also create resentment when contribution, benefit, sacrifice, and recognition are persistently asymmetric.

### 5.4 Dependence and power

Interdependence theory draws attention to dependence as a foundation of relational power. If A needs the relationship far more than B does, and B has attractive alternatives that A lacks, their bargaining positions differ.

This does not exhaust power. Money, housing ownership, immigration status, physical strength, social standing, access to children, knowledge, control of information, family support, and institutional privilege can all produce asymmetry.

A relationship can therefore be highly interdependent but profoundly unequal.

**Interdependence is a description of mutual consequence. It is not evidence of equal power.**

## 6. Relationship uniqueness

The uniqueness principle is one of the most important protections against personality reductionism.

Consider four hypothetical partners:

- A generally wants frequent emotional contact.
- B generally values considerable autonomy.
- C also values autonomy but responds warmly to direct requests.
- D wants frequent contact but responds to uncertainty with criticism.

A may become increasingly anxious with B but relatively secure with C. B may withdraw under A's escalating protest but remain engaged with a partner who communicates distress differently. C's responsiveness may evoke more directness from A. D and B may rapidly create a much harsher pursue/withdraw cycle than A and B.

The point is not that dispositions disappear. It is that dispositions are expressed **inside a relationship-specific ecology**.

This principle also explains why statements such as “I am just like this in relationships” can be partly true and partly misleading. Some patterns generalise strongly. Others vary by partner, stage of life, stress, history, and the partner's responses.

Mikulincer and Shaver's attachment framework is useful here because adult attachment representations can be considered at both more general and more relationship-specific levels. Their broader research synthesis covers attachment-related interpersonal regulation, couple functioning, caregiving, and sexuality rather than reducing attachment to a single personality label.

A useful question is therefore not only:

> What kind of person am I?

but also:

> What repeatedly becomes possible, difficult, threatening, or rewarding for me in this particular relationship?

## 7. Relationship trajectory

Relationships are historical, not static.

A trajectory develops as current interactions are interpreted through previous interactions, and today's response becomes part of tomorrow's history. Finkel and colleagues explicitly define trajectory in terms of continually updated perceptions of relationship-relevant interactions and experiences.

A trajectory can therefore change without a dramatic event.

Trust can grow through hundreds of mundane instances of reliability. Irritation can accumulate through years of unequal labour. Sexual closeness can slowly decline as parenting and fatigue crowd out couple time. Respect can increase because disagreements are consistently handled without humiliation. Avoidance can strengthen because difficult conversations repeatedly end in escalation. A partner can become easier to approach after enough experiences of being met rather than dismissed.

This makes the ordinary highly consequential.

Relationship trajectory should not be confused with a fixed developmental script. Not every relationship follows the same stages, and apparent similarity at one moment does not establish where a relationship will go.

Trajectory is better understood as:

> **history + current interaction + updating + changing context**

The same couple may also have several trajectories simultaneously. Sexual connection can improve while practical resentment worsens. Conflict frequency can fall because partners have learned to regulate better, or because they have disengaged. Commitment can remain high while satisfaction declines. Companionship can deepen while erotic attraction changes.

One variable cannot stand in for the whole relationship.

## 8. Relationship-specific evaluation and perception

People do not respond to relationships only as they objectively are. They respond to how they experience and evaluate them.

Relationship-specific perceptions include:

- “My partner is committed to me.”
- “My partner appreciates me.”
- “My partner is satisfied with us.”
- “I can depend on this person.”
- “I am respected here.”
- “Our sex life works for me.”
- “Conflict is manageable.”
- “My needs matter.”
- “My partner is already halfway out the door.”

These evaluations have real behavioural consequences even when their factual accuracy is uncertain.

Joel and colleagues provide an important empirical anchor. Across 43 longitudinal dyadic datasets, relationship-specific self-reports were more informative about current relationship quality than many individual-difference variables. Among the strongest relationship-specific predictors were perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, and conflict. Actor-reported variables were substantially more predictive than the partner's own reports of their experience.

This finding should not be misread.

It does **not** mean that the actor's perceptions are objectively accurate descriptions of the partner.

It means that how people experience their relationship is highly informative about how they evaluate its current quality.

The distinction is fundamental:

> “I experience you as uncommitted” is evidence about my relationship experience.

It is not automatically evidence that:

> “You are objectively uncommitted.”

Relationship-specific perception sits between individual psychology and observable interaction. It is shaped by both.

## 9. Responsiveness

Responsiveness is one of the strongest bridges between empirical relationship science, attachment theory, and several couple-therapy traditions.

At its simplest, responsiveness means behaving in a way that communicates that the other person's needs, concerns, values, or experience have registered and matter. Finkel and colleagues identify responsiveness as a core relationship principle because responsive behaviour tends to promote relationship quality for both partners.

Responsiveness is not the same as:

- obedience;
- agreement;
- immediate availability;
- solving every problem;
- surrendering boundaries;
- validating a false factual claim;
- never disappointing the partner.

A responsive answer can be:

> “I understand why this matters to you. I cannot agree to what you are asking, but I want us to work out another way.”

Responsiveness involves **recognising the legitimate existence of the other's subjective reality**, not automatically accepting its interpretation or demand.

A relationship can therefore fail at responsiveness in several ways:

1. the bid is not noticed;
2. the bid is noticed but dismissed;
3. the partner responds to a different need from the one being expressed;
4. the response is delayed beyond the point where it is experienced as meaningful;
5. the response is instrumental rather than emotionally engaged;
6. the need is acknowledged but chronically not acted on;
7. responsiveness is demanded one way but not reciprocated.

The quality of responsiveness is also relationship-specific. A gesture that feels deeply caring to one person may feel intrusive to another. Effective responsiveness therefore requires some understanding of the particular partner rather than generic “good relationship behaviour.”

## 10. Trust as an accumulated relational expectation

Trust is not adequately captured by the question “Do I trust my partner, yes or no?”

It contains several related expectations:

- Will this person tell me the truth?
- Will this person keep important agreements?
- Will this person take my interests into account when I am absent?
- Will this person respond when I am vulnerable?
- Will this person use sensitive information against me?
- Can disagreement occur without retaliation?
- Will this person return after conflict?
- Can I predict the boundaries of the relationship?
- Can I depend on care under stress?
- Will this person acknowledge harm rather than rewrite it?

Gottman's trust model treats trust as something constructed or eroded through repeated interactions, including small moments of responsiveness, negative-affect episodes, and conflict. His research and clinical framework places trust, betrayal, attunement, repair, and physiological arousal within one interactional model. The broader couple-therapy literature likewise treats these as relational rather than purely intrapsychic processes.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

### Trust as stored expectation

Repeated experience creates a relatively stable expectation of dependability.

### Trust as momentary action

A partner takes a risk based on that expectation.

### Trustworthiness

The other partner behaves in ways that warrant or violate trust.

### Felt trust

The subjective experience of safety or confidence.

These can diverge.

A highly anxious person may feel little trust despite considerable partner trustworthiness. Conversely, a person may trust a partner who is actively deceiving them. Felt trust therefore cannot be treated as perfect evidence of trustworthiness.

Nor should distrust automatically be psychologised as insecurity. Sometimes distrust is an accurate adaptation to repeated deception.

## 11. Bids, responses, and the microstructure of connection

Gottman's language of **bids** is useful because it brings relationship analysis down to the scale of everyday life.

A bid is an attempt to obtain some form of connection:

- attention;
- interest;
- humour;
- affection;
- practical help;
- sympathy;
- conversation;
- reassurance;
- play;
- sexual contact;
- shared excitement.

Responses can broadly move toward connection, ignore or miss it, or oppose it.

This lens matters because relationship life is not built only during formal conversations about the relationship. It develops while cooking, texting, sitting in the car, making plans, complaining about work, reaching for a hand, telling a joke, sharing news, initiating sex, asking for help, or silently showing distress.

A single missed bid usually proves little. People are distracted, tired, busy, preoccupied, or simply do not recognise what the other was asking for.

The pattern matters.

Repeated turning away can eventually become a relationship-specific expectation:

> “There is no point bringing this to you.”

Repeated hostile responses can create another:

> “Reaching for you makes things worse.”

Repeated responsiveness can create:

> “You may not always understand immediately, but you usually come back and try.”

This is one point at which Gottman's observational language, attachment language, and Gabb and Fink's sociology of ordinary relationship practices functionally overlap. All highlight repeated small interactions. They differ in theory and emphasis.

## 12. Attunement

Attunement is the attempt to understand and respond to another person's emotional experience without requiring that the two people become identical in perspective.

Useful components include:

- noticing emotion;
- turning toward rather than automatically dismissing it;
- tolerating the other's affect;
- listening without immediate counterattack;
- trying to understand before persuading;
- communicating that the experience makes sense from the partner's position;
- preserving awareness that understanding is not the same as factual agreement.

Attunement can fail because of limited attention, defensiveness, high arousal, unresolved injury, shame, incompatible meanings, mentalization failure, or simple lack of skill.

It can also fail because the request for attunement is embedded in attack.

A partner may find it very difficult to process:

> “You are selfish and obviously do not care about me.”

even if the underlying experience is:

> “I felt alone when you did not respond.”

This is why couple processes cannot always be divided cleanly into one person expressing a need and another failing to respond. The form in which the need is expressed changes the receiving conditions.

At the same time, this fact must never be used to erase accountability for a clearly wrongful act. A betrayed partner's angry delivery does not make the betrayal jointly caused.

## 13. Repair attempts

Conflict is unavoidable in close interdependence. The ability to repair therefore matters at least as much as the unrealistic goal of avoiding all rupture.

Repair attempts include:

- clarifying a misunderstanding;
- softening tone;
- taking responsibility;
- acknowledging impact;
- apologising;
- using affection;
- using non-derisive humour;
- asking for a pause;
- returning reliably after a pause;
- restating the other's position fairly;
- acknowledging a valid point;
- signalling continuing goodwill;
- changing behaviour.

A repair attempt is not defined only by the speaker's intention.

It also has to be received.

A joke may de-escalate one conflict and feel contemptuous in another. “Sorry” may repair a minor mistake but be inadequate after repeated deception. A request for a break may regulate arousal when the partner reliably returns, but reproduce abandonment when “taking space” repeatedly means avoiding the issue indefinitely.

The relationship's existing trust and friendship therefore alter whether the same behaviour functions as repair.

This is an important systems point:

> **The function of an action depends partly on the relational context into which it enters.**

## 14. Conflict is not one phenomenon

Conflict can involve very different relational problems.

It may concern:

- a solvable practical issue;
- a persistent difference in preference;
- a threat to attachment security;
- perceived disrespect;
- unequal labour;
- money;
- sexual desire;
- jealousy;
- parenting;
- extended family;
- career priorities;
- cultural expectations;
- boundaries;
- deception;
- incompatible life goals;
- coercion or abuse.

These categories should not be treated as interchangeable.

A couple may argue repeatedly about loading the dishwasher because it has become a symbol of fairness. Another couple may simply disagree about standards of tidiness. Another may be caught in a broader pattern where one partner expects domestic service from the other. The observable topic is similar. The relational meaning is not.

The first analytic task is therefore not to decide which theory explains the conflict. It is to determine **what kind of conflict is actually occurring**.

## 15. Physiological arousal and flooding

Conflict is partly embodied.

Gottman's programme gives substantial attention to diffuse physiological arousal during conflict. High arousal can narrow attention, increase defensive processing, reduce access to humour and empathy, and make flexible problem-solving harder.

The concept of **flooding** is useful when it remains descriptive rather than moralising.

Flooding may help explain why someone:

- stops processing new information;
- repeats the same argument;
- becomes rigid;
- wants to flee;
- becomes defensive;
- struggles to mentalize;
- cannot access affection that is available at calmer times.

Flooding does not establish the cause of the conflict.

It also does not excuse aggression.

“I was flooded” may explain why reflective capacity declined. It does not turn intimidation, threats, physical aggression, sexual coercion, or destruction of property into neutral symptoms of a mutual cycle.

A useful regulation intervention in ordinary conflict is a structured pause followed by reliable return. The return matters as much as the pause. Without it, “self-regulation” can become indistinguishable from avoidance or punishment.

## 16. Recurring cycles

A dyadic cycle exists when the behaviour of each partner becomes part of the conditions producing the other's next behaviour.

The basic structure is:

> A acts → B interprets → B responds → A interprets B's response → A responds again

Over time, anticipation enters the sequence:

> A expects B to withdraw → A protests earlier and harder → B experiences the protest as confirmation that engagement is dangerous → B withdraws sooner → A experiences the withdrawal as confirmation that B cannot be relied upon.

Neither partner needs to consciously intend the cycle.

Once established, the cycle can become faster, more automatic, and less dependent on the original trigger.

But the language of “cycle” should remain a description of **recursive influence**, not a claim that both partners are equally responsible for every component.

## 17. Pursue/withdraw

Pursue/withdraw is one common cycle, but it should not become a universal template.

The pursuer may:

- ask repeatedly;
- intensify;
- complain;
- demand reassurance;
- follow the partner;
- criticise;
- insist on immediate discussion;
- monitor;
- protest distance.

The withdrawer may:

- become quiet;
- leave;
- delay;
- minimise;
- detach;
- intellectualise;
- avoid eye contact;
- shut down;
- refuse engagement.

Attachment theory provides one plausible formulation. A more anxious strategy can involve heightened monitoring of availability and intensified efforts to restore contact, while a more avoidant strategy can involve deactivation, distance, and self-reliance.

But the behaviour does not identify the mechanism automatically.

Pursuit can also arise from:

- a legitimate unresolved issue;
- unequal labour;
- deception;
- poor conflict boundaries;
- anger;
- a partner repeatedly breaking agreements;
- a preference for immediate problem-solving.

Withdrawal can also arise from:

- flooding;
- fear of escalation;
- exhaustion;
- low relationship investment;
- strategic punishment;
- need for processing time;
- depression;
- a safety response to aggression;
- ordinary preference for temporary solitude.

The behaviour is observable. The attachment explanation is a formulation.

---

## 18. Demand/withdraw

Demand/withdraw overlaps with pursue/withdraw but should not be treated as identical.

“Demand” is primarily a behavioural description. It often concerns pressure for change.

“Pursuit” is broader and may include attempts to restore emotional connection.

A partner can demand that the other do more housework without being especially attachment-anxious. A partner can pursue reassurance without demanding any behavioural change beyond emotional contact.

Similarly, withdrawal from a demand can reflect disagreement, power, overwhelm, avoidance, or low motivation.

A demand/withdraw sequence may also be structurally asymmetric. If one partner carries most of the domestic labour, repeated demands for participation may partly reflect the unequal distribution of work rather than a symmetrical communication failure.

A cycle formulation should therefore ask:

> **What is being demanded, why, under what conditions, and what happens to the underlying issue when the interaction stops?**

---

## 19. Attack/defend

Attack/defend often accelerates rapidly because each partner experiences the other's behaviour as evidence that self-protection is required.

A may accuse.

B defends.

A experiences defence as refusal of accountability.

A intensifies.

B experiences intensification as proof of unfair attack.

B counterattacks or withdraws.

The conversation moves away from the original issue into a dispute over:

- who started it;
- whose memory is correct;
- who is more unreasonable;
- whose emotional response is justified;
- who is the “real problem.”

The immediate cycle can be genuinely reciprocal.

The original issue may not be.

If A discovered B's deception, the subsequent attack/defend loop does not transform deception into a jointly caused event. B's defensiveness may amplify A's anger. A's anger may amplify B's defensiveness. That is circular causation around the **conflict process**. It says nothing about equal responsibility for the **precipitating act**.

---

## 20. Protest and disengagement

Attachment-oriented models often understand certain angry or demanding behaviours as protest against perceived disconnection. This can be clinically illuminating when used cautiously.

Protest can communicate:

- “Do I matter?”
- “Will you come back?”
- “Why are you not responding?”
- “I need evidence that this relationship is still safe.”

Disengagement may communicate:

- “I cannot manage this intensity.”
- “Nothing I say helps.”
- “I need distance to regulate.”
- “I have stopped expecting repair.”

But these are possible functions, not automatic translations.

Calling aggressive behaviour “attachment protest” can become dangerous if it sanitises coercion. Following a partner who has asked for physical space, blocking a doorway, threatening self-harm to prevent separation, monitoring devices, or forcing sexual contact are not made benign by locating fear of abandonment underneath them.

Similarly, disengagement should not automatically be pathologised. Leaving an escalating or unsafe interaction can be adaptive.

The function and the behaviour both matter.

## 21. A compact cycle taxonomy

| Observable cycle | Typical surface pattern | Possible underlying mechanisms | Important alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pursue / withdraw | one intensifies contact, the other distances | attachment hyperactivation/deactivation; flooding | legitimate unresolved issue; low investment; need for time |
| Demand / withdraw | one presses for change, the other avoids | power struggle; conflict avoidance; overload | unequal labour; incompatible standards |
| Attack / defend | accusation followed by justification or counterattack | shame; threat; identity protection | actual wrongdoing requiring accountability |
| Protest / disengage | escalating signals followed by emotional retreat | attachment threat; hopelessness | coercion; punishment; safety response |
| Dominate / submit | one defines action or reality, the other yields | identity threat; power asymmetry; learned role | coercive control; material dependence |
| Overfunction / underfunction | one takes increasing responsibility, the other less | anxiety, role reinforcement, competence asymmetry | disability, workload, unequal expectations |
| Criticise / stonewall | global criticism followed by shutdown | contempt; flooding; avoidance | accumulated unaddressed grievances |
| Rescue / resent | one repeatedly fixes, then resents dependence | caregiving identity; difficulty setting boundaries | actual unequal capacity or temporary crisis |
| Test / reassure | repeated tests of commitment followed by reassurance | mistrust; attachment anxiety | actual inconsistency or deception |
| Withdraw / pursue | distance itself triggers pursuit | regulation mismatch | partner's repeated refusal to address issues |

The table should generate questions, not verdicts.

## 22. Attachment inside the dyad

Attachment matters because romantic partners frequently become sources of safety, comfort, and emotional regulation.

But this file uses attachment only where it contributes to understanding **between-partner processes**. Individual attachment architecture belongs primarily at the individual level.

Three dyadic implications are especially important.

### 22.1 Threat changes information processing

When attachment security feels uncertain, perception can become organised around cues of availability, rejection, distance, or abandonment.

A more hyperactivating strategy may intensify attention to these cues.

A more deactivating strategy may suppress or distance from them.

This can create different interpretations of the same interaction.

### 22.2 Strategies can become complementary

One person's intensification can increase the other's withdrawal. The withdrawal then amplifies the first person's alarm.

This is one route by which individual regulatory strategies become a relationship-level pattern.

### 22.3 Attachment is partly relationship-specific

A person may experience greater security with one partner than another. Repeated responsiveness or unreliability can update expectations.

This protects attachment theory from becoming a fixed typology.

A person is not simply “an avoidant” or “an anxious.” Anxiety and avoidance are dimensions, and their expression can vary with relationship and context.

---

## 23. Caregiving

Attachment relationships involve not only seeking care but providing it.

Caregiving includes:

- comforting;
- protecting;
- helping;
- responding to illness;
- practical support;
- emotional availability;
- facilitating rest;
- taking over tasks during overload;
- encouraging exploration and autonomy.

Healthy caregiving is not identical to rescuing.

It should ideally remain sensitive to what the recipient actually needs and preserve the recipient's agency.

Caregiving can become problematic when:

- one partner is permanently cast as fragile;
- care becomes controlling;
- assistance prevents autonomy;
- one partner's needs dominate all available resources;
- caregiving labour is taken for granted;
- the caregiver cannot express their own needs;
- care is used to create debt;
- refusal of unwanted help is treated as rejection.

The caregiving system therefore intersects with power and household labour.

A relationship can be deeply caring while still being unequal.

---

## 24. Emotion regulation is interpersonal and intrapersonal

Greenberg and Goldman's emotion-focused couple model explicitly broadens couple motivation beyond attachment alone. It gives central roles to attachment, identity validation, and attraction, and treats couple relationships as important sites of emotion regulation.

This produces an important relational distinction.

Partners regulate emotion through:

### Other-regulation

- reassurance;
- comfort;
- validation;
- touch;
- responsiveness;
- soothing;
- practical help.

### Self-regulation

- tolerating affect;
- pausing;
- reflecting;
- self-soothing;
- maintaining perspective;
- not requiring the partner to immediately eliminate every painful state.

Couple conflict can become more rigid when either form fails.

A person who cannot self-regulate may demand impossible levels of external regulation.

A person who refuses all co-regulation may turn legitimate interdependence into compulsory self-sufficiency.

A functional relationship usually needs both:

> **I can regulate myself enough not to make you responsible for every feeling, and I can also depend on you enough to allow your care to matter.**

Greenberg and Goldman explicitly stress both self-soothing and other-soothing rather than locating all regulation in the partner.

---

## 25. Identity validation

Partners do more than provide attachment security. They also become important witnesses to identity.

A partner communicates, explicitly and implicitly:

- who they think I am;
- whether my competence is respected;
- whether my values matter;
- whether my work counts;
- whether my experience is believable;
- whether my autonomy is legitimate;
- whether my achievements are celebrated;
- whether my vulnerabilities diminish me in their eyes.

Greenberg and Goldman argue that threats to identity can create influence and dominance cycles organised around shame, powerlessness, loss of control, invalidation, and anger.

This helps explain conflicts that do not primarily concern closeness.

A partner may not be asking:

> “Will you stay?”

They may be asking:

> “Do you respect me?”

or:

> “Do I get to define my own experience?”

or:

> “Do my judgment and competence count here?”

Attachment and identity can interact, but they are not interchangeable.

---

## 26. Power

Power is not one variable.

A useful relationship formulation distinguishes several forms.

### 26.1 Dependence power

Who has greater need for the relationship, fewer alternatives, or higher costs of leaving?

### 26.2 Decision power

Whose preferences more often determine what happens?

### 26.3 Resource power

Who controls:

- money;
- housing;
- transport;
- information;
- social access;
- employment opportunities;
- immigration or legal resources?

### 26.4 Emotional power

Whose distress sets the agenda?

Whose anger ends the conversation?

Whose withdrawal forces accommodation?

### 26.5 Epistemic power

Who gets to define what “really happened”?

Whose interpretation is treated as authoritative?

Whose memory is routinely dismissed?

### 26.6 Labour power

Who performs:

- domestic work;
- emotional labour;
- childcare;
- planning;
- social coordination;
- repair initiation;
- sexual accommodation?

### 26.7 Sexual power

Who controls the conditions under which sexual contact occurs?

Can either partner say no without punishment?

Is consent active and revocable?

### 26.8 Coercive power

Does one partner obtain compliance through:

- intimidation;
- threats;
- surveillance;
- isolation;
- physical force;
- financial restriction;
- sexual pressure;
- fear of retaliation?

These forms cannot be collapsed into “the couple cycle.”

Power is partly interactional and partly structural.

---

## 27. Influence is not domination

Influence is inevitable in intimate relationships.

Partners alter each other's:

- plans;
- priorities;
- habits;
- beliefs;
- moods;
- goals;
- social worlds;
- use of time.

The question is not whether influence exists. It is whether influence can flow in both directions without one person losing legitimate agency.

Gottman's work gives particular importance to accepting influence. In practical terms, this involves being able to register that the partner has a legitimate point and allowing that information to change one's position.

Accepting influence is not compliance.

A relationship in which neither partner can be influenced becomes a contest between rigid positions.

A relationship in which only one partner is expected to be influenced becomes hierarchical.

A more useful test is:

> **Can I remain myself while allowing your experience to alter me?**

That question connects empirical interaction research with the relational psychoanalytic concept of recognition, while keeping the theories distinct.

---

## 28. Mutual recognition and mutual negation

Ringstrom places **mutual recognition** at the centre of intimate development.

Recognition involves relating to the partner as another subject with an experience that is neither identical to one's own nor reducible to a role in one's own psychological story.

Negation occurs when the other person's subjectivity is effectively erased.

Examples include:

- “If you loved me, you would feel exactly as I do.”
- “Your perception cannot be real because it contradicts mine.”
- “Your boundary exists only because you are damaged.”
- “Your anger proves you are irrational.”
- “Your need for autonomy means you do not love me.”
- “My pain means your intention is irrelevant.”
- “Your intention means my pain is irrelevant.”

Mutual recognition is difficult because intimate partners matter greatly to one another. Difference therefore has consequences.

Recognition should not be romanticised into permanent harmony. Ringstrom's relational model understands recognition and rupture as dynamic. The goal is not perfect subject-to-subject relating at every moment. It is greater capacity to recover recognition after it is lost.

This has functional overlap with mentalization and repair, but it remains a relational psychoanalytic formulation rather than an empirical synonym for them.

---

## 29. Tolerance of difference

Intimacy requires closeness.

It also requires difference.

The relationship becomes psychologically constricting when closeness is possible only if one partner:

- agrees;
- wants the same things;
- feels the same way;
- adopts the same interpretation;
- suppresses autonomy;
- abandons friendships;
- relinquishes private thought;
- accepts the other's preferred identity for them.

Morgan's Tavistock account gives particular attention to whether partners can tolerate the **otherness** of the other. Her broader model includes narcissism, sharing psychic space, projective systems, and the capacity to occupy a third position from which the couple can reflect on the relationship.

Difference can be threatening because it limits omnipotent fantasies about what the partner should be.

Mature tolerance of difference does not mean indifference to incompatibility. Some differences genuinely matter.

The relevant question is:

> Can this difference be lived with without one partner having to erase themselves?

---

## 30. Narcissism and otherness as a theory-specific lens

“Narcissism” is often used casually as an insult. It should not be used that way here.

Within Morgan's psychoanalytic framework, narcissistic relational functioning concerns difficulty making psychic room for the partner as a separate other.

One possible manifestation is pressure toward sameness:

> “You should see it the way I see it.”

Another is domination:

> “There is room for one legitimate mind here.”

Another is fusion:

> “Difference itself feels like a threat to the relationship.”

Another is polarisation:

> one partner occupies a position that the other cannot tolerate in themselves.

These are psychoanalytic formulations.

They should not be converted into remote personality diagnosis.

A couple may struggle to tolerate difference because of ordinary conflict style, social norms, rigid beliefs, fear, dependency, power imbalance, or specific unresolved grievances. The narcissistic formulation is one interpretive possibility, not the default explanation.

---

## 31. Attraction is not reducible to attachment

Greenberg and Goldman identify **attraction** alongside attachment and identity as a major relational motivation. Their model links attraction with interest, excitement, enjoyment, novelty, and positive emotion.

This distinction matters.

A relationship can be:

- secure but low in attraction;
- highly attractive but insecure;
- emotionally supportive but sexually unsatisfying;
- erotically strong but poor at caregiving;
- deeply companionate but low in novelty;
- conflictual yet strongly attractive.

Attraction can involve:

- physical desire;
- curiosity;
- admiration;
- play;
- novelty;
- eroticism;
- intellectual interest;
- humour;
- vitality;
- being surprised by the partner.

Attachment security does not automatically generate erotic desire.

Nor does reduced desire automatically prove lack of love or attachment.

---

## 32. Sexuality

Sex is simultaneously bodily, relational, cultural, emotional, and practical.

The Cambridge Handbook treats sexuality as a distinct close-relationship process alongside conflict, intimacy, commitment, social networks, culture, and maintenance.

A relationship formulation should distinguish at least:

- sexual desire;
- attraction;
- arousal;
- affection;
- frequency;
- initiation;
- rejection;
- pleasure;
- sexual scripts;
- consent;
- meaning;
- exclusivity agreements;
- sexual identity;
- health;
- body image;
- age;
- parenting;
- privacy;
- fatigue;
- medication or illness;
- unresolved injury.

Sex should not be used as a simple relationship barometer.

High frequency does not prove relational health.

Low frequency does not prove relational failure.

Desire discrepancy is common and may be managed in many ways.

The central questions are more specific:

- Can desire and non-desire be discussed without humiliation?
- Can both partners say yes or no freely?
- Are sexual boundaries clear?
- Does one person's desire automatically become the other's obligation?
- Are differences negotiated or coerced?
- Is sex a site of mutual pleasure, reassurance, performance, avoidance, pressure, repair, conflict, or several of these?

Sexual coercion is not a dyadic “mismatch.” It is an accountability and safety issue.

---

## 33. Gabb and Fink: ordinary intimacy

A corpus focused on couple dynamics can become badly distorted if it looks only at conflict, pathology, attachment alarm, trauma, and unconscious enactment.

Gabb and Fink provide a major corrective.

Their study of long-term couple relationships directs attention toward **relationship work** carried by ordinary practices: thoughtful gestures, household chores, childcare, cooking, couple time, home comforts, talking and listening, humour, banter, sex, intimacy, parenting, friendships, and cultural context. Their chapter architecture itself makes the ordinary central rather than peripheral.

This matters theoretically.

A relationship is not sustained only by successfully surviving crises.

It is reproduced every day.

---

## 34. Gifts and thoughtful gestures

The value of a caring act is often not proportional to its financial size.

A routine gesture can communicate:

- “I thought of you.”
- “I know what makes your day easier.”
- “Your comfort matters.”
- “I noticed what you are carrying.”
- “I remember what you like.”

A cup of tea, preparing breakfast, buying a familiar food, sending a message before a difficult meeting, filling the car, or doing a disliked task can carry relationship-specific meaning because the gesture reflects accumulated knowledge of the partner.

This is one way **deep knowing** becomes behavioural.

The same act can also lose meaning if it becomes obligatory or is embedded in grossly unequal labour.

A thoughtful gesture is therefore not an isolated unit of goodness. Its meaning depends on pattern, expectation, reciprocity, and context.

---

## 35. Household work and practical labour

Household labour is relationally important precisely because it is mundane.

It communicates, often without explicit discussion:

- whose time is protected;
- whose fatigue counts;
- who notices what needs doing;
- who is expected to remember;
- who can opt out;
- who is thanked;
- whose contribution is invisible;
- who carries responsibility when plans fail.

Gabb and Fink treat household chores, childcare, cooking, and practical work as part of relationship maintenance rather than mere background.

This is also a power issue.

If one partner repeatedly carries more unpaid work, describing the resulting demand/withdraw cycle without naming the material asymmetry is analytically incomplete.

A cycle formulation might correctly observe:

> A criticises → B defends → A intensifies → B withdraws.

But the formulation must also ask:

> Who is doing the work?

A symmetrical interactional description cannot substitute for an audit of unequal burdens.

---

## 36. Cooking, practical care, and embodied recognition

Food frequently sits at the intersection of care, labour, memory, culture, and pleasure.

Cooking may function as:

- service;
- creativity;
- intimacy;
- tradition;
- obligation;
- gendered labour;
- celebration;
- conflict;
- caretaking.

The same task can therefore carry contradictory meanings.

A partner who cooks nightly may experience the activity as an expression of love.

They may also resent that the other assumes it will happen.

Both can be true.

This is why ordinary relationship practices should be examined not only by asking **what happened**, but also:

- Who usually does it?
- How is it experienced?
- Is it recognised?
- Is it chosen?
- Is it reciprocated?
- What happens if it stops?

---

## 37. Time together

Couple time is not simply the number of hours two people occupy the same space.

Time together can be:

- attentive;
- distracted;
- companionable;
- conflictual;
- erotic;
- practical;
- restorative;
- obligatory.

External demands strongly affect access to couple time.

Parenting, shift work, commuting, elder care, financial pressure, illness, and digital intrusion can reduce the amount of unstructured attention available to the relationship.

A deficit of couple time can therefore be a **resource problem**, not evidence of insufficient love.

Conversely, extensive proximity does not guarantee intimacy. Partners can spend nearly all day together while rarely feeling emotionally met.

Useful questions include:

- Is there protected time for connection?
- Is that time experienced as obligation or pleasure?
- Does each partner have enough separate time?
- Is one person responsible for arranging all shared time?
- Are rituals of connection still alive?
- What happens when ordinary time becomes scarce?

---

## 38. Home comforts and ordinary co-presence

Gabb and Fink show that being comfortable in one another's presence can itself be a meaningful relational achievement.

Long-term intimacy often includes low-drama forms of connection:

- sitting together;
- listening to music;
- watching television;
- touching in passing;
- sharing routines;
- chatting while one person gets ready;
- doing separate activities in the same room;
- spontaneously becoming playful.

These practices can seem trivial compared with dramatic therapeutic material.

They are not trivial.

They help construct the felt background of the relationship.

A relationship in which most mundane co-presence feels safe, interested, affectionate, and flexible has a different emotional ecology from one in which ordinary co-presence is dominated by vigilance, criticism, avoidance, or loneliness.

---

## 39. Humour, laughter, and banter

Humour is relationally ambiguous.

It can:

- reduce tension;
- create shared pleasure;
- signal familiarity;
- help partners tolerate awkwardness;
- maintain erotic play;
- transform frustration;
- reinforce a couple identity.

Gabb and Fink treat laughter, humour, and banter as significant features of ordinary couple communication.

But humour can also:

- ridicule;
- disguise hostility;
- invalidate pain;
- permit contempt without accountability;
- reinforce a power hierarchy.

The question is therefore not:

> “Do they joke?”

It is:

> “What does the humour do between them?”

A joke that both experience as affectionate play is different from a joke in which one partner repeatedly becomes the object of derision.

Humour's function is relational.

---

## 40. Routine acts of care

Routine care is often more informative than exceptional romance.

Repeated ordinary acts create evidence about:

- reliability;
- consideration;
- attention;
- generosity;
- reciprocity;
- knowledge of the partner.

This is one place where Gabb and Fink and Gottman converge functionally.

Gabb and Fink show how everyday practices sustain long-term intimate life. Gottman emphasises cumulative responses to bids and ordinary moments of emotional availability. These are different research and theoretical traditions, but both point toward the significance of **small repeated events**.

The larger implication is:

> Relationship quality is often built in increments too small to feel psychologically dramatic while they are happening.

---

## 41. Ordinary intimacy and sexuality

Gabb and Fink's treatment of sexuality explicitly resists a narrow equation of intimacy with intercourse. Their book addresses the significance and insignificance of sex, parenting, making space for intimacy, ageing bodies, and relationship duration.

Intimacy can be sexual without being emotionally close.

It can be emotionally close without being sexual.

It can involve:

- touch;
- disclosure;
- shared humour;
- private rituals;
- caretaking;
- companionship;
- erotic knowledge;
- comfortable silence;
- mutual vulnerability;
- being known over time.

Long-term sexuality also changes with bodies and context.

A static standard for what a “healthy” sexual relationship must look like can therefore generate unnecessary pathology.

The better question is:

> Does the couple have a sexual and intimate arrangement that both can inhabit with agency, honesty, consent, and sufficient satisfaction?

---

## 42. The bias toward crises

Clinical material naturally overrepresents what goes wrong.

Couples seek therapy because:

- conflict escalates;
- sex has become difficult;
- trust has been damaged;
- one partner is withdrawing;
- affairs occur;
- communication fails;
- separation is being considered.

This can produce a distorted theory of relationships in which the most important events appear to be ruptures.

But a relationship that lasts for twenty years contains thousands of ordinary interactions for every major crisis.

This matters for assessment.

A couple may present with one devastating betrayal while having had years of strong ordinary intimacy.

Another couple may have no dramatic rupture but experience chronic emotional neglect.

A third may rarely argue because both partners avoid anything difficult.

A fourth may argue often but repair rapidly and remain affectionate.

The absence or presence of dramatic events does not by itself define relational quality.

---

## 43. Love as practice, not only state

Gabb and Fink's work supports a view of love as something people **do** as well as something they feel.

This does not reduce love to domestic labour.

It means that subjective love becomes relationally consequential through action.

A person may feel profound love while behaving unreliably.

Another may be less verbally expressive while communicating care through repeated practical responsiveness.

For relationship analysis, therefore, three questions should remain separate:

1. What does the person say they feel?
2. What do they repeatedly do?
3. How is their behaviour experienced by the partner?

The first cannot substitute for the other two.

---

## 44. Maintenance

Relationship maintenance refers to processes that help committed relationships persist.

These can include:

- attending to the partner;
- protecting couple time;
- forgiving ordinary imperfections;
- supporting goals;
- maintaining affection;
- preserving rituals;
- responding to bids;
- repairing conflict;
- construing the relationship positively;
- reducing attention to alternatives;
- maintaining shared projects.

Maintenance is not inherently virtuous.

People can maintain relationships that are harmful.

Biases that protect commitment can preserve a good relationship, but they can also delay recognition of serious problems.

The correct question is not simply:

> “What keeps them together?”

It is also:

> “What exactly is being maintained, at what cost, and with what consequences for each partner?”

---

## 45. Goals and instrumentality

People enter relationships with goals.

Some are explicit:

- raise children;
- build financial security;
- travel;
- create a home;
- pursue careers;
- share companionship;
- have a sexual relationship.

Others are less explicit:

- feel valued;
- become more adventurous;
- feel safe;
- have someone witness one's life;
- escape loneliness;
- gain social legitimacy;
- develop a family identity.

A relationship can support one goal and obstruct another.

A high-achieving couple may facilitate career goals while sacrificing time together.

A strong caregiving arrangement may support health but produce caregiver exhaustion.

A partnership may provide security while constraining exploration.

Instrumentality is therefore not about using the partner as an instrument. It concerns whether the relational system facilitates or frustrates important goals.

This becomes especially consequential when the partners' goals diverge.

---

## 46. Standards

Standards are the benchmarks against which a relationship is judged.

They may concern:

- fidelity;
- communication;
- emotional closeness;
- sex;
- money;
- parenting;
- domestic labour;
- independence;
- time together;
- extended family;
- career sacrifice;
- conflict behaviour;
- privacy.

Standards are partly personal and partly cultural.

Distress can arise because:

1. the relationship falls below a shared standard;
2. the partners have different standards;
3. a standard is unrealistic;
4. the standard changed but the couple did not renegotiate it;
5. one partner assumes a standard is obvious and shared;
6. a cultural norm is treated as personal necessity.

A standards conflict is not automatically psychopathology.

It may be a negotiation problem.

It may also reveal genuine incompatibility.

---

## 47. Diagnostic situations

The relationship-science term **diagnostic situation** does not mean psychiatric diagnosis.

It refers to situations in which behaviour provides unusually informative evidence about a partner's motives or priorities.

Examples might include:

- illness;
- bereavement;
- a major career decision;
- temptation by an attractive alternative;
- financial crisis;
- a partner's vulnerable disclosure;
- conflict between partner and family of origin;
- a moment requiring sacrifice;
- a boundary test.

These situations matter because cheap signals and consequential behaviour are not equally informative.

Saying “I support your career” is one form of evidence.

Taking on additional responsibilities during a decisive professional period is another.

But diagnosticity remains probabilistic.

One failed response under extreme stress should not be allowed to erase an entire relationship history without further context.

---

## 48. Alternatives

Relationship alternatives include more than another romantic person.

They can include:

- being single;
- a different partner;
- different relationship structures;
- greater autonomy;
- returning to family;
- a different city or career;
- an imagined better relationship.

Perceived alternatives affect dependence and commitment.

But alternatives should not be interpreted narrowly through monogamous assumptions. Consensually non-monogamous relationships organise exclusivity and alternatives differently.

The important variable is not simply the existence of other relationships.

It is how the current relationship's agreements define commitment, substitution, exclusivity, and betrayal.

An outside sexual relationship may be a clear betrayal in one couple and fully consistent with agreed boundaries in another.

---

## 49. External stress and resource depletion

Many couple problems are partly imported from outside the relationship.

Finkel and colleagues identify external stress as a core principle because demands can worsen outcomes, particularly when they exceed the couple's coping resources. Their broader synthesis also emphasises culture and social networks as part of the relational environment.

Stress affects the relationship through several channels.

### 49.1 Less time

High demands reduce opportunities for:

- conversation;
- sex;
- recreation;
- shared meals;
- repair;
- sleep;
- affectionate contact.

### 49.2 Reduced regulation

Exhausted people have fewer resources for patience, inhibition, perspective-taking, and flexible response.

### 49.3 Increased practical stakes

Financial stress turns small decisions into consequential decisions.

Parenting stress means mistakes affect not only the couple but children.

Illness may make ordinary fairness impossible for a period.

### 49.4 Spillover

Anger, frustration, and helplessness generated elsewhere can enter the couple.

### 49.5 Constricted alternatives

Economic or caregiving constraints can make it harder to change arrangements even when both partners know they are failing.

External stress should therefore be examined before concluding that deteriorating interaction proves deteriorating love.

---

## 50. Stress can expose, create, or amplify problems

Stress can play at least three different roles.

### Stress exposes a latent problem

The couple had a weak conflict process that becomes visible only under heavy load.

### Stress creates a new problem

A previously workable relationship is overwhelmed by circumstances neither partner faced before.

### Stress amplifies an existing problem

A moderate difference in standards becomes intolerable when sleep, time, and money disappear.

These should not be confused.

Nor should resilience be romanticised. Couples should not need adversity to prove the strength of their bond.

Material resources matter.

Psychological formulations that ignore money, work, housing, health, caregiving, and time risk blaming couples for conditions they cannot regulate through insight alone.

---

## 51. Culture and social networks

Couples are never socially isolated dyads.

Relationships exist inside:

- families;
- friendship networks;
- religious communities;
- workplaces;
- legal systems;
- gender norms;
- sexual norms;
- economic conditions;
- cultural expectations.

Culture helps define:

- what counts as a couple;
- who may marry;
- how much autonomy is expected;
- who should earn;
- who should care;
- whether sex is discussed;
- whether separation is acceptable;
- whether family elders are involved;
- what fidelity means;
- what roles men and women should occupy;
- how same-sex relationships are treated.

Networks can support a relationship or undermine it.

They can also offer resources when the couple becomes too closed in on itself.

A relationship system should therefore not be conceptualised as a sealed two-person unit.

---

## 52. Relationship-specific self-states

One of the more clinically useful relational ideas is that different relationships can evoke different configurations of self.

A person may become:

- playful with one partner;
- competent with another;
- ashamed with another;
- unusually dependent;
- unusually controlling;
- sexually alive;
- sexually inhibited;
- articulate;
- silent;
- confident;
- hypervigilant.

Several frameworks can describe this without being made equivalent.

### Relationship science

The uniqueness principle predicts relationship-specific effects arising from the interaction of two people.

### Attachment theory

Relationship-specific working models can alter expectations about availability and security.

### Relational psychoanalysis

Ringstrom emphasises the actualisation of different self-experiences and dissociated self-states inside intimate relationships.

### Mentalization

Stress in a particular relationship can alter the capacity to represent one's own and the partner's mind.

The functional overlap is clear.

The theories differ in explanatory architecture.

---

## 53. The relationship as a “third”

The word **third** is especially vulnerable to conceptual flattening.

At least four different ideas must remain separate.

| Concept | Meaning | What it does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| Dyadic emergence | A and B jointly produce a pattern irreducible to their individual main effects | It does not imply an unconscious relational entity |
| Systemic recursion | A's response becomes input for B, whose response becomes new input for A | It does not establish psychoanalytic thirdness |
| Ringstrom / relational thirdness | A co-created relational space can emerge when partners recognise difference and move beyond rigid dominance/submission | It is not a statistical interaction effect |
| Morgan's couple state of mind | A psychoanalytic capacity to think about the relationship from a third position while preserving both subjectivities | It is not identical to ordinary metacommunication |

Morgan describes the third position as the capacity to observe and reflect on the shared relationship while maintaining each partner's subjective position.

Functionally, all four concepts can support a useful move:

> from “you are the problem” to “what are we creating between us?”

But their theoretical claims are different.

A couple can learn to map a pursue/withdraw cycle without developing psychoanalytic thirdness.

A statistical dyadic interaction can exist without shared unconscious phantasy.

A couple may develop reflective metacommunication without accepting the Tavistock theory explaining why that capacity matters.

---

## 54. Relational thirdness in Ringstrom

Ringstrom's thirdness is closely tied to mutual recognition and the capacity to tolerate difference without collapsing into dominance and submission.

The relational problem is not simply that partners influence each other.

It is that intimate subjectivities can become locked into complementary positions:

- controller / controlled;
- accuser / accused;
- competent / incompetent;
- needy / self-sufficient;
- reasonable / irrational;
- pursuer / avoider.

When these positions rigidify, each partner's behaviour can help stabilise the other's role.

Relational thirdness offers a psychoanalytic account of moving beyond that complementarity. The partners become more capable of relating to the relationship itself, recognising that neither subjective position exhausts reality.

This is conceptually richer than circular feedback, but also more interpretive.

It should therefore remain labelled as relational psychoanalytic theory.

---

## 55. Morgan's couple state of mind

Morgan's “couple state of mind” offers another theory-specific version of relational reflectiveness.

The underlying move is from:

> “My partner is the problem.”

toward:

> “What is happening in the relationship we are creating together?”

The concept also implies a capacity to move between:

- one's own perspective;
- the partner's perspective;
- reflection on the couple as a relationship.

This can function as a useful clinical metaposition.

It should not be used to deny individual responsibility.

The question “What are we creating?” is valuable after ordinary reciprocal conflict.

After unilateral deception, another question remains necessary:

> “What did you do, and what responsibility do you have for it?”

Both questions may be relevant.

They are not substitutes for each other.

---

## 56. Shared unconscious phantasy

Morgan's Tavistock tradition uses **shared unconscious phantasy** to formulate how partners may jointly organise unconscious assumptions about the relationship.

Possible theoretical examples include an unconscious relationship organised around ideas such as:

- one must rescue and the other must remain helpless;
- one must know while the other must not know;
- one must desire while the other refuses;
- separation means destruction;
- disagreement means betrayal;
- intimacy means engulfment;
- one partner must carry anger for both.

These are theory-specific formulations.

They are not directly observed facts.

A recurring complementary pattern can support the hypothesis that such a formulation may be useful.

It does not prove that the phantasy exists as a hidden psychological object.

The formulation should earn its place by explaining observations better than simpler alternatives.

---

## 57. Couple projective systems

Morgan also develops the Tavistock concept of a **couple projective system**, in which each partner may locate disowned or difficult psychological material in the other and participate in an arrangement that stabilises complementary roles.

Again, this should remain theory-specific.

A practical sequence is:

1. observe the complementary roles;
2. establish that they recur;
3. examine what each partner actually does;
4. identify less interpretive explanations;
5. only then consider whether a projective-system formulation adds value.

For example:

> A is repeatedly cast as irresponsible while B becomes increasingly controlling.

Possible explanations include:

- A actually behaves irresponsibly;
- B has unusually rigid standards;
- B's control increases A's passivity;
- A's passivity increases B's control;
- both are under severe stress;
- a long-standing division of labour has become polarised;
- in a Tavistock formulation, the couple may be organising disowned dependency and control projectively.

The final possibility should not erase the earlier ones.

---

## 58. Shared unconscious formulation is not systemic recursion

The distinction deserves explicit treatment.

Suppose:

> A becomes critical when anxious. B withdraws. A becomes more critical. B withdraws further.

A systemic formulation needs no assumption beyond recursive feedback.

A psychoanalytic formulation might additionally hypothesise that the couple is enacting a deeper shared configuration involving dependency, rejection, dominance, or projected self-states.

These formulations differ in explanatory ambition.

The systemic account asks:

> What maintains the pattern?

The psychoanalytic account may ask:

> Why does this particular pattern carry so much unconscious psychological necessity for these particular people?

The second is potentially illuminating.

It is also further from observation.

---

## 59. Mutual recognition, mentalization, and responsiveness are not identical

These concepts overlap functionally but come from different traditions.

### Responsiveness

Primarily asks whether the partner reacts in ways that register the other's needs and values.

### Mentalization

Asks whether a person can represent behaviour in terms of uncertain mental states while preserving the opacity of another mind.

### Mutual recognition

In relational psychoanalysis, concerns recognising the other as a separate subject rather than reducing them to an object within one's own subjectivity.

### Attunement

Concerns emotionally understanding and responding to another's experience.

A partner can mentalize accurately but respond poorly.

A partner can be responsive without articulating sophisticated mental-state representations.

A person can understand what the other feels and still refuse to recognise their autonomy.

Conceptual precision matters.

---

## 60. Relationship processes versus incompatibility

Not every recurring conflict is a dysfunctional process masking a solvable need.

Some conflicts concern genuine incompatibility.

Possible incompatibilities include:

- whether to have children;
- monogamy versus non-monogamy;
- where to live;
- religion;
- financial risk tolerance;
- desired degree of family involvement;
- career priorities;
- fundamental sexual preferences;
- relationship structure;
- substance use;
- lifestyle;
- values;
- willingness to remain in the relationship.

Process and incompatibility interact.

A couple can communicate terribly about a difference that might otherwise be manageable.

They can also communicate beautifully about a difference that remains irreconcilable.

Therapeutic sophistication should not create the illusion that every difference can be processed into compatibility.

A useful formulation asks two separate questions:

1. **How are we handling the difference?**
2. **Is the difference itself livable for both of us?**

Improving the first does not guarantee a favourable answer to the second.

---

## 61. Persistent difference without pathology

Some recurring disagreements reflect stable preference differences.

One partner wants more social contact.

The other wants more solitude.

One prefers financial security.

The other values discretionary spending.

One wants frequent sex.

The other wants less.

One likes detailed planning.

The other prefers spontaneity.

The presence of the difference does not establish pathology.

Relational competence includes the capacity to:

- negotiate;
- alternate;
- accept;
- compartmentalise;
- find compensating arrangements;
- recognise limits.

The aim is not to eliminate all difference.

It is to prevent difference from automatically becoming disrespect, coercion, chronic deprivation, or mutual negation.

---

## 62. When process becomes the problem

A manageable difference can become destructive because of the process surrounding it.

For example:

> Difference: A wants more frequent contact with extended family than B.

Possible healthy process:

- discuss;
- understand meanings;
- negotiate frequency;
- make room for separate attendance;
- revisit as circumstances change.

Possible destructive process:

- A defines B as selfish;
- B defines A as enmeshed;
- each recruits family members;
- every visit becomes a loyalty test;
- withdrawal and retaliation follow.

The original incompatibility may be moderate.

The recursive process has become the larger problem.

This is where relationship-system thinking adds value.

---

## 63. When the substantive issue remains the problem

The reverse also occurs.

A couple may become highly skilled at communicating about a real incompatibility.

The incompatibility remains.

Examples include:

- one person wants children and the other does not;
- one requires monogamy and the other does not want it;
- one wants to relocate permanently and the other cannot accept leaving;
- one requires sobriety for the relationship and the other does not intend to stop using;
- one needs a sexual practice the other does not consent to.

Good communication may clarify rather than solve the problem.

Clarity is still an important outcome.

---

## 64. What the relationship system can explain

A relationship-system perspective is particularly useful for explaining:

- why the same issue repeats;
- how each response alters the other's next response;
- why intentions and outcomes diverge;
- how protective strategies become provocative;
- why expectations become self-reinforcing;
- how roles polarise;
- why one partner behaves differently in different relationships;
- how ordinary practices create relational climate;
- how trust changes through repetition;
- how external stress enters interaction;
- why repair becomes easier or harder over time;
- how each partner can lose access to capacities visible elsewhere.

It can also explain how neither partner intended the final pattern.

Two people can jointly produce an outcome neither wants.

---

## 65. What the relationship system cannot explain by itself

A systemic formulation is insufficient for determining:

- whether a factual allegation is true;
- who lied;
- whether consent was present;
- whether a boundary was agreed;
- whether someone committed aggression;
- whether money was concealed;
- whether one partner is threatening the other;
- whether a sexual act was coerced;
- whether one person is safe;
- whether a partner actually intends to leave;
- whether values are fundamentally incompatible.

The relationship system is an explanatory level.

It is not a fact-finding substitute.

---

## 66. Circular causation does not mean equal responsibility

This is a mandatory safeguard.

A relationship can contain reciprocal feedback while responsibility remains sharply asymmetric.

Consider:

> A discovers B has concealed substantial debt.

A confronts B angrily.

B minimises.

A escalates.

B withdraws.

A follows B and continues demanding an answer.

A systemic account can accurately describe the escalation cycle.

But the debt concealment remains B's action.

The cycle did not “cause itself.”

Similarly:

> betrayal → protest → defence → escalation

is not analytically equivalent to:

> ordinary complaint → defensiveness → counterattack.

The precipitating acts, stakes, and accountability are different.

**Mutual influence is not equal culpability.**

---

## 67. Asymmetric responsibility: explicit cases

| Situation | Reciprocal cycle may still develop? | What systemic analysis can explain | What it must not erase |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Unilateral deception | Yes | how discovery, protest, defence, and withdrawal escalate | responsibility for the deception |
| Betrayal | Yes | how mistrust and defensive reactions become recurrent | the betraying act and its repair requirements |
| Aggression | Yes | triggers, escalation, retaliation, fear responses | responsibility for violent or threatening behaviour |
| Coercion | Yes | how the other adapts, appeases, resists, or withdraws | coercive control and loss of free choice |
| Financial control | Yes | conflict around spending, secrecy, dependence | restriction of access to money or economic autonomy |
| Sexual coercion | Yes | fear, avoidance, resentment, shutdown | absence of freely given consent |
| Boundary violation | Yes | protest, defensiveness, repeated conflict | responsibility for violating an explicit boundary |
| Intimidation | Yes | accommodation and hypervigilance | responsibility for creating fear |
| Retaliation | Yes | reciprocal hostility can follow | deliberate punitive action |

Systemic language becomes harmful when it converts:

> “A did X, B responded Y”

into:

> “They both caused X.”

Those are different statements.

---

## 68. Explanation does not equal exoneration

A behaviour may be understandable and still unacceptable.

A person may lie because they fear conflict.

The fear helps explain the lie.

It does not make lying the partner's responsibility.

A person may become physically aggressive when flooded.

Flooding helps explain reduced regulation.

It does not transfer responsibility for aggression to the person who raised the issue.

A person may control money because of intense anxiety about financial insecurity.

The anxiety is clinically relevant.

Financial control remains a behavioural and power issue.

A relationship formulation should therefore distinguish:

- vulnerability;
- trigger;
- meaning;
- emotion;
- behaviour;
- intention;
- impact;
- responsibility;
- repair.

---

## 69. Power changes the meaning of circularity

The same visible A-B sequence can mean something different under unequal power.

Consider:

> A criticises. B goes silent.

In a relatively equal relationship, this might reflect ordinary attack/withdraw dynamics.

In a coercive relationship, B's silence may be a survival strategy.

Similarly:

> A checks B's location. B becomes secretive.

This could be mutual mistrust.

But if A is using surveillance and threats, describing B's secrecy as an equal contribution to “the trust cycle” obscures coercion.

Power must therefore be mapped **before** symmetrical cycle language is trusted.

---

## 70. Relationship-specific reality and accountability

Partners can genuinely experience the same event differently.

This does not mean all interpretations are equally supported.

Subjective multiplicity and factual reality must coexist.

Possible structure:

### Fact

The account was opened without the partner's knowledge.

### A's experience

“I feel betrayed.”

### B's experience

“I felt desperate and ashamed.”

### Dyadic process

Confrontation repeatedly escalates into defence and withdrawal.

### Context

The couple had significant financial stress.

### Responsibility

B opened the account secretly.

### Formulation

Shame and conflict avoidance may help explain the concealment.

This layered account is richer than either:

> “B is simply bad”

or:

> “They created the secrecy together.”

---

## 71. Betrayal and trust

Betrayal differs from ordinary misattunement because an agreement, expectation, or reliance has been violated in a consequential way.

Possible betrayals include:

- sexual infidelity under an exclusivity agreement;
- sustained deception;
- secret financial behaviour;
- disclosure of private information;
- abandonment during a critical period;
- hidden parallel relationships;
- major breaches of agreed boundaries.

Betrayal often changes later perception.

Neutral events may acquire threat value.

Uncertainty becomes harder to tolerate.

The injured partner may monitor more closely.

The offending partner may feel chronically scrutinised.

A new recursive cycle can emerge around an asymmetric original event.

Both levels require attention.

---

## 72. Repair after asymmetric harm

Ordinary reciprocal conflict can often be repaired through mutual change.

Asymmetric harm usually requires something additional from the person who committed it.

Potential requirements include:

- truthful disclosure;
- acknowledgment;
- responsibility;
- ending the harmful behaviour;
- behavioural transparency where appropriate and freely negotiated;
- respect for boundaries;
- understanding impact;
- consistent changed behaviour;
- tolerance of the injured partner's slower recovery;
- acceptance that forgiveness cannot be demanded.

The injured partner may also need to regulate protest or decide what conditions are necessary for continuation.

But their task is not symmetrical with the task of the person responsible for the breach.

---

## 73. Current relationship quality versus future change

Joel and colleagues provide one of the strongest anti-overprediction guardrails in this corpus.

Their large multi-study project found that relationship-specific self-reports could account for substantial variation in current relationship quality. Yet changes in relationship quality over time were largely unpredictable from the combinations of self-report variables examined.

The significance statement is even more direct: participants' own judgments about their relationship explained a large share of current satisfaction, while the models did not identify who would subsequently improve or deteriorate in relationship quality.

This leads to a crucial distinction.

### Describing the present

We can often say something useful about:

- current satisfaction;
- current conflict;
- perceived commitment;
- appreciation;
- sexual satisfaction;
- attachment-related anxiety;
- current trust.

### Predicting change

We should be much more cautious about saying:

- this couple will separate;
- this person will become more avoidant;
- this relationship is doomed;
- current satisfaction will necessarily decline;
- one conflict reveals the future;
- one attachment score determines trajectory.

The relationship is a dynamic system whose future includes events that have not happened yet.

---

## 74. Why future change is hard to predict

Future relationship quality depends partly on future data.

These may include:

- how partners respond to events that have not yet occurred;
- new stressors;
- changes in health;
- children;
- work;
- sexual changes;
- betrayal;
- successful repair;
- therapy;
- social support;
- financial change;
- maturation;
- new goals;
- changed behaviour.

A baseline measurement cannot contain all of this information.

This is not a reason to abandon formulation.

It is a reason to distinguish:

> **current explanation from future forecast.**

---

## 75. Attachment scores are not relationship destiny

Attachment anxiety and avoidance matter.

They are not sufficient forecasts.

A person high in anxiety may become more secure in a reliably responsive relationship.

A more avoidant person may engage differently with a partner who respects autonomy while remaining available.

Another partner may amplify insecurity.

Context and dyadic experience matter.

Likewise, a current secure pattern does not guarantee future quality in the face of betrayal, coercion, incompatibility, or overwhelming external stress.

Attachment is a mechanism, not fate.

---

## 76. Personality is not destiny

Predispositions matter because people do not enter relationships as blank slates.

But a personality variable cannot by itself answer:

- whether this pairing works;
- whether trust will be built;
- how labour will be divided;
- whether values align;
- whether attraction persists;
- whether a crisis will occur;
- whether repair succeeds.

The empirical relationship-science emphasis on uniqueness makes this explicit.

The relevant object is not only:

> Person A.

Nor only:

> Person B.

It is:

> A-with-B-under-these-conditions-over-time.

---

## 77. One conflict is not a trajectory

A single conflict can be important.

It can even be diagnostic.

But one conflict should not be automatically converted into:

- an attachment classification;
- a personality diagnosis;
- proof of incompatibility;
- proof of abuse;
- proof of relationship failure;
- proof of unconscious projective dynamics.

The correct next question is usually:

> Has this happened before, under what conditions, and what happened after?

Patterns require repetition.

Severe one-off acts can still require immediate accountability even before a pattern is established.

The distinction is between **pattern inference** and **severity of event**.

---

## 78. Current satisfaction is not a forecast

High satisfaction today does not guarantee future resilience.

Low satisfaction today does not establish inevitable decline.

Relationship satisfaction is an important current evaluation, not a destiny score.

The same applies to:

- conflict frequency;
- sexual frequency;
- attachment insecurity;
- commitment;
- one partner's personality.

Relationship assessment should remain longitudinal.

---

## 79. No single theory should predict the future

A Jungian interpretation, attachment formulation, relational psychoanalytic account, systemic cycle, or Gottman-style interaction analysis can illuminate a relationship.

None should be treated as an oracle.

The prediction-humility principle is especially important in self-analysis because theory can create compelling narratives.

A compelling narrative can feel predictive even when it is not.

---

## 80. Practical cycle mapping

The most useful way to analyse a recurring relational pattern is to slow it down.

The mandatory base template is:

> **TRIGGER  
> → A'S MEANING  
> → A'S EMOTION  
> → A'S ACTION  
> → B'S MEANING  
> → B'S EMOTION  
> → B'S ACTION  
> → FEEDBACK TO A  
> → ESCALATION / WITHDRAWAL / REPAIR**

The template should first be completed descriptively.

Only then should theoretical layers be added.

---

## 81. Base cycle template

### TRIGGER

What happened immediately before the cycle began?

Use observable language.

Examples:

- B arrived home two hours later than agreed.
- A asked whether they could discuss household spending.
- B declined sexual contact.
- A looked at their phone during dinner.
- A's parent criticised B.
- B did not respond to a message for several hours.

Do not write:

> “B abandoned A.”

unless abandonment itself is the established event.

---

### A'S MEANING

What meaning did A report or appear to assign?

Prefer direct report.

Examples:

- “I thought I was not important.”
- “I thought you were avoiding responsibility.”
- “I thought you were trying to control me.”

If inferred, label it:

> Possible meaning: “My needs do not matter.”

---

### A'S EMOTION

Distinguish where possible:

- fear;
- sadness;
- anger;
- shame;
- disgust;
- loneliness;
- helplessness;
- jealousy;
- excitement;
- hurt.

Also record bodily arousal.

---

### A'S ACTION

What did A do?

Examples:

- asked again;
- criticised;
- raised voice;
- withdrew;
- sent repeated messages;
- joked;
- touched;
- apologised;
- left the room.

---

### B'S MEANING

How did B interpret A's action?

Again distinguish report from inference.

---

### B'S EMOTION

What did B report feeling?

What physiological state was visible or reported?

---

### B'S ACTION

Describe rather than diagnose.

---

### FEEDBACK TO A

How did B's response alter A's original interpretation?

Did it confirm, disconfirm, or complicate it?

---

### ESCALATION / WITHDRAWAL / REPAIR

What happened next?

Did the system amplify, freeze, or recover?

---

## 82. Extended cycle template

After the base sequence is clear, add the following.

| Layer | Questions |
|---|---|
| Context | What external pressures were present? Sleep? Work? Money? Parenting? Illness? |
| History | What previous events made this trigger meaningful? |
| Attachment | Did either person experience availability, rejection, abandonment, engulfment, or vulnerability as salient? |
| Regulation | Was either person flooded, numb, hyperaroused, or dependent on the other to regulate them? |
| Mentalization | Could each person distinguish what they knew from what they feared or inferred? |
| Identity | Did either feel disrespected, diminished, incompetent, controlled, or unseen? |
| Power | Who had more practical, emotional, economic, or relational leverage? |
| Trust | Did the event activate an existing trust history? |
| Standards | What expectations about relationships were being applied? |
| Goals | What was each person trying to achieve? |
| Alternatives | Was the relationship being compared with another possible life or partner? |
| Culture | Were gender, family, religion, sexuality, or cultural scripts relevant? |
| Ordinary practices | Had time, affection, household work, humour, sex, or routine care changed recently? |
| Accountability | Did either person commit an identifiable harmful act requiring unilateral responsibility? |
| Alternative explanations | What else could explain the same behaviour? |
| Repair | What attempt was made, and was it accepted? |
| Outcome | What changed after the episode? |

---

## 83. Evidence coding inside the cycle

Each entry can be tagged by epistemic status.

**[OBSERVATION]**  
B left the room at 8:42 pm.

**[SUBJECTIVE REPORT]**  
A said, “I felt abandoned.”

**[RECURRING PATTERN]**  
In four recent conflicts, B has left after A intensified questioning.

**[FORMULATION]**  
B may become physiologically flooded and regulate through distance.

**[ALTERNATIVE FORMULATION]**  
B may also use withdrawal to avoid accountability.

**[THEORY-SPECIFIC INTERPRETATION]**  
A relational psychoanalytic formulation might examine whether the couple repeatedly becomes organised around pursuer and withholding self-states.

The labels prevent theory from hardening into fact.

---

## 84. A cycle map should include what did not happen

Absences can be highly informative.

Ask:

- Was there no acknowledgment?
- No apology?
- No return after a pause?
- No expression of need?
- No affection?
- No curiosity?
- No direct request?
- No boundary?
- No practical change?
- No escalation despite disagreement?
- No retaliation?

A relationship is partly organised by omissions as well as actions.

---

## 85. Alternative explanations are mandatory

Suppose B withdraws.

Possible formulations include:

- flooding;
- attachment deactivation;
- low investment;
- conflict avoidance;
- shame;
- anger;
- strategic punishment;
- need for reflection;
- exhaustion;
- fear of A;
- belief that discussion is futile.

Do not pick the most psychologically interesting explanation.

Ask what evidence discriminates between them.

For example:

- Does B reliably return later?
- Does withdrawal occur only at high arousal?
- Does B withdraw from everyone or specifically A?
- Does B become more engaged when A approaches differently?
- Does withdrawal happen mainly when B may be accountable?
- Does B explicitly say they fear A's response?

The relationship becomes a source of data.

---

## 86. Worked map: ordinary practical conflict

### Trigger

B leaves several household tasks undone after agreeing to do them.

### A's meaning

“You expect me to carry the household.”

### A's emotion

Anger, fatigue, resentment.

### A's action

Critical reminder.

### B's meaning

“Nothing I do is good enough.”

### B's emotion

Shame, irritation.

### B's action

Defends previous contributions and disengages.

### Feedback to A

“He will not take responsibility.”

### Escalation

A lists more examples. B withdraws further.

### Possible mechanisms

- unequal domestic labour;
- identity threat;
- demand/withdraw reinforcement;
- different standards for household work;
- external overload.

### Attachment hypothesis

Possible, but not required.

### Power analysis

Audit who actually performs the work before describing the issue as symmetrical.

### Alternative explanation

B may be overloaded rather than entitled.

### Test

Make the division of labour explicit and measurable for several weeks. Observe whether conflict declines when the substantive workload changes.

This test can distinguish a process problem from a material inequity more effectively than additional interpretation alone.

---

## 87. Worked map: pursue/withdraw after uncertain contact

### Trigger

B does not reply for several hours.

### A's meaning

“I am being pushed away.”

### A's emotion

Fear followed by anger.

### A's action

Multiple messages and increasingly accusatory language.

### B's meaning

“I am being controlled.”

### B's emotion

Irritation and pressure.

### B's action

Delays responding further.

### Feedback to A

“The silence proves I do not matter.”

### Escalation

More protest, more withdrawal.

### Attachment formulation

A's behaviour may involve hyperactivation; B's may involve deactivation.

### Alternative explanations

- B was genuinely unavailable;
- B avoids criticism rather than intimacy;
- A has learned distrust from B's previous unreliability;
- the couple lacks a shared communication expectation.

### Trust question

Has B usually been reliable?

### Power question

Does A's messaging cross a boundary into surveillance or coercive monitoring?

### Behavioural test

Agree on realistic expectations for unavailable periods and a reliable return signal. Observe whether uncertainty decreases.

---

## 88. Worked map: sexual desire discrepancy

### Trigger

A initiates sex. B declines.

### A's meaning

“You no longer desire me.”

### A's emotion

Hurt, shame.

### A's action

Becomes distant.

### B's meaning

“Saying no causes punishment.”

### B's emotion

Pressure, anxiety.

### B's action

Avoids later physical affection to prevent another initiation.

### Feedback to A

“Even touch has disappeared. I was right.”

### Escalation

Sexual and affectionate distance increase.

### Possible mechanisms

- identity threat;
- rejection sensitivity;
- pressure reducing desire;
- fatigue;
- parenting;
- medical factors;
- relational resentment;
- ordinary desire discrepancy.

### Attachment hypothesis

Possible, but not sufficient.

### Attraction hypothesis

Relevant if interest, novelty, or positive feeling have broadly declined.

### Power and consent

B's right to decline sex remains non-negotiable.

### Test

Separate affectionate touch from automatic sexual escalation for an agreed period. Examine whether safety and non-demand closeness alter the pattern.

---

## 89. Worked map: betrayal

### Trigger

A discovers B's undisclosed outside relationship.

### A's meaning

“The relationship I thought I had was not real.”

### A's emotion

Shock, anger, grief, fear.

### A's action

Questions B intensely.

### B's meaning

“I am trapped and condemned.”

### B's emotion

Shame and fear.

### B's action

Minimises details and withdraws.

### Feedback to A

“B is still hiding things.”

### Escalation

A intensifies questioning. B becomes more defensive.

### Dyadic cycle

Yes.

### Asymmetry

B remains responsible for the deception.

### Attachment layer

The discovery may now create acute attachment insecurity.

### Trust layer

B's continuing minimisation may make distrust reality-based rather than merely anxiety-based.

### Repair requirement

Cycle reduction alone is insufficient. Truthfulness, responsibility, changed behaviour, boundaries, and time are required if repair is attempted.

This example shows why the systemic and accountability maps must coexist.

---

## 90. Mapping the cycle longitudinally

A recurring-cycle claim should ideally be based on multiple episodes.

Use a table such as:

| Episode | Trigger | A action | B action | Repair | Context | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | money | criticism | defence | none | fatigue | unresolved |
| 2 | chores | criticism | withdrawal | apology next day | work stress | partial |
| 3 | family | direct request | engagement | negotiated plan | lower stress | resolved |
| 4 | money | direct request | initial defence then engagement | repair | moderate stress | improved |

This makes change visible.

It can reveal that what looked like a fixed personality problem is conditional.

For example:

> B does not always withdraw. Withdrawal is more likely when A begins with global criticism and when B is already overloaded.

Or:

> B still withdraws even when A raises the issue calmly, particularly when B may be accountable.

These are different formulations.

---

## 91. Recurrence, frequency, severity, and repair

Four dimensions should be tracked separately.

### Frequency

How often does it happen?

### Severity

How harmful is it when it happens?

### Duration

How long does the system remain dysregulated?

### Repair

What happens afterwards?

A rare but severe coercive incident may be more consequential than frequent low-level bickering.

A couple that argues often but repairs within twenty minutes is different from a couple whose monthly arguments produce three days of silence.

Frequency alone is not enough.

---

## 92. Practical indicators of a more flexible relationship system

No single indicator defines relational health, but greater flexibility often involves increased capacity to:

- state needs directly;
- hear influence;
- tolerate difference;
- distinguish facts from feared meanings;
- return after conflict;
- repair;
- apologise;
- accept responsibility;
- maintain boundaries;
- preserve humour without contempt;
- share practical labour more fairly;
- allow both closeness and autonomy;
- respond to bids;
- remain curious under uncertainty;
- discuss sex without coercion;
- update old expectations when new evidence accumulates.

Flexibility does not mean permanent calm.

It means the relationship has more than one possible next move.

---

## 93. Signs of rigidification

A system becomes more rigid when:

- every disagreement becomes the same argument;
- partners can predict the other's response before speaking;
- predictions become self-confirming;
- one role is permanently assigned to each person;
- attempts at repair are automatically rejected;
- disagreement becomes evidence of disloyalty;
- all behaviour is interpreted through one negative story;
- affection disappears outside rare crisis repair;
- one partner monopolises reality;
- power prevents honest disagreement;
- ordinary routines become chronically resentful;
- the relationship has little room for novelty.

Rigidification can emerge from reciprocal interaction.

It can also be imposed by power.

The distinction matters.

---

## 94. Relationship-specific self versus loss of self

Close relationships naturally influence identity.

Integration is not automatically pathology.

Partners develop:

- shared language;
- rituals;
- memories;
- joint goals;
- couple identity;
- mutual influence.

Problems arise when integration becomes compulsory erasure.

Warning signs include:

- needing permission for ordinary autonomy;
- chronic fear of disagreement;
- loss of friendships;
- inability to form independent opinions;
- one partner defining the other's emotional reality;
- identity becoming entirely organised around caretaking or appeasing.

The goal is not maximum independence.

It is **connection with agency**.

---

## 95. The relationship can expand self

A relational system can also increase capacity.

A partner may become more:

- confident;
- adventurous;
- emotionally articulate;
- sexually comfortable;
- socially connected;
- reflective;
- playful;
- ambitious;
- secure;
- tolerant of uncertainty.

This is one reason relationships cannot be understood only through deficit models.

The relationship can be a developmental environment.

But development should not be equated with staying together. A relationship can facilitate growth for a period and later become incompatible.

---

## 96. Relationship quality is multidimensional

A useful relationship profile should not collapse everything into one global score.

Relevant dimensions can include:

- trust;
- affection;
- respect;
- attraction;
- sexual satisfaction;
- companionship;
- practical fairness;
- conflict process;
- caregiving;
- autonomy;
- shared goals;
- emotional responsiveness;
- power;
- safety;
- humour;
- reliability.

Two couples with the same global satisfaction score may therefore be very different.

Likewise, a person can simultaneously report:

> “I love this person deeply.”

and:

> “This relationship is not workable for me.”

No contradiction is required.

---

## 97. Relationship maintenance versus relationship preservation

Maintenance processes help relationships persist.

Preservation is not always the appropriate goal.

Some relationships should change substantially before they are maintained.

Others may end.

Relationship-outcome neutrality requires that analysis remain open to:

- repair;
- renegotiation;
- acceptance;
- changed boundaries;
- separation;
- ending.

A theory of relationship persistence is not a moral theory of when people should persist.

---

## 98. The relationship as data, not verdict

Recurring interaction gives information.

It should be used to update hypotheses.

For example:

### Hypothesis

B withdraws primarily because A's criticism is overwhelming.

### Test

A raises several difficult issues directly but without global criticism.

### Possible result

B remains present.

Confidence in the hypothesis increases.

### Alternative result

B still refuses discussion whenever accountability is involved.

Confidence shifts toward an avoidance or accountability formulation.

Relationship analysis becomes stronger when it moves from interpretation to observation.

---

## 99. What ordinary acts can test

Small behavioural changes can discriminate between formulations.

### Hypothesis: lack of couple time drives disconnection

Test: protect regular undistracted time.

### Hypothesis: perceived unfairness drives resentment

Test: rebalance concrete labour.

### Hypothesis: flooding drives withdrawal

Test: structured pause plus reliable return.

### Hypothesis: pursuit is triggered mainly by uncertainty

Test: clear availability signals.

### Hypothesis: desire is inhibited by pressure

Test: establish periods of affectionate contact without expected sexual escalation.

### Hypothesis: the problem is basic incompatibility

Test: improve process while leaving the substantive difference unchanged.

If distress remains concentrated on the difference, incompatibility becomes more plausible.

## 101. Core non-equivalences

The following distinctions are mandatory.

| Do not collapse | Why |
|---|---|
| Individual disposition = relationship pattern | A disposition interacts with partner and context |
| Relationship-specific perception = objective partner trait | Perception matters without proving the inference |
| Interaction behaviour = motive | Behaviour is observable; motive is inferred |
| Pursue/withdraw = attachment anxiety/avoidance | Similar behaviour can have different functions |
| Circular causation = equal responsibility | Reciprocal influence can coexist with unilateral wrongdoing |
| Responsiveness = agreement | One can understand without agreeing |
| Validation = factual confirmation | A feeling can be validated while an interpretation is disputed |
| Mentalization = mind-reading | Mentalization preserves uncertainty |
| Trust = felt safety alone | Feelings of trust can under- or overestimate trustworthiness |
| Conflict = incompatibility | Process may create distress around manageable differences |
| Process improvement = compatibility | A real substantive conflict may remain |
| Interdependence = fusion | Mutual influence does not require loss of separateness |
| Attachment = all intimacy | Attraction, identity, power, sexuality, goals, and culture also matter |
| Influence = coercion | Influence is inevitable; coercion removes free choice |
| Relational thirdness = systemic feedback | Different theoretical constructs |
| Couple state of mind = statistical dyadic effect | Different level and evidence status |
| Shared unconscious phantasy = observed cycle | One is a theory-specific interpretation |
| Projective system = ordinary reciprocal reinforcement | Functional overlap does not establish theoretical identity |
| Desire discrepancy = sexual dysfunction | Different desire levels may be ordinary |
| Household conflict = communication problem | Material inequality may be central |
| Repair = forgiveness | Repair can occur without forgiveness |
| Forgiveness = reconciliation | One can forgive and still leave |
| Reconciliation = preservation at any cost | Safety and compatibility remain separate questions |

## 102. What this file can legitimately conclude

The relationship system can be treated as a real level of analysis because partners repeatedly influence one another and develop relationship-specific patterns.

Relationship quality depends partly on:

- what each person brings;
- what happens specifically between them;
- how each experiences the relationship;
- how they respond to one another;
- whether conflict can be repaired;
- whether trust is warranted and felt;
- how power is distributed;
- whether difference can be tolerated;
- whether ordinary care remains alive;
- whether attraction and intimacy have viable expression;
- whether practical labour is workable;
- whether external stress overwhelms resources;
- whether standards and goals are compatible enough;
- whether serious harms occur.

No single one of these variables is the relationship.

---

## 103. What this file cannot legitimately conclude

This framework cannot, from limited material:

- diagnose either partner;
- establish an attachment style from one interaction;
- infer a childhood history from adult behaviour;
- determine an unconscious motive with certainty;
- prove projective identification;
- prove shared unconscious phantasy;
- predict whether a couple will stay together;
- infer relationship viability from one satisfaction score;
- decide whether partners “should” stay;
- treat circularity as evidence of equal blame;
- reinterpret coercion as mutual misattunement;
- use psychological explanation to erase accountability.

## 104. Relationship-system assessment handoff

The sequence below is local to File 02 and keeps relationship-level variables visible. For full cross-corpus integrated formulation and final applied response structure, **File 06 is authoritative**.

For any recurring couple problem, move through the following order.

### 1. Event

What concretely happened?

### 2. Subjective experience

What did each person report feeling, thinking, fearing, wanting, or needing?

### 3. Behaviour

What did each actually do?

### 4. Repetition

Has this sequence happened before?

### 5. Feedback

How did each person's behaviour change the other's next response?

### 6. Relationship-specific perception

What does each currently believe about the relationship and partner?

### 7. Regulation

Were flooding, shutdown, hyperarousal, or difficulties self-soothing relevant?

### 8. Responsiveness and trust

Was there a bid? How was it answered? What existing trust history shaped the meaning?

### 9. Power

Who could say no? Who controlled resources? Whose account defined reality?

### 10. Ordinary relationship context

What was happening with affection, humour, sex, shared time, domestic work, care, and companionship?

### 11. Broader context

What stressors, networks, cultural expectations, and material constraints were active?

### 12. Goals and standards

What was each person trying to achieve, and what standards were being applied?

### 13. Attachment

Did availability, rejection, closeness, or autonomy appear central?

### 14. Identity

Did recognition, respect, competence, shame, or control appear central?

### 15. Attraction and sexuality

Were desire, novelty, erotic interest, or sexual pressure relevant?

### 16. Alternative explanations

What other mechanisms fit?

### 17. Theory-specific depth formulation

Only if useful, consider relational thirdness, shared unconscious phantasy, projective systems, narcissistic difficulty with otherness, or other interpretive models.

### 18. Accountability

Did either person commit an identifiable harmful act?

### 19. Practical test

What low-risk behavioural change could discriminate among competing explanations?

### 20. Longitudinal outcome

What actually happens after the change?

## 105. Final synthesis

The strongest general conclusion is simple:

> **A close relationship is neither just two personalities nor an autonomous entity detached from them. It is an evolving system of interdependence in which two psychologically distinct people repeatedly shape one another under specific historical, practical, social, and cultural conditions.**

Several consequences follow.

1. **The pairing matters.**  
   Knowing each individual separately does not fully predict what they create together.

2. **Perception matters.**  
   People respond to the relationship they experience, not merely to an objective inventory of partner behaviour.

3. **Behaviour matters.**  
   Interpretive depth never makes concrete actions irrelevant.

4. **Sequence matters.**  
   One partner's protection can become the other's trigger.

5. **History matters.**  
   Current events acquire meaning from what has repeatedly happened before.

6. **Responsiveness matters.**  
   Relationships are continually shaped by whether bids, needs, vulnerability, and achievements are noticed and answered.

7. **Repair matters.**  
   Rupture is normal; repeated inability or unwillingness to repair has different implications.

8. **Regulation matters.**  
   High physiological and emotional arousal can radically narrow each partner's available responses.

9. **Identity matters.**  
   Couple conflict is not only about attachment. Recognition, respect, competence, agency, and shame can organise interaction.

10. **Power matters.**  
    Dependence, money, labour, influence, sexual autonomy, and the right to define reality are relational variables in their own right.

11. **Attraction and sexuality matter.**  
    They have their own dynamics and should not be reduced to attachment security.

12. **Caregiving matters.**  
    Adult partnership includes giving as well as seeking comfort, but care should not erase agency.

13. **Ordinary life matters.**  
    Cups of tea, chores, jokes, touch, shared television, messages, meals, small kindnesses, and time together can accumulate into the lived structure of intimacy.

14. **Stress matters.**  
    Material pressure can degrade relationship functioning without proving that the bond itself was originally deficient.

15. **Culture matters.**  
    Couples are embedded in norms, institutions, networks, and histories.

16. **Difference matters.**  
    Intimacy requires some capacity to encounter an actual other rather than demand a replica of oneself.

17. **The “third” must remain theoretically disciplined.**  
    Dyadic emergence, systemic recursion, relational thirdness, and the Tavistock couple state of mind overlap functionally but are not interchangeable.

18. **Unconscious couple formulations are hypotheses.**  
    Shared unconscious phantasy and projective systems can be clinically generative, but they occupy a different evidentiary category from observed interaction and relationship-science findings.

19. **Circularity does not distribute blame equally.**  
    Deception, coercion, violence, sexual coercion, financial control, betrayal, and boundary violations retain asymmetric accountability even when they generate reciprocal cycles.

20. **The present is easier to describe than the future is to predict.**  
    Current relationship-specific experience contains substantial information about current relationship quality, but future change remains difficult to forecast confidently from attachment, personality, satisfaction, or other baseline self-reports alone.

The practical implication is to resist the temptation to find one final explanation.

A strong relational formulation asks instead:

> **What happened? What did each person make it mean? What did each feel and do? What did that evoke in the other? What pattern emerged? What context and history made it more likely? What role did attachment, regulation, identity, power, trust, attraction, sexuality, ordinary relationship practices, standards, alternatives, and stress play? What remains uncertain? Is there an asymmetric act that requires clear accountability? What alternative explanation fits? And what future observation would tell us whether the formulation is actually useful?**

That sequence keeps the relationship visible without making it mystical, keeps both individuals visible without reducing the relationship to personality, and preserves accountability without abandoning the reality of mutual influence.

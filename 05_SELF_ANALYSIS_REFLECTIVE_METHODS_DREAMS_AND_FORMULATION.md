# 05 | Self-Analysis, Reflective Methods, Dreams, and Formulation

## 1. Purpose

The purpose of disciplined self-analysis is not to discover a final hidden explanation for oneself.

It is to improve the quality of observation, reduce confusion between experience and fact, identify recurring patterns, develop plausible formulations, test alternatives, increase agency, and learn from what happens next.

The governing sequence is:

> **OBSERVATION → EXPERIENCE → PATTERN → FORMULATION → ALTERNATIVE → TEST → ACTION → FOLLOW-UP**

This sequence matters because introspection can easily become self-confirming. A person feels something strongly, generates an explanation for it, notices evidence that fits the explanation, and then treats the explanation as proven. The resulting account may be psychologically sophisticated while remaining weakly connected to what actually happened.

The method in this file is designed to interrupt that process.

A useful formulation should leave a person with at least one of the following:

- a clearer record of what occurred;
- a better distinction between fact and inference;
- a more precise account of affect and bodily activation;
- a recurring pattern supported by more than one episode;
- two or more plausible explanations;
- evidence that could distinguish those explanations;
- a safer or more deliberate behavioural alternative;
- a concrete test;
- a clearer boundary;
- greater acceptance of what cannot yet be known;
- longitudinal evidence that something is or is not changing.

If reflection produces only more explanation, more certainty about another person's mind, more theoretical labels, or more reasons to revisit the same material, it is not yet doing its job.

## 2. What this file is not

This file is not:

- a diagnostic instrument;
- a method for diagnosing a partner;
- a substitute for Files 01 to 04;
- a dream-symbol dictionary;
- a system for proving hidden events through dreams;
- an attachment-style test;
- a method for reconstructing childhood from present behaviour;
- a method for deciding whether a relationship should continue;
- a way to explain away betrayal, coercion, aggression, or boundary violations;
- a method for turning every feeling into evidence of an unconscious truth;
- a licence to interpret another person's motives without their report;
- a system for endlessly analysing oneself.

The file uses attachment, mentalization, agency, emotion, relationship cycles, rupture, repair, Jungian concepts, Adlerian concepts, and Lacanian concepts only where they contribute to the practical task of observation and formulation.

Their deeper theoretical reconstruction belongs elsewhere.

Use:

- **File 01** for attachment, internal working models, mentalization, self-agency, emotion regulation, autonomy, identity, and shame;
- **File 02** for dyadic cycles, responsiveness, trust, intimacy, sexuality, power, ordinary relationship practices, and context;
- **File 03** for couple-therapy models, mechanisms, procedures, and treatment evidence;
- **File 04** for rupture, betrayal, attachment injury, accountability, forgiveness, boundaries, safety, incompatibility, and endings;
- **File 06** for integrated multi-lens case formulation and extended couple-cycle analysis.

The present file owns the method by which lived material is turned into evidence that those other files can responsibly interpret.

## 3. The central epistemic discipline

### 3.1 The eight fields that must never be collapsed

Every exercise in this file uses the same epistemic separation.

### WHAT HAPPENED

Observable events.

Where possible, describe:

- words spoken;
- messages sent;
- actions taken;
- timing;
- sequence;
- location;
- directly observable behaviour.

Avoid motive words.

“She did not reply for three hours” is an observation.

“She ignored me for three hours because she wanted control” combines an observation with an inference.

### WHAT I FELT

Subjectively experienced affect.

Examples:

- afraid;
- angry;
- sad;
- ashamed;
- jealous;
- relieved;
- lonely;
- disgusted;
- tender;
- excited;
- confused.

The feeling is real as an experience even when the interpretation associated with it is inaccurate.

### WHAT I THOUGHT

Words, images, memories, interpretations, and automatic meanings that entered awareness.

Examples:

- “This is going badly.”
- “I am being rejected.”
- “I need to fix this now.”
- “Here we go again.”
- “I cannot rely on anyone.”

A thought is evidence that the thought occurred. It is not automatically evidence that its content is true.

### WHAT I FEARED

The anticipated negative outcome beneath or beside the immediate thought.

Examples:

- being abandoned;
- being humiliated;
- losing autonomy;
- being controlled;
- being unwanted;
- conflict becoming unmanageable;
- disappointing someone;
- becoming dependent;
- having one's needs dismissed.

Fear is an expected possibility, not proof that the feared event is happening.

### WHAT I WANTED

The desired outcome, need, request, boundary, or change.

Examples:

- reassurance;
- space;
- clarity;
- recognition;
- apology;
- affection;
- sexual contact;
- privacy;
- autonomy;
- a concrete answer;
- reliable return after a conflict pause.

This field often clarifies whether behaviour actually served the desired end.

### WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER

Any conclusion about the other person's internal state, motive, intention, commitment, character, or future behaviour.

Examples:

- “She does not care.”
- “He is avoiding intimacy.”
- “She is losing interest.”
- “He wants to punish me.”
- “She feels trapped.”
- “He is ashamed.”

These are hypotheses unless the other person has directly reported them.

### WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW

Information supported by:

- direct observation;
- explicit communication;
- reliable records;
- previously established facts.

Even direct statements have limits. “I need an hour alone” establishes that the person said they need an hour alone. It does not establish every motive behind the request.

### WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN

Anything the available evidence does not establish.

This is not an empty residual category. It is a core part of good formulation.

Examples:

- why the person did not reply;
- what they were feeling;
- whether a current episode has the same meaning as a previous one;
- whether a pattern will recur;
- whether a relationship problem reflects attachment insecurity, incompatibility, stress, resentment, habit, or some combination.

A disciplined self-analysis preserves unknowns instead of filling them automatically.

---

### 3.2 Experience is data, but not all data have the same status

Self-analysis often fails through one of two opposite errors.

The first error is to distrust subjective experience completely. A person says, “Maybe I am imagining everything,” and discounts repeated fear, hurt, anger, attraction, resentment, or boundary discomfort.

The second error is to treat subjective experience as direct access to objective reality. A person feels rejected and concludes that rejection has been established.

Both are mistakes.

Subjective experience is valid evidence of the person's experience.

It can reveal:

- what situations activate them;
- what meanings become salient;
- which emotions recur;
- what they expect;
- what they try to prevent;
- what they repeatedly do;
- how much agency remains available under stress.

It does not by itself establish:

- another person's motive;
- another person's attachment orientation;
- whether a betrayal occurred;
- whether a partner is lying;
- whether a feared future event will occur;
- whether a childhood explanation is correct.

The method therefore asks two questions at once:

> **What is this experience telling me about me?**

and

> **What, if anything, does the available evidence establish about the external situation?**

Those questions must remain separate.

## 4. The observation-to-action architecture

The practical method has eight stages.

### Stage 1. Observation

Record what happened before explaining it.

### Stage 2. Experience

Record:

- affect;
- bodily state;
- automatic thought;
- fear;
- desire;
- action tendency.

### Stage 3. Pattern

Ask whether a similar sequence has occurred before.

A pattern requires repetition.

### Stage 4. Formulation

Generate one or more plausible mechanisms.

Examples may include:

- attachment-system activation;
- emotion dysregulation;
- shame activation;
- physiological flooding;
- loss of mentalization;
- hostile attribution;
- learned conflict avoidance;
- threat to autonomy;
- threat to identity;
- unmet expectation;
- reinforcement of pursuit or withdrawal;
- external stress;
- genuine deterioration in trust;
- concrete incompatibility.

The formulation remains a hypothesis.

### Stage 5. Alternative

Generate at least one serious competing explanation.

A formulation that survives only because alternatives were never considered is weak.

### Stage 6. Test

Ask:

> **What evidence would look different if explanation A were true rather than explanation B?**

### Stage 7. Action

Ask:

> **What could I safely do differently that would generate new evidence?**

### Stage 8. Follow-up

Observe what happened after the change.

The full loop is therefore:

```text
EVENT
  ↓
AFFECT + BODY
  ↓
AUTOMATIC MEANING
  ↓
EXPECTATION / FEAR / DESIRE
  ↓
ACTION TENDENCY
  ↓
ACTUAL BEHAVIOUR
  ↓
PARTNER RESPONSE
  ↓
RELATIONSHIP CONSEQUENCE
  ↓
RECURRING PATTERN?
  ↓
PLAUSIBLE MECHANISMS
  ↓
COMPETING EXPLANATIONS
  ↓
DISCRIMINATING EVIDENCE
  ↓
BEHAVIOURAL EXPERIMENT
  ↓
OUTCOME
  ↓
REVISED FORMULATION
```

The final arrow matters. A formulation that cannot be revised is no longer functioning as a hypothesis.

## 5. The basic unit: an episode

The preferred unit of self-analysis is not “my personality,” “my relationship,” or “why I am like this.”

It is the **episode**.

An episode has:

1. a beginning;
2. a trigger or change in context;
3. an internal response;
4. behaviour;
5. another person's response where relevant;
6. a consequence;
7. a possible repair or ending.

Episodes can be compared.

Global self-descriptions are much harder to test.

Compare:

> “I am anxiously attached.”

with:

> “When communication becomes unexpectedly uncertain with this partner, I tend to interpret the uncertainty as possible rejection, feel strong fear, increase contact-seeking, and have difficulty waiting for additional information.”

The second statement is longer but more useful.

It identifies:

- the activating condition;
- the meaning;
- the emotion;
- the strategy;
- the relationship context;
- a potential experiment.

It also leaves open whether the pattern occurs elsewhere.

## 6. The fifteen-variable episode record

A complete episode record contains the following variables.

| Variable | Operational question | Main error to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Event | What occurred? | Adding motive to observation |
| 2. Affect | What did I feel? | Treating emotion as proof |
| 3. Bodily state | What happened physiologically? | Retrospective invention |
| 4. Automatic meaning | What did the event immediately mean to me? | Treating meaning as fact |
| 5. Expectation | What did I expect would happen next? | Confusing prediction with observation |
| 6. Action tendency | What did I feel pulled to do? | Treating impulse as behaviour |
| 7. Actual behaviour | What did I actually do? | Describing intention instead |
| 8. Partner response | What did the other actually do or say? | Mind-reading |
| 9. Relationship consequence | What happened to the interaction? | Blaming the entire cycle on one moment |
| 10. Recurring pattern | Where has this sequence appeared before? | Calling one episode a trait |
| 11. Plausible mechanisms | What processes may explain it? | Premature theoretical certainty |
| 12. Competing explanations | What else could explain it? | Straw-man alternatives |
| 13. Discriminating evidence | What evidence would distinguish accounts? | Looking only for confirming evidence |
| 14. Behavioural experiment | What safe change could produce new evidence? | Using “experiments” to manipulate another person |
| 15. Outcome tracking | What happened after the change? | Judging from one result alone |

Emotion should be considered both as subjective experience and as part of an action-organising system. Emotion-focused couple theory, for example, treats emotion as connected with appraisal, needs, goals, bodily organisation, action tendencies, communication, and interaction rather than as a free-standing “message” that automatically reveals objective truth.

## 7. Journaling as data collection rather than narrative accumulation

### 7.1 What journaling is for

A self-analysis journal should improve recall and comparison.

It should not become an archive of every thought.

Useful journaling captures:

- episodes worth comparing;
- recurring triggers;
- strong changes in affect;
- meaningful decisions;
- boundary events;
- repair attempts;
- behavioural experiments;
- dreams that are unusually affectively salient or recurrent;
- evidence that contradicts a current formulation.

A journal becomes more useful when it contains **comparable observations over time**.

It becomes less useful when entries become increasingly elaborate explanations of the same event.

---

### 7.2 Separate the record from the interpretation

Use two layers.

### Layer A: contemporaneous record

Written close to the event.

Capture:

- sequence;
- words;
- affect;
- body;
- immediate thoughts;
- action tendency;
- behaviour.

### Layer B: later formulation

Added after arousal has reduced enough for reflection.

Capture:

- pattern;
- alternative hypotheses;
- uncertainty;
- possible experiment;
- outcome.

This division helps reveal hindsight effects.

A later explanation should never silently overwrite the earlier record.

---

### 7.3 Preserve disconfirming evidence

If a working formulation is:

> “When my partner takes space, they do not return unless I pursue them,”

then record occasions when:

- they return voluntarily;
- the pause is shorter than expected;
- they initiate repair;
- withdrawal occurs only after specific forms of escalation;
- they explain the pause beforehand.

A formulation improves when contrary observations can be incorporated.

It weakens when contrary observations must be ignored.

## 8. Exercise 1: structured episode reconstruction

### Purpose

Use this when an episode feels emotionally important, confusing, or representative.

Do not begin with “What does this mean?”

Begin with reconstruction.

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:**  
   Record the sequence without motive language.

2. **WHAT I FELT:**  
   Name the affect as precisely as possible.

3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:**  
   Record automatic thoughts, images, memories, and meanings.

4. **WHAT I FEARED:**  
   Identify the feared outcome.

5. **WHAT I WANTED:**  
   Identify the desired outcome, request, boundary, or response.

6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:**  
   List assumptions about motive, intention, emotion, or commitment.

7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:**  
   Identify what is supported by observation or direct communication.

8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:**  
   Preserve unresolved questions.

### Reconstruction procedure

Then reconstruct the sequence:

```text
BEFORE THE EVENT
↓
TRIGGER
↓
FIRST BODY CHANGE
↓
FIRST AFFECT
↓
FIRST AUTOMATIC MEANING
↓
EXPECTATION
↓
ACTION TENDENCY
↓
WHAT I DID
↓
WHAT THE OTHER DID
↓
WHAT I THEN MADE THAT MEAN
↓
NEXT ACTION
↓
IMMEDIATE CONSEQUENCE
↓
LATER CONSEQUENCE
```

The important distinction is between **first response** and **later explanation**.

For example:

- the body tightens;
- fear appears;
- “something is wrong” follows;
- reassurance-seeking begins;
- delayed reassurance increases anger;
- anger is later remembered as the original reaction.

Without reconstruction, the person may conclude that the episode “was about anger” when fear preceded it, or may conclude that fear caused the entire interaction when a genuine boundary violation also occurred.

### Output

The exercise should end with one paragraph no longer than necessary:

> “When X happened, I first experienced Y and interpreted it as Z. I expected A, felt pulled toward B, and actually did C. The other person then did D. This resembles episodes E and F, but I do not yet know whether the main mechanism is G, H, or I.”

That paragraph is a formulation scaffold, not a diagnosis.

## 9. Exercise 2: trigger mapping

### Purpose

A trigger map identifies conditions under which a response becomes more likely.

The term “trigger” should not imply that the response is automatic, inevitable, traumatic, or caused exclusively by the immediate event.

A trigger is simply a condition associated with activation.

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What was the observable trigger?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What affect appeared?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What meaning appeared?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What negative outcome became salient?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What would have restored safety, agency, recognition, or clarity?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What motive or state did I attribute?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What was established?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What part of the trigger's meaning is uncertain?

### Build the map across episodes

Do not map only topics. Map **conditions**.

Potential conditions include:

- delayed response;
- ambiguous tone;
- unexpected change of plan;
- criticism;
- disagreement in public;
- sexual rejection;
- partner needing space;
- feeling excluded;
- uncertainty about commitment;
- broken agreement;
- request for greater closeness;
- request for greater autonomy;
- comparison with another person;
- fatigue;
- alcohol;
- work pressure;
- financial stress;
- unresolved previous conflict.

Then ask:

- Does the same event trigger the same response with different people?
- Does this trigger occur only with one person?
- Does activation depend on fatigue or external stress?
- Is the reaction strongest when the event is ambiguous?
- Does the response change when the other person communicates clearly?
- Is the trigger itself a real breach, or is the uncertainty around it doing most of the work?

### Output

A useful trigger map sounds like:

> “Unexpected uncertainty after a plan has already been agreed appears more activating than ordinary time apart. The activation is stronger when I cannot determine when contact will resume. Clear advance communication seems to reduce it.”

This is more useful than:

> “Distance triggers my abandonment wound.”

The latter may eventually form part of a deeper formulation, but it has skipped several evidentiary steps.

## 10. Exercise 3: affect, body, and action-tendency tracking

### Purpose

Emotion tracking should distinguish:

- affect;
- bodily arousal;
- meaning;
- action tendency;
- behaviour.

These are related but not identical.

Anger may organise approach or confrontation. Fear may organise escape, protest, freezing, vigilance, or reassurance-seeking. Shame may organise hiding, appeasing, attacking, explaining, or withdrawing. None of these pairings is universal.

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What preceded the affect?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** Name the affect without interpretation.
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What meaning accompanied it?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What danger or loss did I anticipate?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What state or response did I seek?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What did I assume they were feeling or intending?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What evidence was present?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** Which links in the emotion sequence are uncertain?

### Track the sequence

For each salient emotion:

```text
AFFECT
→ BODY
→ APPRAISAL
→ ACTION TENDENCY
→ CHOSEN BEHAVIOUR
→ CONSEQUENCE
```

Example:

```text
Fear
→ stomach tension, increased heart rate
→ "contact is becoming uncertain"
→ urge to send another message
→ sent three messages
→ partner withdrew further
```

A different episode might be:

```text
Fear
→ stomach tension
→ "I may be about to be criticised"
→ urge to defend
→ asked for clarification instead
→ misunderstanding resolved
```

The emotion may be similar while behavioural flexibility changes substantially.

That distinction is central to agency.

## 11. Agency tracking

Agency is not the absence of emotion.

It is the capacity to perceive, reflect, choose, and act while emotion is present.

The central question is:

> **What happens to my range of possible responses when this relational system is activated?**

Useful dimensions include:

- Can I notice activation before acting?
- Can I distinguish impulse from choice?
- Can I tolerate not knowing immediately?
- Can I ask rather than infer?
- Can I make a direct request?
- Can I say no?
- Can I pause without disappearing?
- Can I return after pausing?
- Can I receive information that contradicts my first interpretation?
- Can I stay connected without surrendering a boundary?
- Can I remain separate without using withdrawal as punishment?
- Can I act consistently with my values while distressed?

### Exercise 4: agency audit

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What situation activated me?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What affect was present?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What interpretation narrowed my options?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What did inaction, waiting, or directness seem to risk?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What outcome did I seek?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What assumptions shaped my options?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What constraints were real?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** Which feared constraints were assumed?

Then identify:

### Available actions I noticed at the time

List only options that actually entered awareness.

### Actions I can identify retrospectively

This reveals whether stress narrowed the perceived choice set.

### Action I took

Describe it behaviourally.

### Was the action chosen or compelled?

Avoid an absolute answer. Ask instead how much alternative action felt available.

### What increased agency?

Examples may include:

- clearer information;
- time;
- a pre-existing agreement;
- naming the emotion;
- direct communication;
- physical distance from an escalating interaction;
- remembering a boundary;
- knowing that a conflict pause had a reliable return time.

### What reduced agency?

Examples may include:

- ambiguity;
- time pressure;
- sleep loss;
- repeated contact attempts;
- escalating tone;
- perceived humiliation;
- intoxication;
- fear of retaliation;
- actual coercion.

Where retaliation, coercion, intimidation, or violence is credible, the problem is not merely reduced self-regulation. Route to File 04 and prioritise safety.

## 12. Relational pattern tracking

A relationship pattern is not simply “something my partner keeps doing.”

It is a recurring sequence.

A useful cycle map contains:

```text
A EVENT / BEHAVIOUR
→ B INTERPRETATION
→ B AFFECT
→ B ACTION
→ A INTERPRETATION
→ A AFFECT
→ A ACTION
→ B NEW INTERPRETATION
→ ...
```

The pattern may become self-reinforcing even when neither person intends the final outcome.

Circular influence, however, does not mean equal responsibility.

If one person lies, threatens, coerces, stalks, assaults, retaliates, or persistently violates a boundary, a cycle diagram must not convert the concrete behaviour into symmetrical “communication problems.”

### Exercise 5: relationship-cycle record

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What did each person observably do?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What did I experience?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What meaning did I assign?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What outcome did I expect?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What was I trying to obtain or prevent?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What did I assume their behaviour meant?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What did they directly communicate?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What part of their internal process is unavailable?

Then construct:

| Sequence | Observable behaviour | My meaning | My response | Other's observable response | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | |
| 3 | | | | | |

Finally ask:

- Where could I alter **my** part of the sequence without taking responsibility for the other person's conduct?
- What would count as a genuine change in the other person's participation?
- Which parts are mutually reinforcing?
- Which parts are unilateral problems that require accountability or boundary-setting rather than cycle modification?

Detailed theory of the couple system belongs in File 02.

## 13. Attachment-strategy tracking without self-typing

Attachment concepts are useful when they describe regulation under relational threat.

They become less useful when turned into identity statements.

Prefer:

> “Under uncertainty with this partner I sometimes use hyperactivating strategies.”

over:

> “I am an anxious person.”

Prefer:

> “During high-conflict discussions I sometimes deactivate by suppressing need and creating distance.”

over:

> “I am avoidant.”

Attachment theory itself is broad and evidence-based, but adult attachment research distinguishes dimensions and regulatory processes rather than requiring every episode to be explained by a fixed type.

### Exercise 6: attachment-strategy tracker

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What relational cue occurred?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What affect followed?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What attachment-relevant meaning arose?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** Loss, abandonment, engulfment, dependence, rejection, or something else?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** Contact, reassurance, distance, autonomy, certainty, care?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What did I assume about availability or responsiveness?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What evidence of availability or unavailability exists?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What cannot be inferred from this episode?

Then ask:

### Hyperactivating possibilities

Did I:

- intensify bids for contact;
- repeat the request;
- monitor for threat;
- demand certainty;
- protest;
- escalate urgency;
- interpret ambiguity pessimistically?

### Deactivating possibilities

Did I:

- suppress the need;
- minimise distress;
- withdraw;
- deny wanting contact;
- detach cognitively;
- dismiss the importance of the relationship;
- avoid vulnerability?

### Secure or flexible possibilities

Did I:

- state the need directly;
- tolerate a reasonable delay;
- seek clarification;
- maintain boundaries;
- allow the other person's separateness;
- regulate without denying the importance of connection;
- accept reassurance without immediately demanding further proof?

Do not infer childhood facts from the result.

Do not diagnose the partner's attachment orientation from their behaviour in one episode.

## 14. Mentalization failure and recovery

Mentalization means keeping behaviour connected to possible mental states while recognising that minds are partly opaque.

It is the opposite of both:

- treating behaviour as meaningless; and
- acting as if another person's mind were directly visible.

Under stress, mentalization may narrow.

Common signs include:

- one explanation becomes certain;
- ambiguity becomes intolerable;
- a feeling is treated as evidence;
- the other's behaviour is interpreted only through one's own fear;
- complex motives collapse into “they do not care” or “they are controlling”;
- one's own contribution becomes invisible;
- the other's perspective becomes impossible to imagine;
- alternatively, one's own perspective disappears and only the other's possible reasons seem legitimate.

Detailed mentalization theory belongs in File 01.

### Exercise 7: mentalization reset

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** Describe the behavioural record.
2. **WHAT I FELT:** State the affect.
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** State the first interpretation.
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** State the feared meaning.
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** State the desired response.
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** Write the inference explicitly.
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** Reduce the case to established information.
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** Name the inaccessible mental-state information.

Now generate at least three mental-state hypotheses.

Example:

**Observed:** Partner became quiet and left the room.

Possible explanations:

1. overwhelmed and trying not to escalate;
2. angry and withdrawing;
3. wanting to punish;
4. needing time to think;
5. feeling ashamed;
6. disengaging from the relationship;
7. exhausted by an unrelated stressor.

Do not keep all possibilities indefinitely.

Instead ask:

> **What information could distinguish them?**

Possible evidence:

- what the person later reports;
- whether they reliably return;
- whether the withdrawal occurs after specific interaction patterns;
- whether they remain responsive in lower-arousal contexts;
- whether space is used to regulate or to punish;
- whether a pause-and-return agreement changes the pattern.

Mentalization is not successful because the most charitable explanation was selected.

It is successful when unwarranted certainty decreases and better evidence becomes possible.

---

## 15. Assumption testing

An assumption is a proposition being used as though it were established.

Examples:

- “If I ask directly, I will be rejected.”
- “If I do not pursue, we will never resolve anything.”
- “If I set this boundary, the relationship will end.”
- “If they need space, they must be losing interest.”
- “If they cared, they would know without being asked.”
- “If I accept the apology, I am saying the behaviour was acceptable.”
- “If I feel jealous, there must be something wrong.”
- “If the conflict recurs, no progress has occurred.”

Some assumptions may be accurate.

The method does not challenge assumptions merely to replace them with optimistic ones.

It tests them.

### Exercise 8: assumption test

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What event activated the assumption?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What emotion followed?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** State the assumption as a sentence.
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What would happen if the assumption were true?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What outcome was I trying to secure?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What motive or response did I predict?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What evidence currently supports the assumption?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What evidence is missing?

Then complete four tests.

### Test A: confirming observations

What actual observations support the assumption?

### Test B: disconfirming observations

What observations do not fit it?

### Test C: alternative proposition

Construct a genuine alternative, not a reassuring slogan.

Example:

> “Direct requests may sometimes be rejected, but indirect protest may also make the need harder to respond to.”

### Test D: behavioural discrimination

What low-risk action would create useful evidence?

Example:

> Make one direct request without accusation and observe the response.

The result should update the assumption, not merely reward or punish the other person.

---

## 16. Attribution testing

An attribution explains another person's behaviour.

Attributions are especially vulnerable to self-confirmation because the partner's internal state is not directly observable.

The key distinction is:

```text
BEHAVIOUR ≠ MOTIVE
```

Examples:

- silence does not by itself establish punishment;
- reassurance does not establish commitment;
- irritation does not establish lack of love;
- distance does not establish attachment avoidance;
- affection does not establish that a substantive conflict is resolved;
- an apology does not establish future behavioural change.

### Exercise 9: attribution test

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** State the behaviour to be explained.
2. **WHAT I FELT:** Record your affective response.
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** State your first explanation.
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** State what that explanation implied.
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** State what response you hoped for.
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** Write the attribution clearly.
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** Identify direct evidence.
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** Identify the unobservable part.

Create an attribution matrix:

| Hypothesis | Evidence consistent with it | Evidence against it | Missing evidence | Safe way to clarify |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | | | | |
| H2 | | | | |
| H3 | | | | |

A useful alternative must be psychologically and situationally plausible.

Do not create a token alternative such as:

> “Maybe everything is fine.”

The aim is discrimination, not forced reassurance.

## 17. Repeating relational expectations

A relational expectation is an anticipation about how another person is likely to respond in a particular interpersonal situation.

Examples:

- “My needs will be too much.”
- “Conflict leads to abandonment.”
- “Closeness eventually becomes control.”
- “If I show vulnerability, it will be used against me.”
- “I must solve the other person's emotion.”
- “People say they will return, but they do not.”
- “If I depend on someone, I will lose autonomy.”
- “I have to prove my value to remain wanted.”

The important question is not merely where an expectation came from.

The first question is:

> **How does the expectation operate now?**

It may influence:

- attention;
- interpretation;
- behaviour;
- partner selection;
- thresholds for threat detection;
- what is requested;
- what is withheld;
- what evidence is noticed;
- how ambiguous behaviour is read.

### Exercise 10: expectation tracker

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What situation activated the expectation?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What emotion appeared?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What prediction did I make?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What relational outcome was anticipated?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What alternative outcome did I seek?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What role did I assign them in the expected pattern?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What current evidence supports the expectation?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What is being imported from expectation rather than established now?

Then ask:

- Did I behave in a way that made the expected response more likely?
- Did I behave in a way that prevented disconfirming evidence?
- Did the other person actually confirm the expectation?
- Was the confirmation partial?
- Does this expectation appear with multiple people or mainly this relationship?
- What evidence would require me to revise it?

A repeated expectation may be formulated through attachment, learning history, emotion schemas, psychoanalytic theories, Jungian complexes, or other frameworks.

Those formulations are not interchangeable.

## 18. Shame

Shame deserves separate attention because it can rapidly alter both self-perception and interpersonal behaviour.

Possible shame-linked responses include:

- hiding;
- appeasing;
- explaining excessively;
- attacking first;
- contempt;
- defensiveness;
- withdrawal;
- refusing help;
- proving competence;
- seeking reassurance;
- attempting to control how one is seen.

Do not assume that every defensive response is shame.

Do not assume that shame is always hidden beneath anger.

The practical questions are:

1. What happened?
2. What aspect of self seemed exposed or diminished?
3. What did I expect the other person to conclude about me?
4. What did I do to prevent or reverse that conclusion?
5. What was the interpersonal consequence?

### Mandatory epistemic separation for shame analysis

1. **WHAT HAPPENED**
2. **WHAT I FELT**
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT**
4. **WHAT I FEARED**
5. **WHAT I WANTED**
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**

Then distinguish:

```text
EXPOSURE
→ SHAME / THREAT TO IDENTITY
→ DESIRED IMAGE OF SELF
→ DEFENSIVE ACTION
→ PARTNER RESPONSE
→ LATER SELF-EVALUATION
```

The useful question is not “What is wrong with me for feeling shame?”

It is:

> “What did I believe was at stake about who I was in the other person's eyes, and what did I then do?”

## 19. Anger

Anger can be:

- an immediate response to obstruction;
- a response to unfairness;
- a defensive response;
- a secondary response following fear, shame, hurt, or sadness;
- a way of restoring agency;
- a protest;
- a learned conflict strategy;
- an appropriate response to a boundary violation.

Do not automatically reinterpret anger as hidden fear.

Track chronology.

### Mandatory epistemic separation for anger analysis

1. **WHAT HAPPENED**
2. **WHAT I FELT**
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT**
4. **WHAT I FEARED**
5. **WHAT I WANTED**
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**

Then ask:

- What occurred immediately before anger?
- Was another emotion present first?
- What action did anger prepare?
- What did anger help me do?
- What did anger prevent me from doing?
- Was there an actual injustice or boundary breach?
- Did intensity exceed the immediate event?

## 20. Fear

Fear often accelerates interpretation.

The person seeks certainty because uncertainty itself has become aversive.

Typical sequences may include:

```text
AMBIGUITY
→ FEAR
→ THREAT INTERPRETATION
→ CERTAINTY-SEEKING
→ REASSURANCE / CHECKING / PROTEST
→ SHORT-TERM RELIEF
→ RENEWED UNCERTAINTY
```

or:

```text
AMBIGUITY
→ FEAR
→ WITHDRAWAL
→ REDUCED EXPOSURE
→ SHORT-TERM RELIEF
→ LESS CORRECTIVE INFORMATION
```

Neither sequence establishes an attachment mechanism by itself.

### Mandatory epistemic separation for fear analysis

1. **WHAT HAPPENED**
2. **WHAT I FELT**
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT**
4. **WHAT I FEARED**
5. **WHAT I WANTED**
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**

Then identify:

- the feared event;
- its estimated imminence;
- evidence it is occurring;
- what behaviour attempts to prevent it;
- whether that behaviour obtains information or merely temporary relief.

## 21. Avoidance

Avoidance should be described behaviourally before it is theorised.

Possible forms include:

- changing subject;
- postponing a conversation;
- not asking;
- withholding a need;
- intellectualising;
- creating physical distance;
- overworking;
- becoming excessively practical;
- ending a conversation abruptly;
- refusing to read or answer a message;
- using humour to deflect;
- sleeping or distracting oneself rather than engaging.

Avoidance can serve many functions.

It may:

- reduce overwhelming arousal;
- protect against shame;
- prevent escalation;
- preserve autonomy;
- avoid anticipated rejection;
- prevent accountability;
- avoid conflict;
- avoid acknowledging incompatibility.

### Mandatory epistemic separation for avoidance analysis

1. **WHAT HAPPENED**
2. **WHAT I FELT**
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT**
4. **WHAT I FEARED**
5. **WHAT I WANTED**
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**

Then ask:

> “What immediate consequence did avoidance produce, and what later cost did it produce?”

The answer distinguishes regulation from a self-perpetuating avoidance strategy.

## 22. Pursuit and reassurance-seeking

Pursuit is not automatically pathological.

Directly seeking contact, clarity, care, or reassurance may be appropriate.

The important distinctions are:

- request versus protest;
- one bid versus repeated escalation;
- information-seeking versus certainty-seeking;
- reassurance that can be received versus reassurance that must immediately be renewed;
- direct expression versus testing.

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED**
2. **WHAT I FELT**
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT**
4. **WHAT I FEARED**
5. **WHAT I WANTED**
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**

Then map:

```text
UNCERTAINTY
→ REQUEST
→ RESPONSE
→ RELIEF?
→ NEW DOUBT?
→ SECOND REQUEST
→ PARTNER RESPONSE
```

If reassurance repeatedly produces only brief relief, one plausible mechanism is that the behaviour is regulating uncertainty rather than obtaining genuinely missing information.

That is a hypothesis to test, not a diagnosis.

## 23. Boundary difficulties

A boundary is not simply discomfort.

A boundary states what a person will:

- consent to;
- participate in;
- disclose;
- accept;
- continue;
- leave;
- do in response to another person's behaviour.

A boundary is most analytically useful when expressed behaviourally.

Compare:

> “You need to stop being disrespectful.”

with:

> “If shouting starts, I will end the conversation and we can revisit it when we can speak without shouting.”

Boundary analysis must remain separate from questions of attachment insecurity or fear of intimacy.

A boundary can be appropriate even if setting it activates anxiety.

Detailed treatment belongs in File 04.

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED**
2. **WHAT I FELT**
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT**
4. **WHAT I FEARED**
5. **WHAT I WANTED**
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**

Then distinguish:

- preference;
- request;
- agreement;
- boundary;
- consequence;
- threat;
- attempt to control the other.

The central self-analysis question is:

> “When this boundary becomes costly, do I still experience myself as able to choose?”

## 24. Repair attempts

Repair attempts are observable behaviours intended to interrupt or recover from relational disruption.

Examples include:

- acknowledging impact;
- apologising;
- correcting a misstatement;
- asking to restart;
- softening tone;
- returning after a pause;
- making a direct request;
- offering comfort;
- accepting responsibility;
- checking whether the other understood;
- changing behaviour.

Do not equate:

- apology with repair;
- understanding with forgiveness;
- forgiveness with reconciliation;
- reconciliation with continuing the relationship;
- positive emotion with resolution.

The attachment-injury literature is especially useful in showing that meaningful repair involves changes in emotional engagement and responsiveness, not merely intellectual agreement.

## 25. Competing explanations

A core rule of the corpus is:

> **Psychological elegance does not make a formulation true.**

For any consequential formulation, generate at least one credible alternative.

Suppose the observation is:

> “After conflict, my partner asks for several hours alone.”

Possible formulations include:

- physiological flooding;
- habitual conflict avoidance;
- attachment deactivation;
- anger;
- shame;
- fear of saying something damaging;
- preference for slower processing;
- strategic withdrawal;
- punishment;
- exhaustion;
- actual loss of investment.

The next task is not to pick the most sophisticated explanation.

It is to identify discriminating evidence.

---

## 26. Discriminating evidence

Discriminating evidence is evidence expected to differ depending on which formulation is correct.

Consider two hypotheses.

### H1: the pause is primarily regulation

Predictions might include:

- the person communicates the need for space;
- gives some indication of return;
- actually returns;
- can engage more constructively after arousal drops;
- does not use silence to demand compliance;
- accepts reciprocal pause rights.

### H2: the pause is primarily punitive withdrawal

Predictions might include:

- timing is contingent on the other person's submission;
- duration is intentionally uncertain;
- attempts at reconnection are ignored to increase distress;
- withdrawal ends when the other concedes;
- the pattern is accompanied by threats, retaliation, or explicit intent to punish.

Real cases can contain both regulation and hostility.

The purpose of discrimination is not to produce artificial binaries. It is to make the formulation accountable to observable differences.

## 27. Behavioural experiments

### 27.1 The governing question

For any working formulation, ask:

> **What could I safely do differently that would generate new evidence?**

A behavioural experiment is not an attempt to trick, provoke, punish, test loyalty, or manufacture jealousy.

It is a transparent or ethically ordinary change in one's own behaviour that allows observation of a pattern under altered conditions.

---

### 27.2 Good experiments

Good experiments are:

- low-risk;
- specific;
- reversible where possible;
- behaviourally defined;
- limited to one's own actions or mutually agreed actions;
- tied to a hypothesis;
- capable of producing disconfirming as well as confirming evidence;
- followed by observation.

Examples:

### Pause-and-return agreement

**Hypothesis:** Escalation is intensified by uncertainty about whether withdrawal is temporary.

**Experiment:** Agree that either person may request a pause but must specify an approximate return point.

**Evidence:** Does reliable return reduce pursuit and escalation?

### Direct request rather than protest

**Hypothesis:** Indirect protest obscures the underlying need.

**Experiment:** Replace “You obviously do not care” with a direct request for contact, reassurance, or clarification.

**Evidence:** Does the interaction become easier to respond to?

### Boundary statement

**Hypothesis:** Resentment is maintained partly because limits are communicated only after they are crossed.

**Experiment:** State one boundary in advance in behavioural terms.

**Evidence:** Is it respected? Does self-respect increase? Does conflict become clearer?

### Delay reassurance-seeking

**Hypothesis:** Repeated reassurance-seeking is partly regulating short-term anxiety rather than obtaining new information.

**Experiment:** After one reasonable request, delay the next request for a preselected period when safe.

**Evidence:** Does distress rise and then fall? Does new information arrive without further checking? Is the feared event actually more likely?

### Ask rather than mind-read

**Hypothesis:** A recurring conflict is intensified by hostile attribution.

**Experiment:** Replace attribution with a neutral clarification question.

**Evidence:** Is the original attribution confirmed, contradicted, or complicated?

### Change one repeated behaviour

**Hypothesis:** My own repeated response is helping maintain the cycle.

**Experiment:** Change only that response while leaving other conditions as similar as practical.

**Evidence:** Does the sequence change downstream?

## 30. Longitudinal change

Longitudinal analysis asks not merely:

> “Did the problem happen again?”

but:

> “Did the structure of the episode change?”

A recurring argument can coexist with progress if:

- activation is noticed earlier;
- escalation is less intense;
- direct requests replace protest;
- withdrawal is time-limited;
- return becomes reliable;
- attribution is checked;
- repair happens sooner;
- boundaries are clearer;
- fewer assumptions are treated as facts;
- both people recover agency more quickly.

Conversely, apparent calm may conceal deterioration if:

- one person has stopped raising needs;
- conflict disappears because of disengagement;
- boundaries are abandoned;
- resentment accumulates;
- emotional safety has decreased;
- one person has become afraid to disagree.

A current formulation is not a reliable prophecy of relationship trajectory.

## 32. Productive reflection versus rumination

### 32.1 Productive reflection

Productive reflection creates movement in the information state.

It should eventually produce one or more of:

- clearer observation;
- better separation of fact and inference;
- a more precise affect sequence;
- a recurring pattern;
- a plausible alternative;
- a testable hypothesis;
- discriminating evidence;
- greater agency;
- a behavioural experiment;
- a boundary;
- a concrete action;
- acceptance of uncertainty.

### 32.2 Rumination

Rumination repeatedly processes material without materially improving any of those outputs.

Common signs include:

- rewriting the same explanation;
- searching for the “real” hidden motive;
- replaying dialogue without new information;
- moving from theory to theory without new observations;
- interpreting every detail as confirmation;
- looking for total certainty before acting;
- analysing another person's psychology instead of deciding one's own action;
- returning to an episode after a decision has already been made without new evidence;
- repeatedly interpreting dreams to answer questions that require waking-life evidence.

### 32.3 The marginal-information test

Ask:

> **What has this additional round of thinking added?**

Possible valid answers:

- a forgotten observation;
- a new alternative;
- a contradiction;
- a test;
- a boundary;
- a decision;
- a new piece of direct communication.

If the answer is:

> “It made the same explanation feel more convincing,”

that is weak evidence of progress.

### 32.4 The closure rule

Reflection can stop when:

1. the observation is clear enough;
2. fact and inference are separated;
3. the main uncertainty is named;
4. plausible alternatives exist;
5. either a test or action is available, or uncertainty must simply be tolerated.

Do not reopen the formulation merely because certainty has not been achieved.

Reopen it when new evidence arrives.

## 33. Theory-shopping

Theory-shopping occurs when a person keeps changing frameworks until one delivers a satisfying explanation.

A delayed reply becomes:

- attachment avoidance;
- then narcissistic injury;
- then projection;
- then an abandonment complex;
- then a trauma response;
- then Lacanian desire;
- then an interpersonal power strategy.

Each lens may contain concepts capable of describing part of the episode.

That does not mean the episode has been explained.

The governing order is:

```text
OBSERVATION
→ PATTERN
→ BROAD MECHANISM
→ OPTIONAL THEORY LENS
```

not:

```text
THEORY
→ SEARCH FOR MATCHING DETAILS
```

A theory earns its place in self-analysis if it does one or more of the following:

- organises repeated observations;
- identifies a distinction otherwise missed;
- generates a competing hypothesis;
- changes what evidence should be sought;
- suggests a safe experiment;
- clarifies responsibility or agency.

If it only adds vocabulary, it is optional.

## 34. Dream work: epistemic position

Dreams deserve serious attention because they are psychologically rich experiences.

They do not deserve privileged factual status.

Contemporary research supports substantial continuity between waking concerns and dream content, while also emphasising methodological limitations. Domhoff's neurocognitive account describes dream content as drawing on many of the same schemas and memory systems as waking thought, with considerable repetition in people, social interaction, misfortune, emotion, and themes.

Domhoff also distinguishes psychological meaningfulness from a claim that dreams have one established function or purpose. In his formulation, dreams can be coherent and continuous with waking concerns without thereby validating a traditional theory of what every dream is “for.”

Long dream series can show considerable regularity, which is one reason series are often more informative than isolated spectacular dreams. Domhoff illustrates this through longitudinal content analysis, including a journal of thousands of dream reports collected over two decades.

Scarpelli and colleagues likewise describe dreaming as related to waking experiences, memory, cognition, and emotion, while emphasising unresolved questions in dream science and the need for further evidence about clinical use.

A fundamental limitation is that the dream itself is not normally directly observable. Researchers generally work from a waking report of the experience, and recall is affected by the state around awakening and other methodological factors.

Therefore:

> **A dream can be psychologically consequential without being literal evidence.**

---

## 35. Dream constitution

This file explicitly rejects the following propositions.

### 35.1 No universal symbol dictionary

There is no warranted rule such as:

- snake = sexuality;
- house = self;
- water = unconscious;
- death = transformation;
- infidelity = hidden betrayal;
- pregnancy = creativity.

A symbol can acquire meaning through:

- personal association;
- waking context;
- cultural context;
- a theory-specific interpretive tradition.

Meaning must not be assigned solely from a dictionary.

### 35.2 Dream does not equal hidden fact

A dream does not establish that an event occurred.

### 35.3 Dream does not prove abuse

Dream material can be explored for fear, memory, bodily sensation, symbolism, continuity with known experience, and personal association.

It is not forensic evidence of abuse.

### 35.4 Dream does not prove infidelity

Dreaming that a partner is unfaithful does not establish that infidelity occurred.

### 35.5 Dream does not provide direct knowledge of another person

A dream figure representing a partner is part of the dreamer's dream experience.

It is not direct access to the partner's mind.

### 35.6 No interpretation is automatically definitive

Multiple meanings may fit.

An interpretation can be:

- useful;
- evocative;
- emotionally resonant;
- theoretically coherent;

without being uniquely true.

Pesant and Zadra explicitly note that there is no evidence that dreams contain a concealed objective meaning waiting to be uncovered, or that particular dream elements have fixed referents. They recommend a constructivist stance in which meaning is treated as collaboratively or reflectively developed for heuristic and pragmatic value rather than discovered as an objective code.

They also warn that dream material can become confused with waking events and that dream work therefore requires particular caution around memory and factual inference.

## 36. The required dream sequence

Every dream analysis in this corpus follows:

> **DREAM REPORT  
> → AFFECT  
> → PERSONAL ASSOCIATIONS  
> → WAKING CONTEXT  
> → CONTINUITY  
> → DISCONTINUITY  
> → MULTIPLE MEANINGS  
> → OPTIONAL THEORY LENS

Do not skip directly from dream report to symbolism.

## 37. Hill's applied framework: Exploration → Insight → Action

Clara Hill's cognitive-experiential method provides the main applied structure for dream work in this corpus.

The method is deliberately collaborative rather than based on an expert handing down “the” interpretation. Hill organises dream work into **exploration, insight, and action**, with the dreamer actively involved in developing associations, meaning, and possible waking-life implications.

Pesant and Zadra describe the exploration stage through the mnemonic **DRAW**:

- **D**escribe;
- **R**e-experience;
- **A**ssociate;
- **W**aking-life triggers.

They describe the insight stage as considering possible meanings at several levels and the action stage as translating useful understanding into possible change.

The model is used here as a disciplined exploration process, not as proof that a dream contains a predetermined instruction.

2. **WHAT I FELT:** Affect in the dream and on waking.
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** Immediate waking interpretation of the dream.
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What the dream made me fear might be true.
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What I hoped the dream meant, disproved, warned about, or resolved.
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** Any conclusion the dream prompted about another person's mind or behaviour.
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What waking evidence exists independently of the dream.
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What the dream cannot establish.

This first step is essential when the dream concerns:

- betrayal;
- sex;
- violence;
- abandonment;
- death;
- another person's intention;
- a disputed memory.

---

### 38.1 DREAM REPORT

Write the dream as remembered.

Do not improve coherence.

Include:

- setting;
- people;
- objects;
- sequence;
- sensory details;
- actions;
- dialogue;
- ending.

Hill often uses retelling in first-person present tense because this can help re-engage the dream experience. It is a method, not a requirement when it feels forced or destabilising.

Record uncertainty honestly:

> “I think we were in my childhood home, but I am not sure.”

Do not fill missing sections.

---

### 38.2 AFFECT

Identify:

- strongest affect in the dream;
- affect at the beginning;
- affect at the ending;
- affect on waking;
- any mismatch between scene and emotion.

The mismatch may be interesting.

For example:

- threatening scene with calm affect;
- ordinary scene with terror;
- betrayal scene with relief;
- reunion scene with disgust.

Do not automatically explain the mismatch.

---

### 38.3 PERSONAL ASSOCIATIONS

For major dream elements ask:

- What comes to mind first?
- Where have I encountered this person, place, object, or situation?
- What qualities does it have for me?
- What feeling accompanies it?
- What recent event does it evoke?
- What older memory does it evoke?
- Does the image have a private meaning that would not be obvious to anyone else?

Hill and Pesant/Zadra place personal association near the centre of responsible dream work precisely because externally supplied meanings are less grounded in the dreamer's own frame of reference.

For a long or confusing dream, it can be useful to begin with the most striking image, person, setting, or affect rather than forcing exhaustive analysis. Pesant and Zadra specifically cite Weiss in connection with this kind of focal selection.

---

### 38.4 WAKING CONTEXT

Review the period around the dream.

Consider:

- events during the previous day;
- emotionally salient events during the preceding week;
- unresolved conversations;
- current decisions;
- relationship uncertainty;
- work stress;
- bodily states;
- media exposure;
- recent memories;
- anniversaries;
- ongoing conflicts;
- anticipated events.

This is not an attempt to reduce the dream to “day residue.”

It establishes the waking context against which possible continuity can be examined.

---

### 38.5 CONTINUITY

Ask:

> **What in the dream resembles my waking concerns, relationships, expectations, emotions, or recurring preoccupations?**

Continuity can occur at several levels.

### Literal continuity

A person or event appears directly.

### Affective continuity

The dream reproduces a familiar feeling.

### Relational continuity

The same interpersonal configuration appears in altered form.

### Thematic continuity

Themes such as:

- being late;
- being excluded;
- being unable to speak;
- being pursued;
- searching;
- losing something;
- needing to protect someone;

may resemble waking concerns.

### Expectational continuity

The dream may dramatise something the dreamer already fears or expects.

Continuity does not mean confirmation.

A dream of betrayal may be continuous with waking fear of betrayal without showing that betrayal exists.

---

### 38.6 DISCONTINUITY

This step prevents over-assimilation.

Ask:

- What does not fit waking reality?
- Which people are combined?
- What is impossible?
- What is exaggerated?
- What role is reversed?
- Which expected person is absent?
- What emotion is surprising?
- What does the dreamer do that they would not normally do?
- What fails to follow normal chronology?

Discontinuity is important because dreams are not simple recordings of waking life.

If every detail is forced into a waking-life explanation, interpretation becomes unfalsifiable.

---

### 38.7 MULTIPLE MEANINGS

Generate at least two plausible readings when interpretation matters.

Possible classes include:

### Waking-concern reading

The dream reflects current concern or emotional preoccupation.

### Memory-association reading

Recent or older memory material has been recombined.

### Relational-pattern reading

The dream resembles a recurring interpersonal expectation.

### Self-state reading

The dream dramatises tension among different intentions, fears, or identities.

### No-stable-meaning reading

The dream is memorable or affectively charged without supporting a coherent interpretation.

### Theory-specific reading

A Jungian, Adlerian, psychoanalytic, or Lacanian lens may add a hypothesis.

Do not select the theory-specific reading merely because it is more interesting.

---

### 38.8 OPTIONAL THEORY LENS

Only after personal associations, waking context, continuity, discontinuity, and multiple meanings have been considered.

Label the lens explicitly.

Examples:

> **Jungian interpretation:** One possible compensatory reading is...

> **Adlerian interpretation:** One possible purposive reading is...

> **Lacanian interpretation:** One possible reading through demand, desire, repetition, or fantasy is...

The lens is an interpretation, not an upgrade from hypothesis to fact.

---

### 38.9 POSSIBLE ACTION

Ask:

> “If this dream has highlighted something useful, what small waking-life action follows?”

Possible outcomes include:

- no action;
- further observation;
- clarify a need;
- ask a question;
- alter one repeated behaviour;
- record future dreams with the same motif;
- make a decision already supported by waking evidence;
- explore the material with a therapist;
- conduct a behavioural experiment.

Dream-based action should be justified in waking life.

Do not:

- accuse a partner of infidelity because of a dream;
- confront someone with a recovered “fact” based on dream imagery;
- make a major irreversible decision solely because a dream felt authoritative;
- obey an imaginal figure literally.

---

## 39. Hill's three stages in detail

### 39.1 Exploration

Exploration asks:

> **What is here?**

Not:

> “What does it secretly mean?”

Hill's exploration process involves returning to the dream experience, identifying feelings, describing major images, associating to them, and considering waking-life triggers.

The strength of the stage is that it slows interpretation.

### Exploration data

Capture:

- dream sequence;
- salient images;
- affect;
- bodily sensations recalled from the dream;
- associations;
- waking links;
- uncertainty.

### Exploration failure mode

The most common failure is premature explanation.

Example:

> “The locked door represents emotional unavailability.”

Return instead to:

- What did the door look like?
- What was happening before you reached it?
- How did you feel?
- What does a locked door personally evoke?
- Where else has that feeling appeared?

The interpretation may later return, but it must earn its place.

---

### 39.2 Insight

Insight asks:

> **What coherent possibilities emerge from the explored material?**

Insight is not the same as discovering a hidden fact.

Pesant and Zadra's constructivist stance is particularly important here. The aim is a meaning that fits the dreamer's experience and proves useful, while remaining open to revision, rather than a claim to have decoded the dream's one objectively correct message.

An insight should ideally connect more than one element.

Weak:

> “The ocean means emotion.”

Stronger:

> “The dream repeatedly shifts from trying to control the boat to being submerged, and my associations to the water concern situations in which I cannot keep a relationship predictable. One possible meaning is that the dream is dramatising my difficulty tolerating uncertainty.”

The second formulation is still provisional.

---

### 39.3 Action

Action asks:

> **What, if anything, should change in waking life because of what I have learned?**

Hill's action stage is one of the most important safeguards against interpretation becoming endless. Pesant and Zadra note that dream work can move from understanding into changes in thought, emotion, behaviour, or further work with the dream.

Possible actions include:

- rehearse a different response;
- make a direct request;
- approach something being avoided;
- tolerate an uncertainty;
- set a boundary;
- acknowledge grief;
- take a decision already supported by waking information;
- continue observation rather than act immediately.

Action is not compulsory.

Sometimes the appropriate result is:

> “The dream clarified a feeling. No behavioural action is justified yet.”

---

## 40. Dream series

A single dream can be striking.

A series is often better evidence of recurrence.

Pesant and Zadra note that repeated dream material can be examined longitudinally, and that changes across a series may be more informative than one especially salient dream.

Domhoff's work similarly emphasises the regularity and repetition that can emerge when many dreams from one person are studied systematically rather than interpreting isolated dreams.

### 40.1 Dream-series log

Track:

- date;
- title;
- dominant affect;
- main people;
- social interactions;
- major setting;
- threat;
- support;
- conflict;
- dreamer's agency;
- ending;
- repeated motif;
- waking concern;
- confidence in interpretation.

Do not assume that every motif is symbolically stable.

A house in one dream may not carry the same meaning in another.

---

### 40.2 What to look for longitudinally

Possible patterns include:

- recurring characters;
- recurring social conflicts;
- pursuit;
- exclusion;
- helplessness;
- rescue;
- secrecy;
- failed communication;
- inability to arrive;
- changing degrees of agency;
- changing endings;
- repeated negative affect;
- increased or decreased capacity to act.

The primary claim should remain descriptive.

Example:

> “Across nine dreams over four months, being unable to reach the destination appeared five times.”

Only then consider interpretation.

---

## 41. Exercise 13: dream-series review

### Mandatory epistemic separation

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What waking events occurred during this period?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** Which dream and waking affects recurred?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What interpretations have I been making?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What have I feared the dreams reveal?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What have I wanted the dreams to tell me?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** What claims about other people have I been tempted to derive?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What waking evidence independently supports those claims?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What cannot be established from the series?

Then distinguish:

### Descriptive recurrence

What actually repeats?

### Waking-life continuity

What waking concerns recur in parallel?

### Change

Do dream responses, affect, agency, or endings change?

### Interpretation

What explanations fit the series?

### Rival interpretation

What else could account for the pattern?

### Action

Does the series justify any waking action beyond continued observation?

## 42. Empirical guardrails for dream interpretation

### 42.1 Dream report is not the dream itself

The available object is usually a waking recollection and report.

That fact limits claims about exact dream content.

### 42.2 Continuity is not literal correspondence

Dream content can reflect waking concerns without serving as a literal replay.

### 42.3 Psychological coherence does not prove a universal function

A coherent dream may still be compatible with multiple theories of why dreaming occurs.

### 42.4 Repetition is more defensible than a one-off symbolic claim

A recurring motif across a series can be described with greater confidence than a one-time symbolic interpretation.

### 42.5 Clinical usefulness is different from factual truth

A dream interpretation may help a person articulate a neglected feeling.

That does not prove that the interpretation describes an external event.

### 42.6 Empirical evidence does not validate all traditional theory

Domhoff's work explicitly argues that systematic dream research does not support many specific propositions of classical clinical dream theories, even while finding psychologically coherent continuity between dreams and waking concerns.

The appropriate stance is therefore neither:

> “Dreams are meaningless noise,”

nor:

> “Dreams reveal hidden truth.”

It is:

> **Dreams are psychologically rich subjective material whose meaning requires cautious, contextual, and theory-aware exploration.**

---

## 43. Jungian dream lens

This section is explicitly **Jungian**.

It is an optional interpretive layer.

It is not presented as established contemporary dream science.

For deeper reconstruction, use the Jungian Research Corpus.

---

### 43.1 Compensation

A Jungian approach may ask whether a dream **compensates** for a one-sided conscious attitude by presenting material that the waking ego is neglecting, excluding, or undervaluing.

This should be phrased as:

> “A Jungian compensatory reading might be...”

not:

> “The dream is compensating for...”

### Practical use

Ask:

- What waking attitude is currently dominant?
- Does the dream present an opposite, neglected, exaggerated, or corrective position?
- Does that alternative position fit the dreamer's associations?
- What would be gained by taking the opposite seriously without automatically obeying it?

A compensatory interpretation should widen reflection, not dictate behaviour.

---

## 44. Jungian amplification

Amplification explores the wider associations around a dream image.

Hall distinguishes personal, cultural, and archetypal amplification.

The method must proceed carefully because theoretical knowledge can easily be projected into the dream. Hall explicitly warns against replacing close attention to dream details with the interpreter's preferred theory, including excessive archetypal amplification.

### 44.1 Personal amplification first

Ask:

- What does this image mean to me?
- Where have I encountered it?
- What emotions accompany it?
- What memories does it evoke?

### 44.2 Cultural amplification second

Ask whether there is a genuinely relevant cultural meaning.

Do not assume cultural meanings are universal.

### 44.3 Archetypal amplification optionally

Only after personal and contextual work.

Hall treats archetypal amplification as specifically Jungian and also states that archetypal interpretation is not required for good dream work.

The practical rule is:

> **Amplification should return the dreamer to the dream, not carry the dreamer away from it.**

If mythology becomes more interesting than the person's actual problem, the interpretation has probably become disproportionate.

---

## 45. Subjective and objective levels

In Jungian dream work, a known dream figure can be considered at two broad levels.

### Objective level

The figure may refer to the actual person or relationship.

### Subjective level

The figure may personify some aspect, attitude, quality, or psychological configuration of the dreamer.

Both can be considered.

Neither should be assumed automatically.

Hall emphasises the importance of preserving both possibilities and keeping the dream embedded in the dreamer's life context.

### Critical corpus safeguard

An objective-level interpretation does not give access to the external person's hidden mind.

Dreaming that one's partner is cold may be relevant to one's lived experience of the relationship.

It does not establish:

> “My partner unconsciously rejects me.”

That proposition requires waking evidence.

---

## 46. Jungian complexes

A Jungian formulation may consider whether an emotionally charged pattern is being constellated as a **complex**.

Operationally, this can be useful when:

- affect intensity seems disproportionate to the immediate event;
- a recurring image cluster appears;
- behaviour feels unusually autonomous or repetitive;
- current experience evokes older relational material.

But:

- complex ≠ attachment internal working model;
- complex activation ≠ attachment-system activation;
- complex ≠ trauma;
- complex ≠ diagnosis.

Use:

> “In a Jungian frame, this may be understood as constellating a complex organised around...”

Do not convert the concept into a claim about historical fact.

---

## 47. Symbolic attitude

A symbolic attitude does not ask:

> “What does the symbol stand for?”

as if solving a substitution cipher.

It asks:

> “What range of psychological meaning is this image carrying that ordinary literal language may not fully capture?”

A symbol can therefore remain partly unresolved.

The discipline is to resist premature closure.

A dream image can simultaneously evoke:

- a personal memory;
- an affect;
- a current relational conflict;
- a cultural association;
- an archetypal parallel.

The existence of multiple levels is not evidence that every level is equally useful.

---

## 48. Active imagination

This section is explicitly **Jungian**.

Active imagination is not ordinary daydreaming and is not a technique for receiving instructions from an external authority.

Joan Chodorow describes active imagination as beginning from material such as an affect, image, impulse, dream, or disturbed state, with attention deliberately turned toward the arising material while an active and self-reflective conscious position is maintained.

Hall likewise emphasises that the ego must maintain a stance toward imaginal contents rather than merely evoke them or become passively absorbed in them.

---

### 48.1 Conditions for use

Use active imagination only when the person can maintain a stable distinction among:

- the observing and participating ego;
- the imaginal figure;
- affect;
- memory;
- fantasy;
- external reality.

Do not use self-directed active imagination when:

- imaginal material is being confused with external events;
- it produces marked disorientation;
- the person cannot stop or disengage;
- affect becomes overwhelming;
- the exercise substantially impairs sleep or ordinary functioning;
- the person begins treating imaginal figures as external authorities.

Hill's self-guided dream-work discussion similarly cautions that emotionally intense material can overwhelm some people and recommends stopping self-guided work when it becomes too difficult or destabilising.

Chodorow's discussion also preserves the danger of identification with unconscious or archetypal material and the importance of strengthening conscious ego functioning when identification becomes excessive.

---

### 48.2 Exercise 14: active imagination protocol

### Mandatory epistemic separation

Before beginning:

1. **WHAT HAPPENED:** What waking event, dream, image, or mood prompted the exercise?
2. **WHAT I FELT:** What affect is present?
3. **WHAT I THOUGHT:** What interpretation do I already have?
4. **WHAT I FEARED:** What am I afraid the image might mean?
5. **WHAT I WANTED:** What am I hoping to learn or change?
6. **WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER:** Am I using the image to make claims about another person?
7. **WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW:** What waking facts remain established independently?
8. **WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN:** What must remain unresolved regardless of the imagery?

### Step 1: choose a starting point

Use one:

- dream image;
- affect;
- bodily felt image;
- spontaneous fantasy;
- recurring symbolic figure.

### Step 2: establish differentiation

State explicitly:

> “This is imaginal material. I am engaging with it psychologically. It is not an external person or external instruction.”

### Step 3: allow the image to develop

Observe:

- movement;
- speech;
- changes in setting;
- affect;
- impulses.

Do not force the narrative toward a preferred conclusion.

### Step 4: participate

The ego is not absent.

It may:

- ask a question;
- respond;
- refuse;
- negotiate;
- express anger;
- set a limit;
- inquire about the figure;
- act within the imaginal scene.

The goal is dialogue, not surrender.

### Step 5: maintain ego/image distinction

If an imaginal figure says:

> “Leave your relationship tomorrow,”

record it as:

> “The imaginal figure said I should leave.”

Do not transform it into:

> “My unconscious has ordered me to leave.”

### Step 6: record

After the sequence, record:

- images;
- dialogue;
- affect;
- changes;
- ending.

Chodorow notes Jung's use of written, visual, bodily, dramatic, and other expressive forms, and the value of giving unconscious material a form before immediately reducing it to an intellectual explanation.

### Step 7: close the exercise

End deliberately.

Return attention to:

- current place;
- current date;
- external environment;
- waking obligations.

### Step 8: interpret later

Apply the ordinary corpus sequence:

```text
OBSERVATION OF IMAGINAL MATERIAL
→ AFFECT
→ ASSOCIATION
→ WAKING CONTEXT
→ MULTIPLE MEANINGS
→ OPTIONAL JUNGIAN FORMULATION
→ POSSIBLE ACTION
```

### Step 9: integration

Ask:

- What new perspective appeared?
- What conflict became clearer?
- What quality did the figure represent?
- What remains symbolic?
- What waking evidence supports any proposed action?
- Can the insight be tested safely?

---

### 48.3 Stopping conditions

Stop active imagination if:

- the difference between imagination and external reality becomes uncertain;
- the exercise feels compulsory rather than chosen;
- the material becomes overwhelming;
- the person feels unable to end the scene;
- commands are experienced as requiring literal obedience;
- self-harm, violence, or dangerous conduct is being treated as symbolically required;
- the exercise is intensifying rather than clarifying destabilisation.

In those circumstances, do not deepen the symbolic exercise.

---

## 49. Adlerian bridge

This section is explicitly **Adlerian** and is optional.

Adler's psychology gives dreams a purposive or future-oriented formulation. In his own account, dreams can be understood in relation to an anticipated problem and the person's broader style of approaching life's tasks. He also explicitly rejects the idea that dreams are prophetic access to unknowable future facts.

For this corpus, the useful Adlerian question is:

> **What problem, decision, conflict, or anticipated situation might this dream be rehearsing my attitude toward?**

This is a hypothesis-generating question.

It does not establish that every dream has a purposive function.

### Practical Adlerian questions

- What challenge am I anticipating?
- What position do I take in the dream?
- Do I approach, avoid, dominate, submit, postpone, rescue, compete, or withdraw?
- Does this resemble my waking style?
- What outcome does the dreamer's strategy appear organised toward?
- What would a different response look like?

The Adlerian lens is especially useful when a dream repeatedly concerns:

- preparation;
- failure;
- competition;
- avoidance;
- mastery;
- superiority;
- inferiority;
- anticipated confrontation.

Do not use Adler's historical theory as empirical proof of why a modern dream occurred.

---

## 50. Lacanian bridge

This section is intentionally brief.

Use the dedicated Lacanian corpus for serious theoretical work.

Bruce Fink's clinical introduction explicitly organises much of its early clinical discussion around desire, the analytic relationship, interpretation, and the dialectic of desire rather than treating psychoanalytic work as simple symptom decoding.

Four concepts can function as limited reflective lenses here.

---

### 50.1 Desire

Lacanian **desire** is not simply conscious wanting.

A practical question is:

> “What remains in motion even after the explicit request is answered?”

Example:

A person repeatedly asks for reassurance.

The stated request may be:

> “Tell me you still love me.”

If reassurance repeatedly fails to settle the issue, a Lacanian lens may ask what broader position in the other's desire is being sought.

Do not reduce this immediately to attachment insecurity.

The frameworks are not equivalent.

---

### 50.2 Demand

A **demand** is not simply an ordinary request in Lacanian theory.

For limited self-reflection, ask:

> “What else is being addressed through this request?”

A request for a text message may simultaneously concern:

- recognition;
- presence;
- love;
- priority;
- proof of being wanted.

This does not make the ordinary request illegitimate.

It simply distinguishes the practical content of the request from the broader relational address that may accompany it.

---

### 50.3 Repetition

A Lacanian lens can ask:

> “What relational scene keeps being reproduced even when I consciously say I want something different?”

Examples:

- repeatedly pursuing unavailable partners;
- repeatedly demanding certainty and then distrusting reassurance;
- repeatedly creating tests that make disappointment likely;
- repeatedly occupying the rescuer, rejected person, rival, or indispensable person.

The purpose is not to assign a diagnostic structure.

It is to notice repetition that ordinary intention does not fully explain.

---

### 50.4 Fantasy

Lacanian **fantasy** is not ordinary imagination.

For limited reflective use, it can be approached as a recurring structure through which a person anticipates what they are for the other and what the other wants.

Questions include:

- What role do I repeatedly expect to occupy?
- What must the other person be like for this scene to work?
- What do I expect will happen if I cease occupying that position?
- What evidence actually supports the scenario?

Fantasy remains a theory-specific interpretation.

It is not factual knowledge of the partner.

---

## 51. Dream and relationship questions: hard boundaries

When dreams concern a partner, apply the following rules.

### Dream of infidelity

Allowed inference:

> “Infidelity, loss, rivalry, or uncertainty is psychologically salient in the dream.”

Not allowed:

> “The dream proves my partner is unfaithful.”

### Dream of abandonment

Allowed:

> “Abandonment or separation is affectively salient.”

Not allowed:

> “My partner is preparing to leave.”

### Dream of violence

Allowed:

> “Fear, aggression, vulnerability, helplessness, or conflict may require exploration.”

Not allowed:

> “The dream proves a hidden violent intention in another person.”

### Dream of an ex-partner

Allowed:

> “This figure may connect with memory, unresolved feeling, a current relational comparison, a self-state, or another association.”

Not allowed:

> “The dream proves I should reunite with them.”

### Sexual dream

Allowed:

> “Sexuality, attraction, bodily arousal, intimacy, conflict, memory, or symbolic associations may be relevant.”

Not allowed:

> “The dream establishes my true sexual intention or demands action.”

Dreams are material for formulation.

Waking decisions require waking evidence.

## 52. Worked example 1: delayed reply

### Initial episode

A message is sent at 09:00.

At 12:00 there has been no reply.

The person feels increasingly distressed and sends three additional messages.

The partner replies at 13:20 saying they had been in meetings and feels pressured by the repeated messages.

### Epistemic separation

**WHAT HAPPENED**  
One message at 09:00. No reply by 12:00. Three additional messages were sent. Reply at 13:20 referred to meetings and feeling pressured.

**WHAT I FELT**  
Fear, then anger.

**WHAT I THOUGHT**  
“Something is wrong.”  
“I am being ignored.”

**WHAT I FEARED**  
Loss of interest or rejection.

**WHAT I WANTED**  
Reassurance and predictable contact.

**WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**  
“They are deliberately withholding a response.”

**WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**  
No reply occurred for 4 hours 20 minutes. The partner later reported being in meetings.

**WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**  
The exact reason for every minute of delay, the partner's full internal experience, and whether this episode reflects a larger change in investment.

### Episode formulation

```text
DELAY
→ FEAR
→ REJECTION INTERPRETATION
→ URGE TO RESTORE CONTACT
→ REPEATED MESSAGES
→ PARTNER FEELS PRESSURED
→ PARTNER DISTANCES
→ DISTANCE APPEARS TO CONFIRM ORIGINAL FEAR
```

### Plausible mechanisms

1. attachment hyperactivation;
2. learned expectation that delayed contact predicts rejection;
3. genuine recent instability in the relationship;
4. intolerance of ambiguous communication;
5. mismatch in expectations about response time;
6. current external stress increasing sensitivity.

### Discriminating evidence

- Does this occur with other partners or mainly this one?
- Have delayed replies actually preceded relational withdrawal before?
- Does a clear communication agreement reduce the reaction?
- Does one direct reassurance request work better than repeated checking?
- Is the partner otherwise consistently responsive?

### Experiment

Agree on realistic expectations about availability during work hours.

When the next comparable delay occurs:

- send no additional message unless practical need requires it;
- record the fear trajectory;
- observe whether contact resumes without pursuit;
- compare the result.

### Follow-up

If reliable return repeatedly reduces activation, uncertainty itself may be an important mechanism.

If the partner repeatedly violates explicit agreements, disappears unpredictably, or dismisses reasonable needs, the formulation changes.

Self-analysis must not use “my attachment anxiety” to erase actual unreliability.

---

## 53. Worked example 2: conflict withdrawal

### Episode

During an argument, one partner says:

> “I cannot do this right now.”

They leave the room.

The other follows and continues the discussion.

The argument escalates.

### Epistemic separation

**WHAT HAPPENED**  
One person stated they could not continue and left. The other followed and continued speaking. Voices rose.

**WHAT I FELT**  
Fear, frustration, anger.

**WHAT I THOUGHT**  
“If I let them leave, this will never be resolved.”

**WHAT I FEARED**  
Abandonment, avoidance, permanent non-resolution.

**WHAT I WANTED**  
Completion of the conversation and assurance of return.

**WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**  
“They are running away because they do not care.”

**WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**  
They asked to stop the conversation in that moment. It is not yet established whether they intended to return.

**WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**  
Whether the withdrawal reflected flooding, avoidance, anger, punishment, shame, processing style, or some combination.

### Hypotheses

**H1: flooding/regulation**  
A temporary pause could improve later engagement.

**H2: conflict avoidance**  
The pause becomes indefinite and prevents resolution.

**H3: punitive withdrawal**  
Uncertainty is intentionally used to compel or distress the other.

### Experiment

Create a pause-and-return agreement:

> Either person can pause, but the person requesting the pause proposes a return time.

### Evidence

If reliable return leads to lower escalation and better discussion, H1 gains support.

If return repeatedly fails despite explicit agreement, H2 or another explanation gains support.

If pauses are conditional on submission, accompanied by retaliation, or used coercively, the problem should not be reduced to regulation.

---

## 54. Worked example 3: dream of infidelity

### Dream report

The dreamer enters a hotel and sees their partner kissing another person.

The dreamer cannot speak.

The partner walks away laughing.

The dreamer wakes in panic.

### Epistemic separation

**WHAT HAPPENED**  
A dream of infidelity occurred. No waking evidence has yet been identified.

**WHAT I FELT**  
Panic, humiliation, anger.

**WHAT I THOUGHT**  
“Maybe I know something without knowing it.”

**WHAT I FEARED**  
Actual betrayal.

**WHAT I WANTED**  
Certainty and reassurance.

**WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**  
“The dream may be revealing that my partner is unfaithful.”

**WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**  
The dream occurred. The dreamer has recently felt less connected to the partner.

**WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**  
Whether any betrayal exists. The dream supplies no factual evidence of it.

### Personal associations

**Hotel:** temporary, anonymous, travel, secrecy.

**Other person:** resembles a former colleague.

**Unable to speak:** recalls previous arguments in which the dreamer felt unable to explain hurt.

**Laughter:** humiliation.

### Waking context

The couple has had less time together because of work travel.

No independent evidence of infidelity is identified.

### Continuity

The dream strongly resembles waking concerns about distance, exclusion, and inability to obtain reassurance.

### Discontinuity

The hotel is unfamiliar.

The third person is a composite.

The partner's behaviour is unlike their usual waking behaviour.

### Multiple meanings

1. current fear of emotional disconnection;
2. older fear of humiliation activated by temporary distance;
3. concern about being unable to voice relational needs;
4. memory recombination without one stable deeper meaning.

### Optional Jungian lens

A Jungian subjective-level reading could ask whether the unfaithful partner figure dramatises a part of the dreamer's own psyche that feels unavailable, divided, or invested elsewhere.

A Jungian objective-level reading could consider the actual relationship, but it still cannot prove anything about the partner's behaviour.

### Optional Lacanian lens

A Lacanian reading could ask how the dream stages uncertainty about the partner's desire and the dreamer's feared position within it.

It does not answer what the partner actually desires.

### Action

No accusation follows from the dream.

A waking-life action may be:

> “I have noticed that the recent distance is making me feel insecure. Could we talk about how connected we both feel and what contact we need while work is busy?”

The action is justified by the waking experience of disconnection, not by treating the dream as evidence of infidelity.

---

## 55. Worked example 4: reassurance that never settles

### Pattern

A person repeatedly asks:

> “Are we okay?”

The partner says yes.

Relief lasts briefly.

A change in tone then triggers the same question.

### Epistemic separation

**WHAT HAPPENED**  
Repeated reassurance was requested and given.

**WHAT I FELT**  
Fear and temporary relief.

**WHAT I THOUGHT**  
“If I need to ask again, the reassurance must not have been real.”

**WHAT I FEARED**  
Hidden rejection.

**WHAT I WANTED**  
Durable certainty.

**WHAT I INFERRED ABOUT THE OTHER**  
“They may be saying yes only to calm me.”

**WHAT I ACTUALLY KNOW**  
The partner has repeatedly stated that the relationship is okay.

**WHAT REMAINS UNKNOWN**  
No reassurance can establish another person's future state permanently.

### Competing formulations

1. reassurance-seeking is being negatively reinforced by short-term anxiety reduction;
2. the partner's behaviour is genuinely inconsistent enough that verbal reassurance has low credibility;
3. previous betrayal has not been adequately repaired;
4. the relationship lacks agreed behavioural markers of reliability;
5. attachment hyperactivation contributes;
6. the request carries a broader need for recognition that verbal reassurance cannot permanently settle.

### Discriminating evidence

Do words and behaviour align?

Does delayed reassurance-seeking change distress?

Does more behavioural reliability reduce checking?

Has there been an unresolved betrayal?

Does the pattern occur even in demonstrably stable relationships?

### Experiment

After one direct request and answer, delay repeat checking and observe:

- anxiety trajectory;
- new objective evidence;
- whether feared deterioration actually occurs;
- whether the partner behaves consistently.

The experiment is inappropriate if reassurance is being sought because of active deception or a current safety concern.

---

## 56. Formulation without diagnosis

A formulation describes a process.

A diagnosis classifies a disorder.

This file is concerned with the former.

Prefer:

> “During high ambiguity I become vigilant, interpret neutral cues pessimistically, and increase reassurance-seeking.”

over:

> “I have X disorder.”

Prefer:

> “My partner repeatedly stops responding for days after disagreement.”

over:

> “My partner is narcissistic.”

Prefer:

> “This appears to be a pursuit-withdrawal cycle.”

over:

> “I am anxious and they are avoidant.”

Even when a diagnostic category is relevant in professional care, the episode-level formulation remains useful.

---

## 57. The uncertainty register

Every substantial self-analysis should end with an uncertainty register.

Use four categories.

### Known

Supported by observation or direct communication.

### Probable

Supported by repeated evidence, but not certain.

### Possible

Plausible formulation with limited evidence.

### Unknown

Not currently resolvable.

Example:

**Known**
- arguments often escalate when no return time is agreed.

**Probable**
- predictable reconnection reduces my pursuit.

**Possible**
- attachment activation contributes to my response.

**Unknown**
- how much of my partner's withdrawal reflects flooding versus avoidance.

The uncertainty register prevents interpretation from silently hardening into fact.

---

## 58. The evidence ledger

For important recurring patterns, maintain a simple evidence ledger.

| Claim | Evidence for | Evidence against | Evidence status | Next useful evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| “I become more distressed when return is uncertain.” | Episodes 2, 4, 7 | Episode 5 | Recurring pattern | Compare future pauses with explicit return time |
| “Partner uses withdrawal to punish.” | Episode 3 | Episodes 1, 5, 8 | Possible, weak | Ask intent; observe contingency on compliance |
| “Direct requests work better than protest.” | Episodes 6, 8 | Episode 9 | Emerging | Repeat in comparable lower-risk contexts |

This is a better antidote to self-confirmation than simply trying to “be objective.”

---

## 59. Prediction discipline

Self-analysis should not be used to produce confident forecasts such as:

- “This relationship will fail.”
- “They will eventually cheat.”
- “Avoidants always come back.”
- “Once I heal my attachment, this will work.”
- “This dream predicts what is going to happen.”

Prediction requires stronger evidence than formulation.

The proper output is often conditional:

> “If the current pattern continues without reliable repair, this specific problem is likely to remain relevant.”

That is different from predicting the fate of the whole relationship.

---

## 60. When self-analysis itself becomes defensive

Reflection can be used to avoid action.

Examples:

- continuing to analyse whether a boundary is “really justified” instead of stating it;
- theorising about why another person lies instead of deciding what repeated lying means for trust;
- explaining pursuit without ever making a direct request;
- interpreting dreams instead of asking the waking question;
- tracing childhood origins instead of changing current behaviour;
- debating whether anger is primary or secondary after the practical issue is already clear;
- continuing to seek deeper meaning because the available answer is emotionally unsatisfying.

Use this test:

> **If I stopped analysing now, what action or uncertainty would I have to face?**

The answer may reveal why analysis is continuing.

---

## 61. Reflective depth should be earned by evidence

Use the least inferential explanation that adequately organises the case.

A hierarchy:

### Level 1: concrete event

> “The agreement was broken.”

### Level 2: subjective experience

> “I experienced it as evidence I could not rely on the agreement.”

### Level 3: recurring pattern

> “Broken agreements repeatedly produce rapid mistrust and protest.”

### Level 4: formulation

> “Threat sensitivity around reliability may be contributing.”

### Level 5: theory-specific interpretation

> “A Jungian reading might describe a constellated complex around betrayal.”

Do not move to Level 5 because it sounds deeper.

Move there only if it adds something useful while retaining the earlier levels.

## 64. Reflection on ordinary relationship life

Not every useful observation concerns conflict.

A good corpus should also track what supports connection.

Possible observations include:

- spontaneous affection;
- humour;
- shared routines;
- practical support;
- time together;
- sexual connection;
- reliable follow-through;
- bids for attention;
- responsiveness;
- ordinary acts of care;
- capacity to enjoy separateness;
- mutually respected autonomy.

This matters because an analysis based only on conflict records will generate a biased picture of the relationship.

The baseline question is not:

> “What is wrong?”

but:

> “What happens repeatedly, under what conditions, with what consequences?”

---

## 65. Positive episodes should also be reconstructed

Positive experiences can reveal mechanisms that conflict analysis misses.

Example:

> “We had a difficult conversation without escalation.”

Analyse:

- what was different;
- timing;
- context;
- level of fatigue;
- how the conversation opened;
- directness of requests;
- physiological activation;
- tone;
- whether either person checked an assumption;
- how disagreement was tolerated;
- whether repair was needed;
- what allowed it to work.

Do not assume improvement occurred because someone “felt secure.”

Identify the changed behaviour.

---

## 66. Context before personality

Before explaining an episode through a deep psychological construct, examine:

- sleep;
- workload;
- caregiving demands;
- money;
- illness;
- sexual stress;
- family pressure;
- travel;
- alcohol or other substances;
- time constraints;
- recent loss;
- housing;
- social isolation;
- cultural expectations.

External stress is a core relationship variable in relationship science, not background noise.

A highly stressed relationship may reveal vulnerabilities, but it can also be worsened directly by conditions that require practical rather than interpretive solutions.

---

## 67. The “what changed?” rule

When a pattern suddenly intensifies, ask:

> **What changed before deciding that personality changed?**

Potential changes:

- work hours;
- illness;
- contact frequency;
- sexual activity;
- financial stress;
- trust;
- external friendships;
- caregiving;
- sleep;
- alcohol;
- recent betrayal;
- unresolved conflict;
- new commitment;
- increased dependency;
- impending separation;
- changed boundaries.

Temporal sequence matters.

---

## 68. Memory discipline

Self-analysis often uses memory as though it were a recording.

It is not.

When reconstructing older episodes:

- distinguish exact recall from general impression;
- identify written records where available;
- avoid adding current theory to old events;
- note when a partner's account differs;
- avoid converting dream imagery into remembered waking fact;
- do not infer missing childhood events from current symptoms or relationship patterns.

A later formulation can make an old event newly meaningful without making new factual details known.

---

## 69. The difference between explanation and responsibility

A formulation can explain why a behaviour was likely without excusing it.

Examples:

> “I pursued because I was frightened.”

may be true.

It does not imply:

> “Therefore repeated harassment was acceptable.”

Likewise:

> “My partner withdrew because they were overwhelmed.”

does not imply:

> “Therefore disappearing for days after an explicit agreement was harmless.”

Always distinguish:

- mechanism;
- intention;
- impact;
- responsibility;
- repair.

This is especially important when psychological theory produces empathy faster than it produces accountability.

---

## 70. Limits of behavioural experiments

Do not use behavioural experiments when the proposed test would:

- provoke jealousy deliberately;
- threaten abandonment;
- withdraw affection as punishment;
- deceive;
- expose private information;
- violate consent;
- create physical danger;
- test whether an abusive person becomes violent;
- escalate coercive dynamics;
- use sex as leverage;
- endanger housing, finances, employment, or children.

A behavioural experiment is for generating information safely, not forcing the relationship into a crisis.

---

## 71. Safety override

Where credible evidence suggests:

- coercive control;
- physical violence;
- sexual coercion;
- stalking;
- credible threats;
- severe intimidation;
- fear of retaliation;
- unsafe disclosure conditions;

do not use ordinary couple-cycle exercises as though the central problem were reciprocal misattunement.

Do not recommend greater vulnerability to an unsafe partner.

Do not use attachment, mentalization, projection, dream interpretation, or relationship systems theory to obscure concrete harm.

Route to File 04 and appropriate professional or specialist support.

---

## 72. Self-analysis and therapy

Self-analysis can improve therapy when it produces:

- clearer episode records;
- more precise questions;
- longitudinal evidence;
- competing formulations;
- behavioural outcomes;
- dream series;
- uncertainty that can be explored collaboratively.

It becomes less useful when it is used to pre-empt therapy by constructing a total explanation before another mind enters the process.

Hill's model explicitly treats dream work as collaborative and cautions that it should not be forced on people or used without regard to capacity, preference, and the intensity of material.

The same principle generalises well to reflective work:

> **A method should serve the problem, not become another demand to analyse everything.**

---

## 73. When to stop an episode analysis

Stop when one of the following is true.

### Sufficient clarity

The facts, experience, and next action are clear enough.

### A test is available

Further interpretation will not improve the test.

### A boundary is already indicated

No deeper theory is needed to justify it.

### Responsibility is clear

Understanding motive will not change what must be addressed.

### Evidence is unavailable

The remaining question depends on another person's mind or future behaviour.

### The material is becoming repetitive

No new evidence, alternative, or action is emerging.

### The material requires professional containment

Self-guided work is producing overwhelming affect or destabilisation.

The purpose of analysis is not to exhaust meaning.

---

## 74. The one-episode, one-hypothesis, one-test rule

When reflection becomes sprawling, reduce scope.

Select:

1. one episode;
2. one main working hypothesis;
3. one serious alternative;
4. one discriminating observation;
5. one safe experiment or action.

This prevents a single argument from becoming an inquiry into:

- attachment;
- childhood;
- Jungian complexes;
- shadow;
- trauma;
- Lacanian fantasy;
- personality;
- family systems;
- sexuality;
- power;

all at once.

Complexity can be added later if the evidence requires it.

---

## 75. The agency end-point

The highest-value output of self-analysis is often not explanatory certainty.

It is a shift from:

> “What does this say about me or them?”

to:

> “Given what I know, what do I want to do?”

That question may produce:

- a request;
- a pause;
- an apology;
- a limit;
- an experiment;
- a conversation;
- acceptance;
- continued observation;
- a decision not to act yet;
- a decision to end a repeated behaviour.

Agency requires tolerating the possibility that no theory will remove all uncertainty first.

---

## 78. Quality-control checklist

Before accepting a self-analysis as useful, ask:

### Observation

Have I separated what happened from what I think it meant?

### Affect

Have I named emotion without turning it into evidence of external fact?

### Body

Have I distinguished bodily activation from interpretation?

### Other mind

Have I marked inferences about the other person as inferences?

### Pattern

Do I have repeated observations or only one vivid episode?

### Context

Have I checked ordinary contextual explanations?

### Mechanism

Is the formulation plausible rather than asserted?

### Alternatives

Have I generated a serious competing explanation?

### Theory

Have I labelled Jungian, Adlerian, psychoanalytic, or Lacanian readings explicitly?

### Responsibility

Have I avoided using explanation to erase accountability?

### Safety

Have I checked for coercion, intimidation, violence, or unsafe disclosure?

### Experiment

Can any important claim be tested safely?

### Outcome

Have I tracked what happened after the change?

### Rumination

Is further thinking likely to add information?

### Uncertainty

Have I allowed anything to remain unknown?

## 79. Dream-work quality-control checklist

Before accepting a dream interpretation, ask:

1. Did I record the dream before interpreting it?
2. Did I identify affect?
3. Did I generate personal associations?
4. Did I examine waking context?
5. Did I look for continuity?
6. Did I also look for discontinuity?
7. Did I generate multiple meanings?
8. Did I label any theory-specific lens?
9. Did I avoid universal symbol dictionaries?
10. Did I avoid treating the dream as factual revelation?
11. Did I avoid making claims about another person's hidden mind?
12. Did I avoid treating emotional resonance as proof?
13. If the dream concerns abuse, infidelity, betrayal, or danger, did I separate dream material from waking evidence?
14. Does any proposed action make sense independently in waking life?
15. Would no action or continued observation be more appropriate?

If any answer is no, interpretation is incomplete.

## 80. Source architecture and evidentiary status

### 80.1 Clara E. Hill

**Role:** principal applied dream-work framework.

Use for:

- exploration;
- insight;
- action;
- personal association;
- collaboration;
- translating reflection into change;
- self-guided dream work with caution.

Hill's model deliberately integrates experiential, cognitive, psychodynamic, and behavioural elements into a staged process rather than presenting one school as possessing the true interpretation.

**Evidence status:** structured clinical model with an empirical research programme.

Do not infer that empirical support for the usefulness of the process proves any particular dream interpretation.

---

### 80.2 Pesant and Zadra

**Role:** principal cross-theoretical clinical guardrail.

Use for:

- constructivist interpretation;
- collaboration;
- safety;
- description;
- associations;
- interpretation levels;
- action;
- dream series;
- limits of expert interpretation.

Their review explicitly separates therapeutic usefulness from claims that dreams contain one predetermined objective meaning.

**Evidence status:** clinical and empirical review.

---

### 80.3 Domhoff

**Role:** empirical and methodological constraint.

Use for:

- continuity;
- content analysis;
- repetition;
- longitudinal dream series;
- dream-report methodology;
- critique of unsupported traditional claims.

Domhoff's work is especially important because it allows a middle position: dream content can be psychologically coherent and related to waking concerns without validating a universal symbolic or hidden-content theory.

**Evidence status:** empirical neurocognitive and content-analysis tradition.

---

### 80.4 Scarpelli, Alfonsi, Gorgoni, and De Gennaro

**Role:** contemporary dream-science guardrail.

Use for:

- current neuroscientific uncertainty;
- rapid eye movement and non-rapid eye movement dreaming;
- dream recall;
- continuity between waking and dreaming;
- memory;
- emotional processing;
- methodological limits;
- open clinical questions.

Their 2022 review emphasises that direct access to dream experience remains an intrinsic methodological limitation and that the clinical value of dreams still requires further clarification.

**Evidence status:** contemporary scientific review.

---

### 80.5 Lillie Weiss

**Role:** selective clinical technique support.

Use for:

- focal points;
- recurring patterns;
- concentrating on salient dream elements;
- movement from detailed material toward a clinically useful focus.

Do not allow Weiss's interpretive assumptions to override the empirical guardrails above.

---

### 80.6 Pace-Schott, Solms, Blagrove, Harnad et al.

**Role:** selective scientific and historical context.

The volume documents the movement away from simplistic identification of dreaming with rapid eye movement sleep and contains competing neuroscientific views about dreaming, sleep stages, memory, and function.

Use it to preserve scientific pluralism.

Do not use it to settle clinical dream meaning.

---

### 80.7 James A. Hall

**Role:** Jungian interpretive technique.

Use for:

- compensation;
- context;
- amplification;
- objective and subjective levels;
- complexes;
- dream series;
- imaginal work.

Hall's own technical discussion warns against imposing theory on insufficiently described dream material and against excessive archetypal amplification.

**Evidence status:** Jungian clinical and interpretive tradition.

---

### 80.8 Joan Chodorow

**Role:** Jungian active imagination.

Use for:

- turning toward affect or image;
- symbolic engagement;
- dialogue;
- expressive forms;
- maintaining a conscious reflective position;
- integration;
- dangers of identification.

**Evidence status:** Jungian clinical and interpretive tradition.

---

### 80.9 Alfred Adler

**Role:** limited purposive or lifestyle lens.

Use for:

- anticipated problems;
- future-oriented stance;
- recurring style of coping;
- dreamer's position toward challenge.

Do not present Adler's theory of dream purpose as established dream science.

---

### 80.10 Bruce Fink

**Role:** limited Lacanian bridge.

Use only for:

- desire;
- demand;
- repetition;
- fantasy;
- the distinction between analytic interpretation and ordinary conscious explanation.

Do not convert this file into a Lacanian corpus.

Do not translate Lacanian terms into attachment terms.

## 81. Hierarchy for dream claims

When a dream generates a psychologically consequential claim, use the following hierarchy.

### Strongest: direct waking observation

> “We have spent less time together during the last month.”

### Next: waking subjective report

> “I feel less connected.”

### Next: recurring waking pattern

> “Periods of reduced contact repeatedly increase my fear.”

### Next: dream continuity

> “Several dreams during these periods involve exclusion.”

### Next: theoretical interpretation

> “A Jungian reading might understand the exclusion motif as compensatory.”

The hierarchy must not be reversed.

Do not use the theoretical interpretation to “prove” the waking claim.

---

## 82. Hierarchy for self-analysis claims

Similarly:

```text
OBSERVATION
>
REPEATED OBSERVATION
>
SUBJECTIVE PATTERN
>
BROAD FORMULATION
>
THEORY-SPECIFIC INTERPRETATION
```

This is not a ranking of psychological importance.

It is a ranking of distance from direct evidence.

A theory-specific interpretation may be personally profound while remaining less epistemically secure than a recorded event.

## 83. Core anti-error rules

### Rule 1

Strong feeling is not strong external evidence.

### Rule 2

Repeated behaviour is more informative than one dramatic episode.

### Rule 3

Repeated behaviour still does not reveal motive automatically.

### Rule 4

A theory-specific concept is not a diagnosis.

### Rule 5

A dream is not a witness.

### Rule 6

A partner's mind remains partly opaque.

### Rule 7

A plausible mechanism must compete with alternatives.

### Rule 8

Explanation does not erase responsibility.

### Rule 9

A behavioural experiment should generate evidence, not compliance.

### Rule 10

If reflection produces no new observation, alternative, test, action, or acceptance of uncertainty, stop.

## 84. Core method in one page

```text
1. RECORD THE EVENT
   What happened?

2. SEPARATE EXPERIENCE
   What did I feel?
   What happened in my body?
   What did I think?
   What did I fear?
   What did I want?

3. SEPARATE OTHER-MIND INFERENCE
   What did I infer about the other?
   What do I actually know?
   What remains unknown?

4. MAP RESPONSE
   What did I feel pulled to do?
   What did I actually do?
   What did the other actually do next?
   What happened to the relationship?

5. CHECK RECURRENCE
   Has this sequence happened before?

6. FORMULATE
   What broad mechanisms fit?

7. COMPETE
   What serious alternative explanations fit?

8. DISCRIMINATE
   What evidence would differ across explanations?

9. TEST
   What could I safely do differently that would generate new evidence?

10. ACT
    Make the smallest useful change.

11. FOLLOW UP
    What happened?

12. REVISE
    What does the new evidence change?

13. STOP
    If no new evidence is available, tolerate the remaining uncertainty.
```

## 85. Final operating principles

1. **Observation before interpretation.**
2. **Experience is valid data but not automatic external fact.**
3. **Separate what happened from what was inferred.**
4. **Preserve the opacity of another mind.**
5. **Track episodes before assigning traits.**
6. **Track patterns before invoking deep theory.**
7. **Attachment strategies are contextual processes, not fixed identities.**
8. **Mentalization should increase uncertainty tolerance, not sophisticated mind-reading.**
9. **Emotion should be tracked through body, appraisal, action tendency, behaviour, and consequence.**
10. **Agency is measured by the capacity to choose under activation, not by emotional detachment.**
11. **A formulation is a hypothesis.**
12. **A consequential formulation needs a credible alternative.**
13. **Useful hypotheses generate discriminating evidence.**
14. **Behavioural experiments should be safe, specific, and ethically ordinary.**
15. **Outcome tracking matters more than interpretive confidence.**
16. **A recurring cycle does not imply equal responsibility.**
17. **Explanation does not exonerate harmful behaviour.**
18. **Boundaries do not require a diagnostic justification.**
19. **Repair requires observable change, not only insight.**
20. **Longitudinal change should be assessed through the structure of episodes, not only whether conflict recurs.**
21. **Dreams are psychologically meaningful material, not privileged factual revelation.**
22. **Dream reports require attention to affect, personal association, waking context, continuity, and discontinuity.**
23. **No universal symbol dictionary governs dream interpretation.**
24. **Dreams do not prove abuse, infidelity, another person's motive, or future events.**
25. **Hill's exploration → insight → action sequence is the principal applied dream-work structure.**
26. **Pesant and Zadra's collaborative, constructivist stance constrains claims of definitive interpretation.**
27. **Domhoff and contemporary dream science constrain traditional theoretical claims.**
28. **Jungian compensation, amplification, complexes, subjective/objective levels, symbolic interpretation, and active imagination must be labelled Jungian.**
29. **Active imagination requires stable differentiation between ego, image, affect, and external reality.**
30. **Imaginal content is never a command requiring literal obedience.**
31. **Adlerian purposive readings are optional hypotheses, not established dream functions.**
32. **Lacanian desire, demand, repetition, and fantasy are specialist interpretive bridges, not ordinary synonyms and not substitutes for attachment theory.**
33. **Theory should earn its place by improving formulation, discrimination, or action.**
34. **Productive reflection changes the information state.**
35. **Rumination repeats analysis without changing the information state.**
36. **When further interpretation becomes more certain than the evidence permits, return to observation.**
37. **When no more evidence is available, uncertainty is an acceptable endpoint.**
38. **The purpose of self-analysis is not maximal interpretation.**
39. **The purpose is the most defensible useful formulation.**
40. **A good formulation should eventually return the person to deliberate action in waking life.**

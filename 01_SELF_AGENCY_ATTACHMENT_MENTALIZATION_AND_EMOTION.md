# 01 | Self-Agency, Attachment, Mentalization, and Emotion

## 1. What is the self in this corpus?

The self in this corpus is not a fixed personality type, a hidden essence, or a stable list of traits.

The working question is:

> **What happens to my capacity to perceive, regulate, mentalize, choose, and act when a relational system becomes activated?**

The self is treated here as a set of capacities that are embodied, developmental, relational, and context-sensitive. Those capacities include:

- experiencing oneself as the subject of one's actions;
- distinguishing one's own mind from another person's mind;
- identifying affect and bodily activation;
- regulating affect alone and with others;
- maintaining a coherent enough sense of self when attachment, identity, or intimacy is threatened;
- representing another person's perspective without claiming certainty about it;
- choosing among possible actions rather than simply enacting the strongest impulse;
- expressing needs, preferences, limits, and disagreement;
- accepting dependence where dependence is real without collapsing autonomy into distance;
- tolerating separateness without converting it automatically into abandonment, rejection, control, or indifference;
- tolerating intimacy without equating it with surrender of agency;
- updating expectations when present evidence differs from prior expectations;
- recovering flexibility after periods of relational activation.

This makes the self both **personal** and **relational**. A person's capacities are not invented from nothing inside an isolated mind. They develop through bodily action, interaction, affect regulation, recognition, response, misattunement, repair, and the gradual ability to represent mental states. Yet relational origin does not mean relational determinism. The adult is not merely the product of a partner's responses. Agency includes the capacity to observe one's own patterns, revise expectations, tolerate uncertainty, set boundaries, seek support, self-soothe, and act deliberately even when another person remains unchanged.

Jean Knox's account of self-agency is especially useful here because it resists a false choice between autonomy and relationship. Her developmental argument is that agency emerges through interaction. Early bodily action gives the infant experience of effects in the world. Turn-taking and mutual regulation then contribute to a sense that one's signals matter. Over time, implicit relational knowing becomes increasingly integrated with symbolic and reflective capacities. A mature sense of agency is therefore not the achievement of needing nobody. It is the ability to remain a subject in relationship.

Fonagy, Gergely, Jurist, and Target add a complementary developmental account. The capacity to understand behaviour in terms of mental states develops through relationships in which affect is marked, mirrored, represented, and rendered thinkable. A self capable of mentalization does not merely feel. It can increasingly experience feelings as internal states with meanings, causes, uncertainties, and alternatives. The same applies to thoughts, desires, beliefs, intentions, and memories.

Mikulincer and Shaver add an attachment-regulation model. Under threat, the adult attachment system can organize attention, memory, appraisal, proximity seeking, and defensive strategies. This means that the same person may think, feel, and act differently when relational security is not in question than when it is. A calm self and an attachment-activated self are not necessarily different personalities. They may be different states of the same person under different regulatory demands.

Greenberg and Goldman add an emotion-focused distinction that prevents attachment from becoming the explanation for everything. Intimate relationships also organize identity and recognition, influence and power, attraction and liking, in addition to attachment and security. Threats to identity can produce shame, anger, powerlessness, or defensive dominance even where fear of abandonment is not the main issue. Loss of attraction can matter without being reducible to attachment insecurity. This corpus therefore treats attachment as a major regulatory system, not as a total theory of intimate life.

The self is therefore best represented as a dynamic configuration:

> **BODY + AFFECT + ATTENTION + MEMORY + EXPECTATION + SELF/OTHER REPRESENTATION + REGULATION + MENTALIZATION + AGENCY + RELATIONAL CONTEXT**

None of these terms is a synonym for the others.

## 2. Self-agency as a central organising construct

Self-agency means experiencing oneself as an originator and owner of action, attention, meaning, choice, and response. It does not imply unlimited control. It does not mean that motives are fully conscious. It does not mean that the person is independent of others. It does not mean that social influence disappears.

Agency is better understood as a **capacity for participatory authorship**.

A person with adequate agency can often do the following, at least to some degree:

1. notice what is happening internally;
2. distinguish an impulse from an obligation;
3. distinguish another person's request from a command;
4. identify what they want, do not want, or do not yet know;
5. tolerate the existence of another viewpoint;
6. respond rather than only react;
7. own an action without making a global identity judgment;
8. revise an interpretation when evidence changes;
9. remain psychologically present during disagreement;
10. recover after losing flexibility.

Knox's developmental account places agency in a relational sequence. Bodily action and contingency detection provide early experiences of effect. Social turn-taking adds the experience of influencing another person and being influenced in return. Mutual regulation gives the infant a practical sense that states can change through interaction. Later symbolic development and mentalization permit a more explicit sense of ownership: *this is my thought, my feeling, my intention, my choice; that is yours; they can differ*.

This matters clinically and relationally because agency can contract under activation. A person may say:

- “I had no choice but to keep asking.”
- “I had to leave immediately.”
- “I could not say what I wanted.”
- “I knew exactly what she meant.”
- “If I disagree, I will lose him.”
- “If I need her, she controls me.”
- “I only realised later that I did not want to agree.”

These statements can describe genuine constraint, but they can also describe states in which the range of mentally available options has narrowed. The analytical task is to determine which is which.

Agency must therefore be assessed against reality. It is not useful to tell a person to become more agentic when they are subject to actual coercion, economic captivity, violence, threats, stalking, sexual pressure, or credible retaliation. In those conditions, reduced freedom may not primarily be an intrapsychic problem. The corpus distinguishes **subjective loss of agency** from **objective restriction of agency**.

A practical definition is:

> **Self-agency is the context-sensitive capacity to experience one's own mind and action as one's own, to represent alternatives, and to act with enough freedom to take responsibility for one's conduct.**

This definition also protects responsibility. Psychological explanation may show why a person's options felt narrow. It does not automatically erase the fact that the person acted.

### 2.1 Agency is not independence

An adult can exercise agency by asking for help.

An adult can exercise agency by depending on a partner in a domain where interdependence is reasonable.

An adult can exercise agency by accepting comfort.

An adult can exercise agency by saying, “I cannot regulate this well alone right now.”

Conversely, refusing support can be unagentic when it is a rigid defensive necessity rather than a considered choice.

Mikulincer and Shaver's attachment account is explicit that dependence on attachment figures is not inherently immature. Secure functioning allows proximity seeking under threat and renewed exploration after security is restored. The secure base model therefore makes mature dependence compatible with autonomy.

### 2.2 Agency is not control over another person

A common category error is to interpret agency as the ability to make the partner respond correctly.

That is not agency.

Agency concerns one's own contribution:

- what one says;
- what one asks;
- what one discloses;
- what one refuses;
- what one does next;
- what one owns;
- what one tests;
- what boundaries one sets;
- what uncertainty one tolerates.

Another person's mind and conduct remain partly outside one's control.

### 2.3 Agency has state and trait-like aspects

People differ in habitual confidence, self-efficacy, attachment expectations, reflective capacity, affect tolerance, and learned relational strategies. Those relatively durable patterns matter. But agency also fluctuates.

It can be reduced by:

- acute fear;
- shame;
- anger;
- exhaustion;
- pain;
- substance use;
- sleep deprivation;
- grief;
- threat;
- rejection cues;
- perceived coercion;
- actual coercion;
- high attachment activation;
- cognitive overload;
- humiliation;
- repeated invalidation.

The fact that agency is state-sensitive is one reason not to infer a personality structure from a single conflict episode.

## 3. The relational self

The relational self refers to the fact that self-experience changes in relation to particular others and particular relational contexts.

This is not the claim that the self is unreal. It is the claim that self-experience is partly organized by interaction.

Different relationships activate different expectations, memories, roles, needs, and regulatory strategies. A person may be composed and assertive at work but highly uncertain with an intimate partner. A person may be warm and dependent with one partner but distant with another. A person may mentalize well in most contexts but become rigid when a specific recurring conflict is activated.

Mikulincer and Shaver's hierarchical account of attachment working models supports this context sensitivity. They describe episodic memories, relationship-specific working models, and more generalized models. A person can possess multiple models of security, hyperactivation, and deactivation. Their relative accessibility varies with relationship history, current cues, mood, goals, and threat.

This means that statements such as “I am avoidant” or “I am anxious” are often too crude for applied work. A better question is:

> **In which relational situations do avoidant or anxious strategies become more likely, with whom, in response to what, and what changes them?**

Ringstrom's relational psychoanalytic account adds the language of multiple self-states. His clinical emphasis is that partners can move among different configurations of self-experience. These self-states are not automatically pathology. Multiplicity can be a normal feature of relational life. What matters is whether the person can reflect on, link, and negotiate among states, or whether one state becomes so dominant that alternative perspectives become inaccessible.

For this corpus, **relational self-state** means:

> A temporary configuration of affect, bodily state, expectation, self-representation, representation of the partner, action tendency, and perceived options that becomes especially available in a particular relationship context.

This is a descriptive construct, not a diagnosis.

### 3.1 The relational self does not erase personal responsibility

Saying that behaviour is relationally evoked does not mean that the partner caused it in a morally exculpatory sense.

A partner may trigger fear. The person still owns whether they threaten, insult, leave, ask for reassurance, take a pause, or communicate directly.

A partner may genuinely behave badly. The person may still bring prior expectations that intensify or distort some interpretations.

Both can be true.

The corpus therefore preserves two simultaneous questions:

1. **What did the other person actually do?**
2. **What did my system make of it?**

Neither question replaces the other.

## 4. Attachment as a regulatory system

Adult attachment theory is most useful here when treated as a dynamic regulatory model rather than a typology.

The core sequence in Mikulincer and Shaver is approximately:

> **Perceived threat -> attachment-system activation -> assessment of attachment-figure availability -> proximity seeking / internalized security -> felt security, or secondary strategy if security is not attained**

The attachment system is activated by perceived threat. Threat may be physical, psychological, interpersonal, or symbolic. In adulthood, attachment activation can increase the accessibility of attachment-related thoughts, memories, images, and action tendencies before the person has consciously formulated what is happening.

The primary strategy is proximity seeking, broadly understood. In adults this can be behavioural, emotional, cognitive, or symbolic. A person may seek physical closeness, contact, reassurance, advice, emotional availability, or the internal representation of a trusted attachment figure.

When availability is appraised positively, the person can experience relief and security and return attention to other systems such as exploration, work, caregiving, sexuality, creativity, or play.

When availability is experienced as uncertain, unavailable, rejecting, dangerous, or ineffective, secondary strategies become more likely. Mikulincer and Shaver describe two principal strategic dimensions:

- **hyperactivation**, associated with attachment anxiety;
- **deactivation**, associated with attachment avoidance.

These are not personalities. They are regulatory strategies that can become chronically accessible.

### 4.1 What attachment explains well

Attachment theory is especially useful for understanding:

- distress under separation or threatened unavailability;
- proximity and reassurance seeking;
- comfort with dependence;
- defensive distance from dependence;
- expectations of partner availability and responsiveness;
- safe-haven use under distress;
- secure-base support for exploration and growth;
- threat monitoring;
- some patterns of emotion regulation;
- caregiving and receiving care;
- some recurring couple interactions.

### 4.2 What attachment does not automatically explain

Attachment should not automatically be used to explain:

- every disagreement;
- every sexual problem;
- every power struggle;
- every instance of anger;
- every withdrawal;
- every lie;
- every affair;
- every boundary conflict;
- every identity conflict;
- every loss of attraction;
- every incompatibility;
- every instance of abuse or coercion;
- every personality difference;
- every reaction to work, health, money, culture, family, or life-stage stress.

A partner may withdraw because they are avoidantly deactivating. They may also withdraw because they are exhausted, angry, flooded, afraid of escalation, finished with the conversation, seeking strategic advantage, protecting themselves from aggression, or simply needing solitude.

The observable behaviour does not identify the mechanism.

## 5. Internal working models

Internal working models are representations and expectations concerning self, others, and relationships that help organize prediction and action.

In Mikulincer and Shaver's synthesis, repeated attachment-related interactions are represented in memory. Episodic experiences can contribute to relationship-specific models, which can in turn contribute to more generalized expectations. These representations form a hierarchy rather than a single master belief.

Important implications follow.

### 5.1 Working models are not literal recordings

They are neither pure fantasy nor perfect copies of reality.

They may contain:

- accurate learning from actual partner behaviour;
- selective attention;
- defensive exclusion;
- memory bias;
- current-mood effects;
- generalized expectations from prior relationships;
- relationship-specific learning;
- current contextual cues.

This is a crucial anti-gaslighting principle. If a person expects a partner to be unreliable, the expectation may partly reflect prior relational learning. It may also accurately reflect repeated present unreliability. Attachment theory does not license the analyst to assume that a painful expectation is merely an internal distortion.

### 5.2 Working models are probabilistic expectations

A useful translation is:

> “When I need closeness or protection, what do I expect will happen, and what do I expect that will mean about me?”

Possible expectations include:

- “You will come toward me.”
- “I will be too much.”
- “You will leave.”
- “I will be mocked for needing you.”
- “I will lose myself if I depend on you.”
- “My needs will not matter.”
- “I can ask without disaster.”
- “We can separate and reconnect.”

These are hypotheses about the organization of expectation. They should be inferred from repeated patterns, not imposed after one event.

### 5.3 Working models can be updated

Attachment theory does not require fixed destiny. Mikulincer and Shaver review both continuity and change. Important changes in the responsiveness and availability of significant attachment figures, new close relationships, loss, separation, supportive partners, and psychotherapy can all contribute to revision.

Change is therefore neither infinitely easy nor impossible. A useful formulation is:

> **working models are relatively stable because they are repeatedly rehearsed and self-confirming, but they remain open to discrepant relational experience and new learning.**

### 5.4 Working model is not complex, schema, personality type, or diagnosis

These constructs can overlap phenomenologically but belong to different theoretical systems.

An internal working model is specifically an attachment-derived representational construct.

It should not be silently renamed as:

- a Jungian complex;
- a cognitive schema;
- an emotion scheme;
- a dissociated self-state;
- a personality disorder;
- a trauma response.

Comparison may be useful later. Equivalence should not be assumed.

## 6. Security

Attachment security is not a personality badge. It describes a pattern in which attachment needs can be acknowledged, proximity or support can be sought when appropriate, support can be used, and the person can return to exploration and ordinary functioning.

In self-report attachment research, security is often operationalized as relatively low anxiety and low avoidance. That dimensional language matters. Mikulincer and Shaver explicitly warn that categorical shorthand can make continuous dimensions look like discrete types.

Security has both interpersonal and intrapersonal implications.

### 6.1 Safe haven

Under threat, an attachment figure can function as a source of comfort, protection, soothing, or help.

### 6.2 Secure base

Once adequate security is present, the person can explore, work, create, take risks, and pursue goals without the attachment system monopolizing attention.

### 6.3 Internalized security

Repeated experiences of support can become available as internal resources. Self-soothing is therefore not necessarily the opposite of co-regulation. In attachment theory, capacities for internal regulation can develop partly through the internalization of reliable relational regulation.

### 6.4 Security and agency

Security can expand agency by freeing attention from threat monitoring.

The secure person is not necessarily calm all the time. Security means that activation can be regulated without requiring chronic hyperactivation or deactivation. In practical terms, the person is more likely to retain access to multiple behavioural options.

### 6.5 Security is not proof of relationship quality

A person can have relatively secure attachment tendencies and still be in an incompatible, exploitative, sexually unsatisfying, or unsafe relationship.

Likewise, a relationship can contain many secure processes and still end.

Relationship-outcome neutrality therefore remains intact.

## 7. Anxiety and hyperactivation

Attachment anxiety is a continuous dimension. It concerns heightened concern about partner availability, rejection, abandonment, and one's value to the attachment figure.

Hyperactivation is the associated regulatory strategy of increasing attention to threat and intensifying proximity-seeking efforts when security is uncertain.

Common processes described in the attachment literature include:

- vigilance for signs of unavailability;
- repeated checking;
- rumination;
- intensified bids for reassurance;
- difficulty disengaging from attachment threat;
- amplification of distress;
- protest;
- strong sensitivity to ambiguous cues;
- increased accessibility of negative attachment-related material;
- difficulty returning attention to other tasks.

These are tendencies, not diagnostic criteria.

### 7.1 Hyperactivation is functional before it becomes costly

The strategy has an intelligible goal: restore contact and security.

It can become costly when the effort to ensure closeness generates more threat. Repeated criticism, interrogation, testing, protest, or coercive demands can make a partner withdraw, become defensive, or retaliate. The resulting withdrawal may then confirm the initial expectation of unavailability.

This creates a possible feedback loop:

> threat -> intensified proximity seeking -> partner strain or withdrawal -> more threat -> more intensification

That loop is a formulation, not proof of motive.

### 7.2 Do not translate every pursuit into attachment anxiety

Pursuit can also reflect:

- a legitimate unresolved practical issue;
- anger at broken agreements;
- a need for accountability;
- intolerance of uncertainty unrelated to attachment;
- identity threat;
- a learned communication style;
- strategic control;
- cultural expectations;
- acute situational stress.

The attachment formulation gains credibility when the subjective meaning specifically concerns availability, abandonment, lovability, closeness, or inability to restore security, and when similar patterns recur across attachment-relevant contexts.

### 7.3 Agency under hyperactivation

Hyperactivation can narrow agency because the person's attention becomes organized around restoring contact.

The available options can feel reduced to:

- get an answer;
- get reassurance;
- prevent separation;
- keep talking;
- obtain certainty;
- stop the partner from leaving.

Agency recovery therefore involves widening the action set, not simply suppressing need.

## 8. Avoidance and deactivation

Attachment avoidance is also a continuous dimension. It concerns discomfort with closeness, dependence, vulnerability, and reliance on attachment figures.

Deactivation is the associated regulatory strategy of reducing attachment-system activation by inhibiting proximity seeking, suppressing attachment-related thoughts and feelings, or emphasizing self-reliance and distance.

Common deactivating processes described by Mikulincer and Shaver include:

- diversion of attention from attachment threat;
- suppression of attachment-related thoughts;
- minimization of need;
- reduced help seeking;
- preference for psychological distance;
- discomfort with disclosure and dependence;
- attempts to preserve control and self-reliance;
- downplaying separation distress;
- reduced accessibility of vulnerable affect in awareness.

Again, these are tendencies, not a personality diagnosis.

### 8.1 Deactivation is not absence of emotion

A person may report little distress while showing other signs of activation. The attachment literature includes findings in which overt reports and physiological responses do not always align. More generally, suppression can reduce conscious access without eliminating underlying arousal.

The safe conclusion is not “avoidant people secretly feel X.” The safe conclusion is that self-report, behaviour, and physiological response can diverge, so absence of reported distress should not always be treated as proof of absence of activation.

### 8.2 Withdrawal does not prove avoidance

Withdrawal might reflect:

- deactivation;
- flooding;
- fear of escalation;
- exhaustion;
- anger;
- strategic punishment;
- shame;
- inability to formulate a response;
- need for solitude;
- a reasonable boundary;
- disengagement from the relationship;
- physical illness;
- cognitive overload.

One behaviour can arise from many mechanisms.

### 8.3 Agency under deactivation

Deactivation can look highly agentic because it emphasizes independence and control. But rigid self-reliance may actually reduce freedom. If the person cannot ask for help, cannot tolerate dependence, cannot name need, or must leave whenever vulnerability appears, the behavioural repertoire is narrow.

The key question is not whether the person is distant. It is whether distance is **chosen flexibly** or **required defensively**.

## 9. Context sensitivity and change

Attachment theory is often popularized as if each adult possesses one fixed attachment style that explains most close relationships.

The source literature is more nuanced.

Mikulincer and Shaver describe both chronic tendencies and contextual activation. Working models exist at different levels, from specific episodes to relationship-specific and more generalized models. Contextual cues concerning availability, support, rejection, threat, current goals, and current mood can change which representations become most accessible.

This yields four practical rules.

### 9.1 Use dimensions, not boxes

Anxiety and avoidance are continuous dimensions. Categories can be useful shorthand, but they discard information and encourage over-identification.

### 9.2 Ask “with whom?”

A person's pattern with one partner may not be identical to the pattern with another.

### 9.3 Ask “under what conditions?”

Stress, illness, conflict topic, distance, life transition, sexual vulnerability, financial pressure, grief, and perceived betrayal can change regulation.

### 9.4 Ask “what changes it?”

A pattern is more informative when we know what increases and decreases it.

Examples:

- Does clear reassurance reduce pursuit?
- Does agreed time-limited space reduce withdrawal?
- Does a direct request work better than criticism?
- Does the pattern disappear when workload falls?
- Does the same reaction occur with friends and colleagues?
- Does the person behave differently with a reliably responsive partner?

These observations help distinguish trait-like tendencies from interaction-specific adaptation.

### 9.5 Change does not require rewriting childhood

Attachment patterns may be shaped by early relationships, but adult change can occur through new relational experience, deliberate reflection, therapy, and changed circumstances. Present behaviour does not prove a particular childhood history.

The correct evidentiary direction is:

> **current pattern -> possible developmental hypothesis**

not:

> **current behaviour -> certain childhood cause**

## 10. Attachment and emotion regulation

Attachment is a regulation system. Emotion regulation is therefore not an optional add-on to attachment theory.

Mikulincer and Shaver describe security, hyperactivation, and deactivation as distinct regulatory organizations.

### 10.1 Secure regulation

Under security, distress can be acknowledged without monopolizing the whole system. Support can be sought and received. The person can use internal and external resources and then redirect attention to other goals.

### 10.2 Hyperactivating regulation

Hyperactivation tends to sustain or amplify attachment-relevant distress. Attention remains drawn to threat. Negative thoughts and memories can become more accessible. The person may repeatedly rehearse the problem, monitor the partner, or seek reassurance.

### 10.3 Deactivating regulation

Deactivation attempts to regulate by turning down attachment activation. Attention is moved away from need and vulnerability. Thoughts and feelings that could reactivate dependence may be suppressed or excluded from awareness.

### 10.4 Regulation is not the same as emotional health

A strategy can reduce conscious distress while creating other costs.

For example:

- reassurance seeking may briefly reduce anxiety but reinforce uncertainty;
- emotional suppression may reduce conscious distress but impair communication;
- anger may restore a temporary sense of power while damaging connection;
- withdrawal may prevent escalation while also blocking repair;
- self-soothing may increase flexibility, or it may become a way to avoid necessary conversation.

Regulation must therefore be assessed by function, not by appearance.

## 11. Self-regulation and co-regulation

Self-regulation and co-regulation are often discussed as if one were mature and the other immature.

That is too simple.

Human affect regulation is relational from the beginning. Infants rely heavily on caregivers to regulate arousal, attention, and distress. Over development, capacities that were initially supported interpersonally become increasingly available internally. Adult regulation therefore usually contains both self-regulatory and interpersonal components.

A useful distinction is:

- **self-regulation**: actions or mental operations through which a person modulates their own arousal, attention, emotion, meaning, or behaviour;
- **co-regulation**: reciprocal processes through which two people influence one another's emotional state;
- **other-regulation**: attempts by one person to change the other's emotional state;
- **delegated regulation**: expecting the partner to carry responsibility for one's state without adequate self-regulatory participation.

These are not moral categories. Each can be adaptive or maladaptive depending on context and function.

### 11.1 Healthy self-regulation

Healthy self-regulation may include:

- orienting attention away from catastrophic rumination;
- naming the emotion;
- slowing physiological arousal;
- tolerating an urge without acting immediately;
- distinguishing what is known from what is feared;
- asking for a pause;
- remembering previous successful repair;
- using social support outside the couple where appropriate;
- sleeping, eating, moving, or reducing overload when physiology is contributing;
- returning to a conversation after arousal decreases;
- making a direct request rather than escalating indirectly.

Self-regulation is not emotional suppression. A person can regulate by becoming more able to feel an emotion without being governed by it.

### 11.2 Healthy co-regulation

Healthy co-regulation may include:

- responsive touch where welcome;
- calm presence;
- listening;
- reassurance;
- shared humour;
- validation;
- predictable return after a pause;
- practical help;
- negotiated proximity;
- explicit recognition of hurt;
- repair after misattunement.

Co-regulation works best when the partner is willing and safe enough to participate. It cannot be demanded as proof of love.

### 11.3 When co-regulation becomes coercive

A request for contact can become coercive when the person treats the partner as having no legitimate separate mind, limits, fatigue, or need for space.

Examples include:

- repeated messaging after a clear request for time;
- blocking departure;
- threatening self-harm to force contact;
- demanding immediate disclosure;
- treating refusal to reassure as betrayal;
- insisting that the partner regulate emotions produced by one's own aggression.

Attachment need does not erase consent or agency.

### 11.4 When self-regulation becomes defensive isolation

Self-regulation can also be distorted into compulsory self-sufficiency.

Examples include:

- refusing all help because need feels humiliating;
- treating dependency as weakness;
- hiding distress until resentment accumulates;
- using “I need space” as an indefinite avoidance of accountability;
- leaving every difficult conversation without agreed return;
- equating intimacy with loss of self.

The goal is not maximum self-regulation. It is **flexible regulation**.

### 11.5 Greenberg and Goldman: both self-soothing and other-soothing

Greenberg and Goldman are especially useful because they explicitly resist the idea that couple regulation should be outsourced entirely to the partner. They retain the importance of empathic responsiveness and mutual soothing, but add a stronger emphasis on self-soothing, tolerating disappointment, and transforming maladaptive emotion schemes.

Their point is clinically important. A partner may become more responsive and still be unavailable sometimes. Durable functioning therefore requires both:

> **I can use you when you are available**

and

> **I can remain a psychological subject when you are not immediately available.**

That combination links attachment security with agency without making them identical.

## 12. Mentalization and reflective function

Mentalization is the capacity to understand behaviour in terms of intentional mental states such as feelings, beliefs, desires, intentions, wishes, thoughts, and goals.

Reflective function is closely related terminology used in the Fonagy tradition, especially in relation to the capacity to reflect on mental states and the developmental processes that support it.

For this corpus, the practical meaning is:

> **Mentalization is the capacity to hold behaviour and experience in mind as meaningfully related to mental states while remembering that mental states are internal, changeable, partly opaque, and not directly visible.**

This definition contains several safeguards.

### 12.1 Mentalization is not mind-reading

Mentalization does not give privileged access to another person's motives.

A mentalizing statement sounds like:

- “I wonder if you felt dismissed.”
- “I am imagining that my silence may have looked like indifference.”
- “I notice I am assuming you wanted to punish me, but I do not actually know that.”
- “Part of me thinks you are leaving because you do not care; another part knows you may simply be overwhelmed.”

A non-mentalizing certainty sounds like:

- “You did that because you wanted control.”
- “I know you do not care.”
- “Your silence proves you are ashamed.”
- “You only said that to manipulate me.”

The second set may sometimes be correct, but the form of certainty itself exceeds the evidence available from observation alone.

### 12.2 Mentalization includes the self

A person can be very skilled at theorizing about the partner while being poorly mentalized about themselves.

Self-mentalization asks:

- What am I feeling?
- What do I believe is happening?
- What do I want?
- What am I afraid of?
- What is my body doing?
- What action am I preparing to take?
- What am I assuming?
- What earlier experience is becoming salient?
- What do I not know yet?

Mentalization is therefore not primarily an intellectual analysis of the other. It is a two-sided reflective capacity.

### 12.3 Mentalization is embodied and affective

Fonagy and colleagues do not reduce mentalization to abstract cognition. Their developmental model links the representation of affect with the development of self-understanding and self-regulation. Parental affect mirroring is important because it helps the child represent internal states as mental states rather than simply being immersed in them.

Affect that can be represented can increasingly be thought about.

Affect that cannot be represented is more likely to be enacted, somatized, discharged, or treated as literal reality.

### 12.4 Mentalized affectivity

Fonagy and colleagues use the concept of **mentalized affectivity** to describe a mature capacity to experience affect while reflecting on it and regulating it. The person is not detached from emotion and is not simply overwhelmed by emotion.

The process involves, in broad terms:

- identifying affect;
- modulating affect;
- expressing affect in a way that remains connected to meaning and relationship.

This is highly relevant to intimate relationships. The aim is not to replace emotion with thought. It is to permit emotion to become thinkable without being drained of its force.

---

## 13. Knowing one's own mind

Knowing one's own mind sounds simple but is developmentally and relationally demanding.

A person can know a fact about external reality more easily than they can know what they want when several motives conflict.

Examples:

- wanting closeness and space at the same time;
- wanting reassurance and resenting the need for it;
- wanting to forgive and still feeling angry;
- wanting to leave and fearing loss;
- wanting sex and fearing exposure;
- wanting recognition and fearing dependence on recognition;
- wanting to say no and fearing the consequences of saying no.

The capacity to know one's mind therefore includes the capacity to tolerate internal multiplicity.

Ringstrom's relational model is useful here. He emphasizes multiple self-states and the need to negotiate among them rather than forcing a premature single answer. His recurring clinical question concerns the ability to have “a mind of one's own” while remaining in relationship.

### 13.1 Knowing one's mind is not the same as immediate certainty

A hurried answer may be less agentic than a delayed answer.

“I do not know yet” can be a highly agentic statement when it protects the time needed to identify one's actual position.

### 13.2 Knowing one's mind requires separating layers

A useful sequence is:

1. **body**: what sensations are present?
2. **affect**: what emotion is present?
3. **thought**: what interpretation arose?
4. **fear**: what negative outcome is anticipated?
5. **want**: what desired outcome is present?
6. **need**: what broader need may be implicated?
7. **value**: what matters here beyond immediate relief?
8. **action tendency**: what do I want to do?
9. **choice**: what action do I endorse after reflection?

The distinction between action tendency and chosen action is central to agency.

### 13.3 Self-knowledge remains fallible

Mentalization does not require the belief that introspection is infallible.

People can misunderstand themselves because of:

- defensive processes;
- retrospective reconstruction;
- shame;
- wishful thinking;
- state-dependent memory;
- limited emotional vocabulary;
- competing motives;
- social desirability;
- fear of consequences.

Therefore self-report receives high evidentiary value as evidence of subjective experience, but not absolute authority as evidence of every causal mechanism.

---

## 14. Knowing another mind without pretending certainty

Another person's mind is partly knowable and partly opaque.

This principle is called **partner opacity** in this corpus.

The partner can report thoughts, feelings, intentions, motives, fears, and memories. Those reports matter. Behaviour provides additional evidence. Repeated patterns provide more. Yet no observer has direct access to the totality of another person's internal state.

This has practical consequences.

### 14.1 A hierarchy of claims about the partner

From strongest to weakest, a claim may be based on:

1. **directly observable behaviour**;
2. **the partner's explicit report**;
3. **repeated behaviour across comparable situations**;
4. **a plausible formulation consistent with several observations**;
5. **an inferred motive**;
6. **a deep developmental or unconscious interpretation**.

Confidence should normally decrease as one moves down the list unless evidence accumulates.

### 14.2 Validation does not require mind-reading

One can validate a partner's reported experience without claiming to know more than they know about themselves.

For example:

> “I can see that my leaving the room frightened you.”

is different from:

> “You were frightened because your father abandoned you and now you are reenacting that trauma.”

The first may be grounded in direct report. The second may be a developmental hypothesis requiring much more evidence.

### 14.3 Suspicion is not forbidden

Partner opacity does not require naive trust.

A person may have strong reasons to suspect deception, hostility, or strategic behaviour. The rule is simply to distinguish:

- what is observed;
- what is reported;
- what is inferred;
- what evidence would raise or lower confidence.

This is especially important in betrayal, abuse, and coercion contexts. “We cannot know another mind perfectly” must not become a device for ignoring credible behavioural evidence.

### 14.4 Mentalization under disagreement

A mature mentalizing position can hold:

> “I think your action had this meaning for me. You report a different intention. The impact is real. The intention remains partly dependent on your report and the broader evidence. We may not reach identical interpretations.”

This permits accountability without fictional omniscience.

## 15. What happens to mentalization under stress

Mentalization is state-sensitive.

People who mentalize well under ordinary conditions may mentalize poorly under intense affect, attachment threat, humiliation, anger, fear, exhaustion, or interpersonal pressure.

Under stress, thinking can become:

- more concrete;
- more certain;
- more polarized;
- more action-oriented;
- less curious;
- less able to hold multiple perspectives;
- more dominated by immediate affect;
- more dependent on old expectations;
- more likely to confuse feeling with fact.

Fonagy and colleagues provide two especially useful developmental concepts for understanding these shifts: **psychic equivalence** and **pretend mode**.

### 15.1 Psychic equivalence

In psychic equivalence, internal experience is treated as equivalent to external reality.

The practical form is:

> “Because it feels true, it is true.”

Examples:

- “I feel abandoned, therefore you are abandoning me.”
- “I feel controlled, therefore you are trying to control me.”
- “I feel ashamed, therefore you think I am worthless.”
- “I feel unsafe, therefore you are dangerous.”

Sometimes the external claim is correct. The problem is not the conclusion alone. The problem is the collapse of the distinction between internal state and external evidence.

### 15.2 Pretend mode

In pretend mode, thoughts and representations become insufficiently anchored to lived reality.

The person may discuss mental states fluently while the discussion remains disconnected from embodied affect, behaviour, or consequences.

In adult applied work, this can look like:

- highly sophisticated theorizing without emotional contact;
- describing a partner through psychological labels rather than current evidence;
- endless interpretation that does not change behaviour;
- speaking about feelings while remaining affectively absent;
- constructing elaborate narratives that do not survive factual checking.

One should be cautious about applying the developmental term too casually. It is a theoretical model, not a pejorative label for intellectual people.

### 15.3 Integration and mentalizing mode

Mature mentalization integrates inner and outer reality.

The person can say:

> “My fear is real, but my interpretation may be wrong.”

or:

> “Your explanation may be sincere, and the behavioural evidence still leaves me uncertain.”

or:

> “I am angry, and I need to understand whether the anger is protecting a boundary, covering shame, responding to actual mistreatment, or some combination.”

This both/and stance is one of the most important markers of recovered agency.

### 15.4 Couple escalation as reciprocal mentalization failure

When both partners lose mentalization, each person's protective move becomes the other's evidence.

For example:

1. Partner A feels uncertain and presses for reassurance.
2. Partner B experiences the pressure as control and withdraws.
3. A treats withdrawal as proof of rejection.
4. B treats pursuit as proof that closeness is dangerous.
5. Both become more certain about the other's motive.
6. Curiosity falls.
7. Behaviour becomes increasingly organized by those certainties.

This is a useful process formulation.

It does not imply equal responsibility. It does not mean either interpretation is false. It means that the loop can reduce each person's ability to test interpretations.

## 16. Affect regulation and development of self

Fonagy and colleagues place affect regulation near the centre of self-development.

The core developmental problem is not merely how a child stops feeling too much. It is how bodily and emotional states become represented, differentiated, understood, and regulated.

Their social biofeedback model of parental affect mirroring proposes that caregiver responses can help the infant build second-order representations of internal states. The caregiver does not simply reproduce the infant's affect. Effective mirroring is marked and contingent enough to communicate both recognition and separateness.

The developmental achievement is therefore not emotional fusion.

It is increasingly differentiated representation:

> “This is my feeling. You can recognize it without becoming it. It can be represented, named, modulated, and survived.”

### 16.1 Affect representation and selfhood

A person who can represent affect has more options than a person who can only enact it.

Represented fear can become:

- “I am afraid you will leave.”

rather than only:

- clinging;
- interrogating;
- threatening;
- panicking;
- shutting down.

Represented anger can become:

- “I am angry because I believe a boundary was crossed.”

rather than only attack.

Represented shame can become:

- “I feel exposed and diminished.”

rather than disappearance, contempt, or counterattack.

The point is not that verbalization always solves the problem. It is that representation creates psychological distance between state and action.

### 16.2 Development does not imply a simple parenting formula

Fonagy and colleagues engage multiple developmental, genetic, neurobiological, cognitive, attachment, and psychoanalytic perspectives. Their model should not be reduced to “bad parenting causes poor mentalization.”

Development is probabilistic and multiply determined.

### 16.3 Affect regulation in adulthood

Adult relationships can support affect regulation through:

- accurate recognition;
- marked responsiveness;
- non-coercive dialogue;
- capacity to tolerate difference;
- repair after misattunement;
- acknowledgement of impact;
- space for self-definition;
- reliable return after separation.

They can also undermine regulation through chronic unpredictability, contempt, intimidation, humiliation, invalidation, or coercion.

Again, present relational reality must be assessed before interpreting the reaction solely as an internal vulnerability.

## 17. Agency, autonomy, and intimacy

Agency, autonomy, and intimacy are related but distinct.

### 17.1 Agency

Agency concerns ownership, choice, action, and the experience of being a subject.

### 17.2 Autonomy

Autonomy concerns the capacity for self-direction, self-definition, and action according to one's own reasons and values.

### 17.3 Intimacy

Intimacy concerns forms of closeness that involve disclosure, responsiveness, emotional knowledge, bodily or sexual connection, shared experience, and mutual influence.

The central developmental challenge is not choosing autonomy over intimacy. It is maintaining enough autonomy and agency **within** intimacy.

Knox's work is especially direct on this point. Her account of the “fear of love” describes situations in which relationship becomes associated with coercion and loss of subjectivity. If early or later relationships repeatedly communicate that closeness requires submission, the person may protect agency through distance, emotional opacity, compliance, or control.

The resulting problem can look paradoxical:

- the person longs for relationship but fears what relationship will do to the self;
- the person protects autonomy by preventing emotional impact;
- the person may experience another's interpretation as intrusion;
- need itself can feel dangerous because need creates dependence;
- closeness can be experienced as a demand to become the object another person wants.

### 17.4 Autonomy is not distance

Distance is behavioural and relational.

Autonomy is psychological.

A person can be physically distant and psychologically governed by the partner's imagined judgment. A person can be deeply intimate while retaining a strong capacity for self-definition.

### 17.5 Intimacy is not fusion

Intimacy requires two minds.

If difference cannot be tolerated, closeness becomes fragile because disagreement threatens the existence of the bond or the self.

A practical marker of mature intimacy is the capacity to say:

> “I can be affected by you without becoming identical to you.”

and:

> “You can disagree with me without ceasing to be a person I can remain in relation with.”

That capacity is not limitless. Some disagreements concern values, safety, fidelity, children, money, sex, or life plans in ways that may create genuine incompatibility.

### 17.6 Agency can increase through intimacy

A responsive relationship can expand agency by providing security, recognition, accurate feedback, encouragement, and room for exploration.

Agency is therefore not a zero-sum resource where the partner's influence automatically reduces the self.

The relevant question is whether influence is **negotiated and metabolized** or **coercive and engulfing**.

## 18. Dependency and interdependence

Dependency is often moralized.

Attachment theory is useful precisely because it treats reliance on significant others as a normal part of adult functioning.

Adults differ in how much support they need in different domains and at different times. Illness, grief, financial stress, parenting, disability, migration, career transition, and acute threat can temporarily increase dependence.

The clinically useful distinction is not independent versus dependent.

It is among forms of dependence.

### 18.1 Flexible dependence

Characteristics can include:

- ability to ask for help;
- ability to receive help without humiliation;
- ability to tolerate the helper's limits;
- reciprocal caregiving over time;
- continued access to one's own preferences and judgment;
- ability to use more than one source of support;
- ability to function when the attachment figure is temporarily unavailable.

### 18.2 Compulsive self-reliance

Characteristics can include:

- refusal of help even when help is needed;
- shame about dependence;
- minimizing distress;
- excessive control of exposure;
- withdrawal when need becomes visible;
- devaluation of people on whom one depends.

### 18.3 Coercive dependence

Characteristics can include:

- treating availability as an entitlement;
- punishing normal separateness;
- threatening consequences for independent action;
- repeated boundary violations in pursuit of reassurance;
- making the partner solely responsible for one's emotional stability.

### 18.4 Structural dependence

Some dependence is material rather than primarily psychological:

- income;
- housing;
- immigration status;
- caregiving responsibilities;
- health care;
- disability support;
- social isolation.

Structural dependence affects agency and should not be reinterpreted simply as attachment insecurity.

### 18.5 Interdependence

Interdependence is the condition in which each person's outcomes are partly affected by the other's behaviour and decisions.

The relationship-science literature treats interdependence as a basic feature of close relationships. It does not imply symmetry. One partner can be more dependent in a particular domain. Power often follows from asymmetries in dependence, alternatives, resources, and decision control.

For self-analysis, the question is:

> **Where do I genuinely depend on this person, where do I merely prefer their support, where am I pretending not to depend, and where has dependence become coercive or structurally constrained?**

## 19. Shame

Shame is not simply low self-esteem. It is an affective state involving exposure, diminishment, defectiveness, inferiority, loss of standing, or a wish to hide from the eyes of self or other.

Shame is especially important in intimate relationships because partners often become major sources of recognition. Their attention can affirm worth, competence, attractiveness, desirability, morality, or belonging. Their contempt, ridicule, dismissal, comparison, or withdrawal can therefore become unusually potent.

Greenberg and Goldman treat identity validation as distinct from attachment. Their framework is valuable because it prevents every painful response to a partner from being translated into abandonment fear. A person may become activated because they feel demeaned, controlled, incompetent, undesirable, or unseen even while feeling confident that the relationship will continue.

### 19.1 Shame and attachment can overlap without being identical

A shame response may contain:

- “I am unlovable.”
- “I am inadequate.”
- “I am weak.”
- “I am ridiculous.”
- “You do not respect me.”
- “I have failed.”
- “I cannot bear being seen like this.”

Some of these meanings may become attachment-relevant, especially if loss of love is anticipated. Others are more directly about identity and recognition.

### 19.2 Common responses to shame

Shame may lead to:

- withdrawal;
- hiding;
- appeasement;
- silence;
- self-attack;
- collapse;
- counterattack;
- contempt;
- defensiveness;
- perfectionistic repair;
- attempts to restore dominance;
- denial of vulnerability.

The same observable behaviour can therefore emerge from shame or from other affects.

### 19.3 Do not translate all withdrawal into shame

Withdrawal can reflect shame, but it can also reflect deactivation, anger, exhaustion, fear, flooding, strategic punishment, or a reasonable need for time.

### 19.4 Shame and agency

Shame can contract agency by shifting the task from “What do I want to do?” to “How do I stop being seen as defective?”

Agency recovery requires separating:

- what happened;
- what criticism was actually made;
- what global identity conclusion followed;
- whether the criticism is accurate in whole, in part, or not at all;
- what responsibility is specific to conduct;
- what action is now possible.

A useful discipline is:

> **conduct can be owned without converting conduct into total identity.**

“I lied” is not identical to “I am nothing but a liar.”

“I failed at this task” is not identical to “I am a failure.”

This distinction preserves responsibility while reducing global shame.

## 20. Fear

Fear is oriented toward anticipated threat.

In intimate relationships, the feared outcome may concern:

- abandonment;
- rejection;
- humiliation;
- engulfment;
- control;
- violence;
- loss of status;
- sexual rejection;
- betrayal;
- financial insecurity;
- emotional exposure;
- conflict escalation;
- being unable to cope;
- becoming dependent;
- being trapped;
- loss of the relationship;
- loss of self within the relationship.

Fear should therefore not automatically be categorized as attachment fear.

### 20.1 Attachment fear

Attachment-related fear is especially plausible when the threat concerns availability, separation, protection, responsiveness, or loss of the bond.

### 20.2 Identity fear

Identity-related fear may concern loss of standing, control, competence, respect, or self-definition.

### 20.3 Reality-based fear

Fear can be an accurate response to actual danger.

Where there is violence, credible threat, stalking, coercive control, serious retaliation, or sexual coercion, the analysis should not begin by asking how the fearful person can better regulate themselves to remain connected. Safety is prior.

### 20.4 Learned fear and present reality

A person can carry learned sensitivity from prior experience and still face a real present threat.

The existence of an old pattern does not disprove the new event.

A careful formulation therefore asks:

1. What is feared?
2. What present evidence supports that fear?
3. What evidence weakens it?
4. Is the intensity proportionate to the present event?
5. Does the same fear recur across different relationships and contexts?
6. Does reassurance or clarification change it?
7. Is there actual danger requiring protective action rather than interpretive work?

### 20.5 Fear and agency

Fear narrows temporal horizon. Immediate safety or reassurance can crowd out longer-term values and choices.

Agency recovery often requires slowing the sequence enough to distinguish:

> threat signal -> threat appraisal -> predicted outcome -> action urge -> chosen response

The goal is not to suppress fear. It is to regain enough reflective space to respond to what the fear is actually about.

## 21. Anger

Anger deserves separate treatment because it is frequently oversimplified.

Anger can be:

- primary and adaptive;
- primary and maladaptive;
- secondary or reactive;
- instrumental;
- attachment protest;
- a response to injustice;
- a response to boundary violation;
- a defence against shame;
- a defence against fear;
- a response to frustration;
- a dominance strategy;
- an attempt to restore agency;
- a component of coercive control.

These functions are not interchangeable.

### 21.1 Do not translate all anger into fear

Some couple models emphasize vulnerable affect beneath reactive anger. This can be clinically useful, especially where anger functions as protest or defence.

But anger can also be the primary appropriate emotion to violation, obstruction, injustice, deception, or coercion.

Greenberg's broader emotion framework is useful because it asks about the **function** of the anger rather than assuming a single hidden emotion.

### 21.2 Attachment protest

In attachment terms, anger can arise when proximity seeking is frustrated or an attachment figure is experienced as unresponsive. Protest can intensify attempts to restore availability.

Yet identifying anger as protest does not excuse aggression.

### 21.3 Assertive anger

Anger can support agency when it clarifies:

- “No.”
- “This matters.”
- “This boundary was crossed.”
- “I will not accept this condition.”
- “Something needs to change.”

Adaptive anger can therefore counter shame, helplessness, or submission.

### 21.4 Secondary anger

Anger may cover or follow another emotion.

Examples:

- fear -> anger;
- shame -> anger;
- hurt -> anger;
- grief -> anger.

The sequence must be demonstrated in experience, not assumed from theory.

### 21.5 Instrumental anger

Anger can sometimes be used to influence another person, intentionally or habitually.

Examples include escalating until the other gives in, using intimidation to end discussion, or learning that visible rage reliably produces compliance.

This is different from merely feeling angry.

### 21.6 Anger and agency

Anger can both restore and destroy agency.

It can restore agency by mobilizing action against violation.

It can destroy agency when arousal becomes so dominant that the person later says, “I was no longer choosing what I did.”

The responsible formulation keeps both truths available:

> the emotion may be understandable, and the behaviour still belongs to the actor.

---

## 22. Sadness and loss

Sadness is oriented toward loss, absence, disappointment, or recognition that something valued is unavailable.

In intimate relationships, sadness may concern:

- physical separation;
- emotional distance;
- loss of trust;
- loss of a hoped-for future;
- loss of attraction;
- infertility or reproductive loss;
- illness;
- change in sexuality;
- unmet expectations;
- grief for the relationship as it was;
- grief for a version of oneself that existed in the relationship;
- grief after separation or death.

### 22.1 Sadness is not always attachment panic

Attachment and grief are closely related, but sadness can concern losses that do not primarily involve fear of abandonment.

For example, a partner may remain available while an important shared dream becomes impossible.

### 22.2 Sadness can facilitate acceptance

Where fear often mobilizes prevention and anger mobilizes protest, sadness can support recognition that some losses cannot be reversed.

This does not make sadness passive. Grief can clarify value, commitment, and necessary adaptation.

### 22.3 Secondary reactions to sadness

Sadness may be covered by:

- anger;
- numbness;
- compulsive problem-solving;
- intellectualization;
- self-criticism;
- frantic pursuit;
- withdrawal.

Again, these are hypotheses, not universal transformations.

### 22.4 Loss and relationship-outcome neutrality

Grief does not tell us whether a relationship should continue.

A person can grieve while staying.

A person can grieve while leaving.

A person can grieve the loss of an idealized version of the relationship and then choose either renewed commitment or separation.

The developmental task is accurate mourning, not predetermined preservation.

## 23. Identity and recognition

Identity needs concern being recognized as a person with value, agency, competence, integrity, distinctiveness, and legitimate aspirations.

Greenberg and Goldman explicitly distinguish **attachment**, **identity**, and **attraction** as three major relational motivations. This distinction is one of the most important anti-flattening rules in this file.

Attachment asks:

> “Will you be there for me?”

Identity asks:

> “Do you see, respect, value, and recognize me?”

Attraction asks:

> “Are we drawn to one another with interest, warmth, desire, pleasure, and vitality?”

These systems interact, but none can be reduced to another.

### 23.1 Identity threat in couples

Identity threat can arise when a partner:

- dismisses competence;
- ridicules interests;
- attacks moral character;
- treats the other as incapable;
- repeatedly overrules decisions;
- denies the legitimacy of aspirations;
- humiliates publicly;
- imposes a role the person rejects;
- treats difference as defect.

The subjective response may involve shame, anger, fear, or powerlessness.

### 23.2 Recognition is not agreement

To recognize the other as a subject is not to agree with every belief or request.

Recognition means acknowledging that the other person has a separate point of view, legitimate subjective experience, and agency.

Ringstrom's relational framework makes this central. Mutual recognition requires enough separateness for two subjectivities to coexist. When that capacity collapses, interaction can become organized around dominance and submission, doer and done-to positions, or reciprocal negation.

### 23.3 Recognition and agency

Recognition supports agency because the person does not have to disappear to remain in relation.

A recognition-supportive relationship can contain statements such as:

- “I disagree, but I understand that this matters to you.”
- “Your experience is not mine, but I believe that you experienced it that way.”
- “I do not consent to that request, and I am not denying your right to want it.”
- “I need you to own the impact even if your intention was different.”

### 23.4 Identity conflict is not always insecurity

Some identity conflicts are substantive.

They concern:

- religion;
- political values;
- gender roles;
- sexuality;
- career;
- family obligations;
- parenting;
- cultural belonging;
- money;
- lifestyle;
- moral commitments.

A formulation that turns every such conflict into attachment insecurity can erase genuine value difference.

---

## 24. Attraction as distinct from attachment

Attraction includes liking, romantic interest, sexual desire, curiosity, pleasure, novelty, and positive affect directed toward the partner.

Greenberg and Goldman's explicit separation of attraction from attachment matters because a relationship can be:

- secure but low in attraction;
- highly attractive but insecure;
- deeply affectionate but sexually low-desire;
- attached and committed but low in positive interest;
- exciting but low in reliability;
- high in recognition but low in erotic connection.

These configurations are different.

### 24.1 Attachment security does not guarantee attraction

A partner can be dependable, caring, and emotionally available while erotic or romantic attraction declines.

### 24.2 Attraction does not guarantee security

Powerful desire can coexist with unpredictability, betrayal, fear, or poor regulation.

### 24.3 Attraction does not prove compatibility

People can be strongly drawn to partners whose values, life plans, or conduct are incompatible with their own.

### 24.4 Low attraction should not automatically be psychologized

Possible explanations include:

- relationship resentment;
- stress;
- hormonal or medical factors;
- medication;
- depression;
- fatigue;
- sexual pain;
- ageing;
- attraction to others;
- unresolved injury;
- erotic habituation;
- incompatible sexual preferences;
- changing identity;
- ordinary fluctuation.

The mechanism requires evidence.

### 24.5 Agency and attraction

Desire cannot be ethically commanded.

A person can choose actions around attraction, such as disclosure, exploration, boundaries, or treatment. They cannot simply decide to feel desire because the relationship would be easier if they did.

This is another domain where outcome neutrality matters. The corpus can help clarify mechanisms. It cannot turn attraction into an obligation to preserve a relationship.

---

## 25. Relational self-states

A relational self-state is a temporary organization of experience that becomes especially available in relation to a specific person or situation.

It may include:

- bodily arousal;
- emotion;
- attention;
- memory;
- self-image;
- image of the partner;
- expectation;
- action tendency;
- moral narrative;
- attachment strategy;
- perceived power;
- perceived options.

### 25.1 Example: criticism

The observed event is:

> Partner says, “You forgot again.”

Possible self-state A:

- body: chest tightens;
- affect: shame;
- thought: “I never get anything right.”
- partner representation: judge;
- self representation: incompetent;
- action tendency: defend;
- agency: narrowed.

Possible self-state B:

- body: heat;
- affect: anger;
- thought: “I am being spoken to disrespectfully.”
- partner representation: controlling;
- self representation: unfairly treated;
- action tendency: confront;
- agency: may be narrow or intact depending on arousal.

Possible self-state C:

- affect: mild annoyance;
- thought: “Fair point.”
- action: acknowledge and repair;
- agency: broad.

The same sentence can activate very different systems.

### 25.2 Self-states are not personalities

One should not conclude from an activated state that the person “really is” the activated version.

The relevant questions are:

- How often does the state occur?
- How rapidly does it appear?
- What triggers it?
- How long does it last?
- What other states remain accessible?
- Can the person reflect while in it?
- Can the person recover?
- Does it occur across relationships or mainly in one?

### 25.3 Integration versus domination

A flexible self can move among states while retaining continuity and reflective capacity.

A stressed self may become dominated by one state so completely that alternative interpretations temporarily disappear.

Ringstrom links the capacity to negotiate multiple self-states with self-reflexivity and mentalization. This provides a useful bridge to Fonagy without claiming the theories are identical.

## 26. Agency loss in conflict

Agency loss in conflict does not mean literal absence of all agency. It means a contraction in the person's ability to represent options, mentalize, regulate affect, and act according to broader intentions.

A common sequence is:

> **trigger -> appraisal -> affective surge -> narrowed attention -> rigid self/other representation -> protective strategy -> reciprocal response -> further activation -> reduced agency**

### 26.1 Markers of agency contraction

Possible markers include:

- “I had to make you answer.”
- “I had to leave.”
- inability to identify what one wanted;
- certainty about the partner's motive;
- loss of temporal perspective;
- all-or-nothing interpretations;
- inability to hear new information;
- repeating the same argument despite worsening results;
- acting against one's stated values;
- later surprise at one's own behaviour;
- inability to tolerate pause or uncertainty;
- inability to formulate a direct request;
- treating the partner as an object to control or escape.

### 26.2 Mechanisms that can reduce agency

Agency can contract through several different pathways:

- attachment hyperactivation;
- attachment deactivation;
- intense shame;
- anger escalation;
- fear;
- grief;
- physiological flooding;
- sleep deprivation;
- pain;
- substance use;
- identity threat;
- coercion;
- trauma-related activation;
- learned submission;
- habit;
- practical urgency.

Do not select one automatically.

### 26.3 External constraint versus internal contraction

A person may have reduced options because of actual external conditions.

Examples:

- threat of violence;
- financial dependence;
- immigration leverage;
- fear of losing housing;
- childcare constraints;
- retaliation;
- surveillance;
- workplace consequences.

These conditions must not be redescribed as “difficulty with autonomy.”

### 26.4 Recursive cycles do not cancel responsibility

Both partners may become less reflective during escalation. Yet responsibility for specific conduct remains individual.

A cycle can explain sequence.

It does not erase:

- who threatened;
- who lied;
- who hit;
- who broke the agreement;
- who blocked the door;
- who disclosed confidential information;
- who returned after the agreed pause;
- who repaired.

---

## 27. Agency recovery

Agency recovery means widening the range of mentally available and behaviourally possible responses after activation.

It is not the same as calming down completely.

A person can still be angry, afraid, ashamed, or sad while becoming more agentic.

### 27.1 The recovery sequence

A practical sequence is:

1. **Stop adding new data to the conflict when arousal is too high.**
2. **Name the observed event.**
3. **Name the current body state.**
4. **Name the strongest affect.**
5. **Separate affect from factual conclusion.**
6. **Identify the feared outcome.**
7. **Identify the desired outcome.**
8. **Identify the current urge.**
9. **Generate at least two alternative explanations.**
10. **Generate at least two behavioural options.**
11. **Check safety and power.**
12. **Choose an action that one can own later.**
13. **Observe the result.**

This is a corpus synthesis, not a protocol attributed wholesale to any single author.

### 27.2 Recovery through mentalization

Agency expands when a person can say:

- “I am assuming.”
- “I do not know.”
- “Part of me thinks X, another part thinks Y.”
- “This feels like an emergency, but I am not sure it is.”
- “I want reassurance, but I do not want to force it.”
- “I need space, and I can state when I will return.”

### 27.3 Recovery through regulation

Agency expands when arousal becomes tolerable enough for alternative responses to re-enter awareness.

Possible methods include:

- time-limited pause;
- breathing or grounding;
- movement;
- changing physical setting;
- writing before speaking;
- sleep;
- food;
- reducing alcohol or substances;
- calling a safe support person;
- using agreed repair rituals;
- returning at a defined time.

Mind/body sources support the broad proposition that physiological stress changes cognition, attention, and health, but this corpus does not treat generic stress reduction as a substitute for relationship formulation.

### 27.4 Recovery through boundary clarity

Sometimes agency returns not through soothing but through clear limits.

Examples:

- “I will talk when there is no shouting.”
- “I will not remain in the room if you block the door.”
- “I need the financial records before I can make this decision.”
- “I am not agreeing tonight.”

Boundary clarity can reduce ambiguity and restore authorship.

### 27.5 Recovery through recognition

Being accurately recognized by another person can restore agency because the person no longer needs to fight to establish the existence of their experience.

But agency recovery should not be made wholly dependent on the partner's recognition. When recognition is unavailable, self-validation, external support, factual clarification, and boundary action may become more important.

## 28. How attachment, mentalization, emotion, and agency interact

The four central frameworks in this file answer different questions.

| Domain | Primary question | Typical unit | Main risk if overextended |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attachment** | What happens when security, availability, protection, or proximity is threatened? | Attachment system, working model, regulatory strategy | Explaining every relationship problem as attachment insecurity |
| **Mentalization** | Can I represent my own and another person's behaviour in terms of uncertain mental states? | Reflective capacity under current conditions | Turning reflection into detached theorizing or mind-reading |
| **Emotion** | What affect is occurring, what does it signal, and what action tendency does it organize? | Moment-to-moment affect and emotion scheme | Assuming one emotion always hides another |
| **Agency** | Can I experience myself as a subject with options and own the action I take? | Choice, authorship, self-definition, action | Confusing agency with independence, dominance, or control |

The frameworks interact dynamically.

A common sequence may look like this:

> **event -> appraisal -> attachment/identity relevance -> affect -> regulation strategy -> mentalization quality -> perceived options -> action -> partner response -> updated appraisal**

This is an integrative map, not a claim that every episode follows a single linear chain.

### 28.1 Example: delayed reply

Observed event:

> A partner does not reply to a message for four hours during a workday.

Possible formulation A, attachment hyperactivation:

- appraisal: “Something is wrong between us.”
- affect: fear;
- strategy: repeated messaging;
- mentalization: partner motive becomes increasingly certain;
- agency: narrows toward obtaining immediate reassurance;
- behaviour: calls repeatedly;
- likely reciprocal effect: partner feels pressured and becomes less responsive.

Possible formulation B, ordinary contextual stress:

- appraisal: “They are probably busy.”
- affect: mild frustration;
- strategy: wait;
- mentalization: uncertainty preserved;
- agency: broad;
- behaviour: returns to work.

Possible formulation C, present-reality concern:

- context: partner had previously promised to reply about an urgent medical matter;
- appraisal: “This commitment has not been met.”
- affect: anger and worry;
- strategy: seek alternative information and later address the broken agreement;
- agency: may remain broad;
- attachment formulation: possible but not necessary.

Same behaviour from the partner. Different meaning and mechanism.

### 28.2 Mentalization can regulate attachment activation

If a person can hold multiple explanations in mind, uncertainty becomes more tolerable. This may reduce the urgency of hyperactivation.

But mentalization should not be used to talk a person out of credible evidence. If the partner has repeatedly broken agreements, “consider other explanations” is not a substitute for updating one's model from observed conduct.

### 28.3 Attachment security can support mentalization

When felt security is adequate, the person has more cognitive resources available for curiosity, perspective-taking, and exploration. Under intense attachment threat, those capacities can contract.

This does not mean insecure attachment causes every failure of mentalization. Sleep loss, pain, trauma activation, humiliation, intoxication, cognitive overload, and acute anger can also impair reflection.

### 28.4 Emotion gives urgency to working models

Working models are not cold propositions. They are activated in affective contexts.

The expectation “you will not be there” becomes psychologically consequential because it is linked to fear, sadness, anger, shame, bodily arousal, and action tendencies.

### 28.5 Agency determines what is done with activation

Two people can experience similar fear and choose different actions.

One may ask directly for reassurance.

Another may accuse.

Another may withdraw.

Another may self-soothe and wait.

Another may set a boundary because present evidence shows actual unreliability.

The presence of affect does not determine behaviour uniquely.

## 29. What these frameworks explain well

The frameworks in this file are strongest when used at the level for which they were designed.

### 29.1 Attachment theory explains well

Attachment theory gives a coherent account of:

- proximity seeking under threat;
- distress at unavailability;
- safe-haven and secure-base functions;
- anxiety and avoidance dimensions;
- hyperactivating and deactivating strategies;
- relationship-specific and generalized working models;
- some patterns of support seeking and caregiving;
- some aspects of emotion regulation;
- links between security and exploration;
- context-sensitive activation and relative stability.

The evidence base summarized by Mikulincer and Shaver is broad, but the Project source is an earlier edition and should not be treated as an exhaustive statement of research through 2026.

### 29.2 Mentalization theory explains well

Fonagy and colleagues provide a powerful account of:

- the development of reflective function;
- the link between affect representation and self-development;
- the distinction between internal reality and external reality;
- why certainty about mental states can be problematic;
- how mentalization can fail under affective pressure;
- psychic equivalence and pretend mode as developmental modes of psychic reality;
- the role of relationships in supporting the capacity to understand minds;
- mentalized affectivity.

The theory is developmental and clinical. Not every adult conflict should be labeled a mentalization disorder.

### 29.3 Knox explains well

Knox is particularly useful for:

- self-agency as an embodied and relational achievement;
- the importance of turn-taking and mutual regulation;
- the difference between symbolic communication and coercive communication;
- how relationship can become associated with loss of agency;
- why autonomy and intimacy need not be opposites;
- the danger of therapists or partners treating interpretations as privileged facts;
- the importance of allowing another person to remain a separate subject.

Knox's account is integrative and theoretically ambitious. It draws on developmental research, attachment, neuroscience, psychoanalysis, and analytical psychology. Claims should therefore be attributed to the relevant level rather than treated as one homogeneous empirical model.

### 29.4 Greenberg and Goldman explain well

Their couple formulation is especially useful for:

- distinguishing attachment, identity, and attraction;
- treating affect regulation as central to coupling;
- differentiating self-soothing from other-soothing;
- recognizing shame and power in addition to attachment fear;
- understanding dominance-submission as potentially identity-related;
- distinguishing primary from secondary/reactive emotion;
- showing how emotion can organize interaction.

Their work is clinical-theoretical and process-oriented. It should not be treated as proof that every couple conflict derives from the three motivational systems.

### 29.5 Relationship science adds context

The broader relationship literature adds several correctives:

- relationship outcomes depend on both people and the unique interaction between them;
- external stress matters;
- partner responsiveness matters;
- individual differences matter, but relationship-specific experiences often carry substantial predictive weight;
- relationship quality is difficult to predict over time from self-report variables alone;
- culture, social networks, norms, resources, and life circumstances shape relationship processes.

This protects the corpus from psychologizing every problem inside the individual.

## 30. What these frameworks do not explain

A useful theory becomes dangerous when it becomes total.

### 30.1 Attachment does not explain every motive

People pursue status, autonomy, pleasure, justice, achievement, sex, identity, novelty, moral integrity, belonging, and many other goals. Some intimate conflicts are primarily about those goals.

### 30.2 Mentalization does not decide factual disputes

Both partners can mentalize well and still disagree about:

- finances;
- parenting;
- fidelity;
- location;
- religion;
- sex;
- career;
- caregiving;
- household labour;
- relationship structure.

Understanding each other's mind does not guarantee compatible preferences.

### 30.3 Emotion does not determine truth

Emotion is important data about appraisal and need. It is not a direct detector of external reality.

Fear can arise without danger.

Fear can also correctly detect danger.

Anger can reflect injustice.

Anger can also reflect frustrated entitlement.

Shame can reflect a real moral failure.

Shame can also reflect excessive self-condemnation.

The emotion must be understood in context.

### 30.4 Agency does not eliminate constraint

People do not choose from unlimited options. Material resources, health, discrimination, law, children, employment, geography, disability, culture, and power shape available choices.

### 30.5 These frameworks do not establish hidden childhood causes from present behaviour

A recurring adult pattern may suggest a developmental hypothesis. It does not establish the exact origin.

### 30.6 They do not decide whether a relationship should continue

A relationship can improve in security and still remain incompatible.

A relationship can end without proving attachment failure.

A relationship can continue without proving health.

The corpus remains neutral on outcome.

### 30.7 They do not replace safety assessment

Violence, coercive control, stalking, intimidation, sexual coercion, and serious retaliation require direct assessment. They should not be reduced to mutual dysregulation, attachment panic, or a negative cycle.

## 31. Common category errors

| Category error | Why it is wrong | Better formulation |
|---|---|---|
| “I am an anxious attachment type.” | Adult attachment is better represented dimensionally; strategy is context-sensitive. | “I show more attachment-anxious responses in these contexts.” |
| “He withdrew, so he is avoidant.” | Withdrawal has multiple possible mechanisms. | “He withdrew. Deactivation is one hypothesis among several.” |
| “She pursued, so she fears abandonment.” | Pursuit can reflect attachment threat, anger, accountability seeking, uncertainty intolerance, or control. | “What did the pursuit mean subjectively, and what evidence distinguishes mechanisms?” |
| “My attachment style comes from my childhood.” | Developmental continuity is probabilistic, not a simple one-to-one inference. | “Early relationships may contribute, but present evidence does not prove a specific history.” |
| “If I need someone, I lack agency.” | Dependence and agency are compatible. | “Agency concerns how I own and negotiate dependence.” |
| “Autonomy means distance.” | Distance is one behaviour; autonomy is self-direction. | “Can I remain self-directed while connected?” |
| “Mentalization means knowing what my partner really feels.” | That is mind-reading. | “Mentalization holds hypotheses about mental states with uncertainty.” |
| “If I feel abandoned, I am being abandoned.” | This is a psychic-equivalence error unless external evidence supports it. | “The feeling is real; the external conclusion needs evidence.” |
| “If I can explain the pattern psychologically, the behaviour is excused.” | Explanation and responsibility are separate. | “What explains the action, and what still must be owned?” |
| “Anger is always secondary to fear.” | Anger has multiple functions and can be primary adaptive. | “What function does this anger serve in this episode?” |
| “Withdrawal is always shame.” | Withdrawal can reflect many affects and motives. | “What was experienced immediately before the withdrawal?” |
| “Shame means low self-esteem.” | Shame is a specific affective organization involving exposure/diminishment. | “What exactly felt exposed, defective, or diminished?” |
| “Attachment need equals identity need.” | Security and recognition are distinct motivational domains. | “Is the central concern availability, recognition, attraction, or several at once?” |
| “Attraction means secure attachment.” | Desire and security are different systems. | “Assess attraction and attachment separately.” |
| “Self-soothing means I should not need my partner.” | Self- and co-regulation are complementary. | “Can I use both internal and relational regulation flexibly?” |
| “Co-regulation means my partner must calm me.” | Co-regulation requires reciprocity and consent. | “What support can I reasonably ask for, and what remains my responsibility?” |
| “The cycle made me do it.” | Cycles describe reciprocal influence, not exoneration. | “How did the cycle increase pressure, and what action did I still choose?” |
| “Both contributed, so both are equally responsible.” | Mutual influence is not equal responsibility. | “Separate interactional contribution from responsibility for each act.” |
| “Good mentalization will save the relationship.” | Understanding does not remove incompatibility or danger. | “Mentalization can clarify the decision without predetermining it.” |
| “Secure attachment means a healthy relationship.” | Security is one domain of functioning. | “Assess conduct, compatibility, sexuality, power, trust, and context separately.” |

## 32. Applied self-observation questions

These questions are designed to preserve the governing sequence:

> **observation -> report -> pattern -> formulation -> interpretation**

They are not diagnostic tests.

### 32.1 Event-level questions

1. What happened that a camera could have recorded?
2. What did I directly hear, see, or read?
3. What did I infer?
4. What did my partner explicitly report?
5. What remains unknown?

### 32.2 Body and affect

6. What happened in my body first?
7. Which emotion was strongest?
8. Did another emotion appear before it?
9. What action did the emotion prepare me to take?
10. Did the intensity change after clarification, reassurance, distance, sleep, food, or time?

### 32.3 Attachment

11. Did the event threaten availability, responsiveness, proximity, or security?
12. Did I seek more contact, certainty, or reassurance?
13. Did I suppress need, distance, or insist on self-reliance?
14. Did the partner's actual responsiveness change my state?
15. Is this pattern specific to this relationship or common across close relationships?

### 32.4 Mentalization

16. What do I know about my own state?
17. What am I assuming about the other person's state?
18. How certain am I, from 0 to 100%?
19. What direct evidence supports the assumption?
20. What evidence contradicts it?
21. What alternative mental state could also fit the behaviour?
22. Can I distinguish “I feel rejected” from “you reject me”?

### 32.5 Agency

23. What options felt available in the moment?
24. What options can I see now?
25. What did I want from the partner?
26. What could I ask for directly?
27. What part of the situation is under my control?
28. What part is not?
29. Did I act in a way I endorse now?
30. What would a more agentic response look like without requiring the partner to change first?

### 32.6 Identity and recognition

31. Was I afraid of losing connection, or of being diminished, controlled, disrespected, or unseen?
32. What did the event seem to say about me?
33. Did I convert criticism of conduct into a global identity conclusion?
34. Was there an actual identity-relevant insult or humiliation?

### 32.7 Attraction

35. Is the issue about security, recognition, or attraction?
36. Am I assuming that reduced attraction must mean attachment insecurity?
37. Are medical, stress, resentment, sexual, or contextual factors relevant?

### 32.8 Pattern testing

38. Has this happened at least three times in comparable form?
39. What usually triggers it?
40. What usually escalates it?
41. What interrupts it?
42. Does it occur with other people?
43. Does the same partner behaviour always produce the same response?
44. What observation would make my current formulation less likely?

### 32.9 Safety and responsibility

45. Was there threat, coercion, intimidation, stalking, blocked exit, sexual pressure, property destruction, or physical aggression?
46. Who did what?
47. What must be owned regardless of explanation?
48. Is conjoint vulnerability work safe under current conditions?

### 32.10 Outcome neutrality

49. Am I using psychological theory to justify staying?
50. Am I using psychological theory to justify leaving?
51. What becomes clearer if I temporarily remove the question “Should the relationship continue?”
52. What facts, values, and boundaries remain after the psychological formulation is complete?

## 33. Competing formulations

A strong formulation does not merely produce a plausible explanation. It compares alternatives.

The minimum applied standard is:

> **one observed pattern + at least two plausible mechanisms + evidence for and against each + a test that could discriminate them**

### 33.1 Core formulation table

| Observed event | Subjective experience | Affect | Regulatory strategy | Mentalization capacity | Agency | Behaviour | Possible attachment formulation | Alternative explanation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Partner does not reply for hours | “I am being forgotten” | Fear | Hyperactivation | Narrows; motive certainty rises | Reduced | Repeated messages | Availability threat activates anxious strategy | Partner had broken an explicit urgent-response agreement; concern may be reality-based |
| Partner asks for time after argument | “I am trapped” | Anger/fear | Deactivation | May narrow to “they want control” | Reduced or intact | Leaves | Closeness under stress is experienced as threatening | Pause may be reasonable response to flooding or aggressive interaction |
| Mild criticism | “I am worthless” | Shame | Defend / counterattack | Self and partner become globally negative | Reduced | Attack | Attachment anxiety may amplify fear of rejection | Identity vulnerability or learned shame may be more central than attachment |
| Partner becomes quiet | “They do not care” | Sadness/fear | Pursuit | Other-mind uncertainty collapses | Reduced | Demands explanation | Possible attachment alarm | Partner may be tired, processing, angry, afraid, or simply quiet |
| Partner withdraws | “If I stay I will explode” | Anger/flooding | Distancing | May remain partially intact | Moderate | Takes 30-minute break and returns | Could be deactivation | Could be deliberate affect regulation with intact accountability |
| Partner withdraws | “I want them to suffer” | Anger | Punitive distance | Motive is self-reported | Agency intact but behaviour harmful | Silent treatment | Attachment language not required | Strategic punishment/control |
| Partner requests reassurance | “I need to hear we are okay” | Fear | Direct proximity seeking | Intact | High | Clear request | Normative attachment strategy | No pathology required |
| Partner refuses help | “If I need anyone, I lose myself” | Fear/shame | Deactivation | Partly intact | Narrowed | Compulsive self-reliance | Avoidant strategy plausible | Learned identity ideal of self-sufficiency or prior coercive relationship |

### 33.2 Same observable behaviour, different mechanisms

#### Withdrawal

Withdrawal might reflect:

- attachment deactivation;
- physiological flooding;
- fear of escalation;
- shame;
- anger;
- exhaustion;
- strategic punishment;
- need for solitude;
- dissociation;
- a boundary in response to aggression;
- disengagement from the relationship.

Useful discriminators:

- What was felt immediately before withdrawal?
- Was a return time communicated?
- Does the person return when regulated?
- Is withdrawal used to punish?
- Does proximity increase or reduce arousal?
- Is the pattern relationship-specific?
- Is there actual danger?

#### Reassurance seeking

Reassurance seeking might reflect:

- ordinary attachment need;
- attachment hyperactivation;
- obsessive doubt;
- recent betrayal;
- medical uncertainty;
- shame;
- low confidence in a concrete agreement.

Useful discriminators:

- Is reassurance effective?
- How long does relief last?
- Is the concern based on actual unreliability?
- Does the request escalate despite consistent evidence?

#### Anger

Anger might reflect:

- attachment protest;
- injustice response;
- boundary defence;
- shame defence;
- fear defence;
- learned coercive strategy;
- frustration;
- entitlement;
- exhaustion.

Useful discriminators:

- What appraisal preceded it?
- What does the anger seek to accomplish?
- Does vulnerable emotion emerge when arousal decreases?
- Is there a real violation?
- Is anger used to compel compliance?

#### Compliance

Compliance might reflect:

- flexible generosity;
- fear of conflict;
- learned submission;
- strategic accommodation;
- cultural role expectation;
- exhaustion;
- low investment in the issue;
- actual coercion.

Useful discriminators:

- Could the person safely say no?
- Did they know their own preference?
- Is resentment accumulating?
- Are consequences imposed for disagreement?

### 33.3 Competing formulations for a recurring pursue-withdraw pattern

A pursue-withdraw cycle is descriptive. It does not establish why either position exists.

Possible formulations include:

1. **Attachment formulation**: pursuit hyperactivates contact; withdrawal deactivates threat.
2. **Emotion-regulation formulation**: one partner externalizes distress; the other reduces stimulation.
3. **Identity formulation**: pursuit seeks recognition; withdrawal protects against shame or perceived domination.
4. **Power formulation**: pursuit is coercive demand; withdrawal is resistance or counter-control.
5. **Context formulation**: one partner needs resolution before sleep; the other is exhausted from shift work.
6. **Present-reality formulation**: pursuit follows repeated broken commitments; withdrawal avoids accountability.
7. **Compatibility formulation**: partners have chronically incompatible expectations about contact and conflict processing.

The best formulation is the one that explains the most evidence with the fewest unsupported assumptions and remains open to revision.

## 34. Implications for couple dynamics

The self described in this file is inherently relevant to couple dynamics because each partner's regulatory state becomes part of the other's environment.

### 34.1 One person's behaviour becomes the other's event

If A criticizes, that criticism becomes an event for B.

B appraises it, feels something, regulates, and acts.

B's action then becomes a new event for A.

This produces recursion.

But recursion should be described before it is moralized.

### 34.2 The couple cycle is not a personality map

A person who pursues in one relationship may withdraw in another.

A person may pursue around sex and withdraw around money.

A person may become highly anxious only after actual betrayal.

A person may become distant because the current partner is intrusive, not because distance is a global trait.

Therefore cycle position should not be converted directly into attachment identity.

### 34.3 Mentalization is dyadically fragile

Couple conflict often becomes destructive when both partners lose uncertainty about the other mind.

The sequence becomes:

> “I know what you mean” -> “I know why you did it” -> “I know what this says about us” -> action based on certainty -> partner reacts -> certainty strengthens

Restoring partner opacity is therefore a practical intervention:

> “What do I know, what did they report, and what am I inferring?”

### 34.4 Emotion organizes interaction

Emotion is communicated through:

- voice;
- posture;
- facial expression;
- timing;
- proximity;
- silence;
- speed;
- intensity;
- touch;
- withdrawal.

The receiving partner reacts not only to words but to the affective signal.

This is why a content-only analysis of arguments often misses the mechanism.

### 34.5 Agency is relationally contagious but individually owned

A partner can facilitate agency by:

- listening;
- allowing time;
- not interrupting;
- accepting no;
- recognizing uncertainty;
- not imposing motives;
- inviting correction;
- keeping agreements about pauses and return;
- acknowledging impact.

A partner can undermine agency by:

- intimidation;
- ridicule;
- coercion;
- relentless interpretation;
- controlling the terms of conversation;
- demanding immediate answers;
- punishing difference;
- denying factual reality.

Yet each person remains responsible for their own conduct within the degree of actual freedom available.

### 34.6 Security, recognition, and attraction should be tracked separately

A couple can improve one domain without improving the others.

| Domain | Core question | Possible improvement | Possible remaining problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attachment** | Are you available and responsive when needed? | More reassurance and reliable return | Low attraction remains |
| **Identity/recognition** | Do you respect and see me as a separate person? | Less contempt, more validation | Attachment anxiety remains |
| **Attraction** | Are we drawn to and interested in each other? | More affection or erotic engagement | Major value incompatibility remains |
| **Agency** | Can I remain a subject with options? | Clearer boundaries and direct requests | Relationship may still be unsafe or incompatible |
| **Mentalization** | Can we remain curious about minds under stress? | Less motive certainty | Broken agreements may still require accountability |

This is why emotional reconnection should not be treated as the universal endpoint of couple work.

### 34.7 Relationship-specific experience can matter more than trait labels

Large-scale relationship research has found that people's own relationship-specific perceptions, such as perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, and conflict, predict substantial variance in current relationship quality. Individual differences including attachment anxiety and avoidance also matter, but trait-like variables do not replace the person's experience of the relationship itself.

The practical implication is straightforward:

> **Do not let a trait formulation eclipse the actual relationship.**

### 34.8 External stress enters the relationship system

The Cambridge relationship literature and stress models emphasize that couple functioning is embedded in wider contexts. Work pressure, financial strain, caregiving, illness, housing, parenting, discrimination, and social networks can alter regulatory capacity.

A couple may look more “insecure” when chronically overloaded.

That does not make the attachment lens wrong. It means the context must be included.

### 34.9 Power must remain visible

Relationship systems are not always symmetrical.

Power can derive from:

- money;
- housing;
- social status;
- immigration control;
- physical strength;
- access to children;
- sexual leverage;
- information;
- social networks;
- alternatives to the relationship.

An interactional formulation that ignores power can falsely equalize responsibility.

### 34.10 Safety overrides vulnerability work

Where there is violence, intimidation, stalking, coercive control, sexual coercion, credible retaliation, or serious fear, the primary question is not how each partner can become more vulnerable.

Vulnerability without safety can increase risk.

- explanation does not erase responsibility;
- mutual influence does not imply equal responsibility;
- repair does not require forgiveness, reconciliation, or continuation;
- safety and boundaries can take priority over emotional reconnection.

File 06 should operationalize this file through disciplined episode analysis.

> **OBSERVED EVENT -> DIRECT REPORT -> SUBJECTIVE EXPERIENCE -> AFFECT -> REGULATORY STRATEGY -> MENTALIZATION -> AGENCY -> BEHAVIOUR -> RECIPROCAL EFFECT -> RECURRING PATTERN -> COMPETING FORMULATIONS -> TEST -> ACTION -> FOLLOW-UP**

File 06 should never convert this sequence into diagnosis.

It should require:

- partner opacity;
- evidence for and against each formulation;
- alternative hypotheses;
- longitudinal testing;
- explicit responsibility;
- safety assessment;
- relationship-outcome neutrality.

## 37. Evidence discipline

This file draws on sources with different evidentiary status.

**Crosswalk to File 00:** the categories below are local working categories, not a replacement for the two-axis epistemic model in File 00. Empirical and theoretical source status belongs to **Axis B**. Clinical inference and applied hypothesis describe **case-level formulation** and should be mapped to Axis A and to explicit confidence language.

### 37.1 Empirical findings

Empirical findings include:

- psychometric evidence supporting dimensional representations of adult attachment anxiety and avoidance rather than simple discrete types;
- experimental and correlational work on attachment-system activation, security priming, threat processing, emotion regulation, interpersonal perception, and support;
- developmental research on affect mirroring, contingency, reflective function, and social cognition;
- relationship-science research on interdependence, responsiveness, stress, conflict, and relationship quality.

Empirical findings should be read with attention to:

- sample characteristics;
- measures used;
- self-report limitations;
- experimental artificiality;
- cultural context;
- cross-sectional versus longitudinal design;
- whether data concern individuals or dyads;
- whether findings are group-level rather than individual predictions.

### 37.2 Theoretical models

Theoretical models include:

- Mikulincer and Shaver's dynamic model of attachment-system activation and strategies;
- Fonagy and colleagues' developmental model of mentalization, affect mirroring, psychic reality, and mentalized affectivity;
- Knox's account of relationally developed self-agency;
- Greenberg and Goldman's affect-regulation model of attachment, identity, and attraction.

These models organize evidence. They are not themselves direct observations.

### 37.3 Clinical inference

Clinical inference includes claims such as:

- a withdrawal may function defensively;
- anger may protect shame;
- a pursuit may seek reassurance;
- a partner may experience intimacy as coercive;
- a self-state may reflect a learned relational expectation.

Such claims become stronger when supported by:

- direct self-report;
- repeated occurrence;
- temporal sequence;
- response to intervention;
- cross-context comparison;
- convergent evidence.

### 37.4 Applied hypothesis

Applied hypothesis is the lowest-confidence layer.

Examples:

- “This episode may involve attachment hyperactivation.”
- “Shame may be more central than abandonment fear.”
- “The withdrawal may be a deliberate regulation strategy rather than deactivation.”
- “The certainty about motive may reflect temporary mentalization collapse.”

Applied hypotheses should always include alternatives.

### 37.5 Causality discipline

Correlation between attachment anxiety and relationship distress does not prove that anxiety caused the distress.

A distressed relationship can increase insecurity.

Third variables can influence both.

Partners influence one another over time.

Similarly, an association between reflective function and attachment security does not imply a simple single-direction causal chain.

The corpus therefore prefers language such as:

- “is associated with”;
- “is consistent with”;
- “may contribute to”;
- “is theorized to”;
- “is one plausible mechanism.”

Causal language should be reserved for designs and evidence that warrant it.

## 38. Source-specific notes and limitations

### 38.1 Mikulincer and Shaver

The Project source is *Attachment in Adulthood: Structure, Dynamics, and Change*. Its central value for this file is the systematic integration of adult attachment theory and a large pre-publication evidence base around attachment-system activation, working models, security, anxiety, avoidance, hyperactivation, deactivation, emotion regulation, self-regulation, couple functioning, caregiving, sexuality, and change.

Important source controls:

- use anxiety and avoidance dimensionally;
- do not convert strategies into fixed personalities;
- distinguish relationship-specific from generalized working models;
- preserve contextual activation;
- do not infer childhood history from adult style alone;
- recognize that the source reflects the evidence available to its publication period.

### 38.2 Fonagy, Gergely, Jurist, and Target

*Affect Regulation, Mentalization, and the Development of the Self* provides the central developmental account of reflective function, affect regulation, social biofeedback, self-development, psychic equivalence, pretend mode, and mentalized affectivity.

Important source controls:

- mentalization is not mind-reading;
- developmental modes should not become casual adult labels;
- clinical examples do not establish prevalence;
- borderline personality disorder chapters belong to a specific clinical argument and should not be imported into ordinary couple conflict;
- developmental models are probabilistic, not deterministic.

### 38.3 Jean Knox

*Self-Agency in Psychotherapy: Attachment, Autonomy, and Intimacy* provides the central agency frame.

Its most useful contribution for this corpus is that self-agency develops through embodied and relational processes, and that intimacy can become threatening when relationship is experienced as coercive or annihilating of subjectivity.

Important source controls:

- Knox integrates multiple literatures and offers clinical interpretation in addition to empirical review;
- her “fear of love” formulation should be used as a hypothesis, not as a label;
- her criticism of coercive interpretation supports the corpus rule that an analyst's inference is not privileged fact.

### 38.4 Greenberg and Goldman

Their emotion-focused couple work provides the distinction among attachment, identity, and attraction and the emphasis on self- and other-regulation.

Important source controls:

- do not collapse their model into Johnson's attachment-centred Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy;
- primary emotion does not simply mean “deeper” emotion;
- anger, fear, shame, and sadness require functional analysis rather than preset translations;
- couple process theory and clinical procedure do not by themselves establish comparative efficacy.

### 38.5 Cambridge Handbook of Personal Relationships

This is a broad relationship-science source rather than a single theory. It is useful for interdependence, attachment, cognition, emotion, physiology, support, conflict, sexuality, stress, satisfaction, intimacy, culture, and repair.

Its breadth is a corrective to monocausal formulation.

### 38.6 Ringstrom

Ringstrom contributes a relational psychoanalytic account of multiple self-states, self-reflexivity, mutual recognition, and the difficulty of maintaining a mind of one's own in intimate relationship.

His work is primarily clinical-theoretical. It is used selectively and should not be treated as direct evidence for Fonagy's model or for attachment mechanisms.

### 38.7 Adler

Adler is used selectively for historical context around agency, inferiority, social embeddedness, striving, and relational life. His early formulations reflect their historical period and should not override contemporary empirical work.

### 38.8 Mind/Body Health

This source is used only for the broad point that stress, arousal, sleep, physical state, and social relationships can materially affect regulation and health. It is not used to infer specific psychodynamic mechanisms.

## 39. Compact synthesis

The central claim of this file can be stated in ten propositions.

1. **The self is not a fixed type.** It is an embodied, affective, relational, representational, and agentic system whose functioning changes under relational stress.
2. **Agency is not independence.** Mature agency includes the capacity to depend, ask, receive, refuse, negotiate, and remain a subject in intimacy.
3. **Attachment is a regulatory system, not a total theory of relationship.** Anxiety and avoidance are dimensions; hyperactivation and deactivation are strategies.
4. **Working models are neither pure fantasy nor perfect records.** They combine learning from actual relationships with selective attention, memory, defensive processes, and current context.
5. **Mentalization is disciplined uncertainty about minds.** Certainty about another person's motive can represent a failure of mentalization rather than superior insight.
6. **Emotion must be differentiated by function.** Anger is not always fear, withdrawal is not always shame, and sadness is not always attachment panic.
7. **Attachment, identity, and attraction are distinct.** A couple can be secure yet low in attraction, attached yet invalidating, or attracted yet unsafe.
8. **Self-regulation and co-regulation are complementary.** Neither compulsory self-sufficiency nor compulsory partner-soothing is the goal.
9. **Agency can contract and recover.** The practical target is widening the range of perceived options, restoring mentalization, and choosing behaviour one can own.
10. **Interpretation must remain downstream of evidence.** Observation, direct report, recurring pattern, competing formulation, and behavioural testing take precedence over confident psychological storytelling.

The operational question is therefore not:

> “What type of person am I?”

It is:

> **“Under what relational conditions does my capacity to perceive, regulate, mentalize, choose, and act expand or contract, and what evidence would help me understand the mechanism more accurately?”**

## 41. Final use rule

When this file is used for self-analysis or couple formulation, do not begin with an attachment label, a developmental story, or an inferred motive.

Begin with:

1. what happened;
2. what each person directly reported;
3. what was felt and done;
4. whether the pattern recurs;
5. what present reality supports;
6. which mechanisms remain plausible;
7. what evidence would distinguish them;
8. what action preserves safety, responsibility, and agency.

The deepest interpretation is not automatically the best interpretation.

The best formulation is the one that remains accountable to evidence, preserves the other person's opacity, expands rather than contracts agency, and can be revised when reality fails to support it.

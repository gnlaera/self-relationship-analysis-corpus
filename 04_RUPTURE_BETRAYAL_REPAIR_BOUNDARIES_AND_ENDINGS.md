# Rupture, Betrayal, Repair, Boundaries, and Endings

## 1. Purpose and central proposition

A painful event in a relationship does not tell us, by itself, what has happened psychologically or what should happen next.

An argument may be a disagreement, a misattunement, a rupture, a broken agreement, a betrayal, an attachment injury, evidence of incompatibility, aggression, coercive control, or some combination of these. These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Treating every painful event as an attachment injury makes attachment theory too broad. Treating every recurring conflict as a communication failure can conceal substantive incompatibility. Treating violence or coercion as a mutually generated "cycle" can obscure responsibility and create danger.

The central task is therefore not simply to ask whether the relationship is "damaged." It is to determine:

1. **What happened?**
2. **What, specifically, was ruptured?**
3. **What type of rupture is this?**
4. **Whose responsibility belongs where?**
5. **What would count as repair at the relevant level?**
6. **What evidence exists that repair is occurring?**
7. **What remains unresolved even if emotional contact improves?**
8. **Is there sufficient safety for ordinary couple work?**
9. **Are the partners dealing with a repairable process problem, an enduring difference, a substantive incompatibility, or an unsafe relationship structure?**
10. **Does the relevant developmental task concern reconnection, differentiation, renegotiation, separation, or continued uncertainty?**

This file therefore rejects a single equation:

> **pain = rupture = attachment injury = repair required = forgiveness = reconciliation = staying together**

Every term in that chain is distinct.

A relationship can be repaired in one respect and not another. Partners may achieve factual clarity without emotional reconciliation. They may understand each other's motives without restoring trust. They may forgive without resuming the relationship. They may resume the relationship before trust has genuinely returned. They may separate after substantial repair. They may remain together despite unresolved differences. They may also discover that the central problem was not deficient repair but incompatible commitments or an unsafe power structure.

The governing position of this file is **relationship-outcome neutrality**. Preserving the couple is not the overriding criterion of successful work. Ending the couple is not inherently therapeutic failure. The relevant question is what has become clearer, safer, more accountable, more reality-based, and more freely chosen.

## 2. Evidence map and limits

This file integrates sources of different evidentiary status. They should not be flattened into one level of authority.

| Source | Principal contribution here | Evidentiary status | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Millikin (2000) | Discovery-oriented task analysis of attachment-injury resolution | Preliminary dissertation research | Very small sample; should be treated as research lineage, not broad validation |
| Makinen & Johnson (2006) | Verification-stage process study of attachment-injury resolution in Emotionally Focused Therapy | Peer-reviewed process study | Specific sample, therapy model and inclusion conditions; not evidence for every form of betrayal |
| *Clinical Handbook of Couple Therapy* | Affairs, partner aggression, separation/divorce, sexual problems, clinical applications | Specialist clinical handbook drawing on research and treatment models | Chapters synthesize different models; not a single unified theory |
| Gottman, *The Science of Trust* | Trust, betrayal, attunement, repair, repeated interaction | Empirical-clinical theory grounded in observational relationship research | Particular operational definitions should not be mistaken for exhaustive definitions of trust |
| Carr | Review of evidence for couple, family and systemic interventions | Evidence review | Findings apply to defined populations and interventions, not all couples |
| Ringstrom | Mutual recognition, multiple subjectivities, rupture/repair, negotiation, relational thirdness | Relational psychoanalytic clinical theory | Not equivalent to outcome research |
| Morgan | Couple state of mind, reflective third position, psychic separateness, endings | Psychoanalytic couple theory and clinical framework | Clinical-theoretical rather than experimental evidence |
| Mikulincer & Shaver | Adult attachment system, regulation, couple functioning | Large theoretical and empirical synthesis | Attachment constructs should not replace event-level analysis |
| Greenberg & Goldman | Emotion, attachment, identity, influence/power, self- and other-regulation | Emotion-focused theory with process evidence | Related to but distinct from Johnson's attachment-centred Emotionally Focused Therapy |
| Gabb & Fink | Everyday relationship practices, communication, intimacy, non-monogamies and ordinary relationship work | Qualitative sociological research | Descriptive and contextual, not a treatment model |

Millikin's study explicitly described its task-analysis model as preliminary and developed it from three couples. Makinen and Johnson subsequently studied 24 couples receiving Emotionally Focused Therapy and compared resolved with unresolved cases; 15 of the 24 were classified as resolved. The latter therefore carries more evidentiary weight for the attachment-injury sequence, while still remaining a bounded process study rather than a universal model of betrayal.

The *Clinical Handbook* itself separates aggression, affairs, separation/divorce, and sexual problems into distinct treatment domains. That architecture is important: these problems should not be compressed into a generic category of "relationship rupture."

## 3. Mandatory distinctions

The following distinctions govern the entire file.

| Distinction | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **intention ≠ impact** | What a person meant to do and what happened to the other person are different questions. Benign intention can coexist with serious impact. Malicious intention is not required for injury. |
| **explanation ≠ excuse** | Developmental history, attachment fear, loneliness, alcohol use, relationship dissatisfaction or emotional dysregulation may help explain conduct. Explanation does not erase agency or responsibility. |
| **understanding ≠ validation** | One can understand how a person's perception or action arose without affirming it as appropriate. |
| **validation ≠ factual agreement** | A person's fear, humiliation or grief can be recognised as real without agreeing with every interpretation of events. |
| **mutual contribution ≠ equal responsibility** | Both partners may contribute to a dysfunctional interactional cycle while responsibility for a specific deception, assault or broken agreement remains asymmetrical. |
| **apology ≠ repair** | An apology is a communicative act. Repair requires additional evidence, often including acknowledgment, behavioural change and time. |
| **remorse ≠ behavioural change** | Remorse concerns emotional response to one's conduct. Trust depends materially on what happens afterward. |
| **forgiveness ≠ reconciliation** | Forgiveness concerns how an injured person relates to the injury, resentment or offender. Reconciliation concerns renewed relationship. |
| **reconciliation ≠ trust restoration** | People can resume a relationship before trust has returned, or maintain a relationship while trust remains conditional. |
| **repair ≠ staying together** | Repair can occur even when the relationship ends. |
| **staying together ≠ healthy repair** | Continuation may reflect attachment, constraint, fear, habit, hope, children, finances, ambivalence or many other factors. It does not prove repair. |
| **ending ≠ therapeutic failure** | A clearer and more accountable ending may represent meaningful therapeutic work. |
| **conflict ≠ incompatibility** | Conflict is interaction around differences. Incompatibility concerns differences that cannot be jointly enacted under the relationship's available constraints. |
| **privacy ≠ secrecy** | Privacy preserves legitimate separateness. Secrecy withholds information material to the partner's consent, agreed expectations or ability to understand what relationship they are actually in. |
| **boundary ≠ demand** | A boundary defines conditions of one's participation, consent or exposure. It is not simply a rule imposed on another person's internal life. |
| **safety ≠ absence of current assault** | Fear, intimidation, retaliation, stalking, coercive control and sexual coercion can make conjoint vulnerability unsafe even without a recent physical assault. |

These distinctions are not semantic niceties. They determine what kind of intervention is relevant.

## 4. Taxonomy: what kind of event or pattern is this?

### 4.1 Conflict

**Conflict** is a clash of preferences, goals, perceptions, interests or needs.

Conflict does not itself imply pathology, rupture or betrayal. Conflict can be handled constructively, destructively or somewhere between the two. Gottman-oriented work explicitly treats conflict as inevitable and places greater emphasis on how negativity is regulated and repaired than on eliminating disagreement. The clinical model describes early repair, accepting influence, self-soothing and compromise as features of constructive conflict management.

Examples:

- disagreement about household labour;
- competing spending priorities;
- different desires for time together;
- different approaches to parenting;
- different sexual preferences;
- tension between career and family demands.

Conflict describes the **interactional problem**, not its moral or safety status.

### 4.2 Disagreement

A **disagreement** is narrower. Two partners hold different views, preferences or judgments.

A disagreement can remain calm and need no repair beyond clarification or negotiation.

Examples:

- whether a purchase is affordable;
- how frequently to see extended family;
- whether a particular social interaction was flirtatious;
- what constitutes adequate privacy.

Disagreement becomes conflict when the difference recruits competing actions, strong emotion or recurring relational consequences.

### 4.3 Misattunement

**Misattunement** occurs when one partner fails to read, understand, respond to or appropriately calibrate to the other's emotional state or relational bid.

Misattunement is expected in ordinary relationships. It is not synonymous with neglect or betrayal.

Examples:

- offering advice when the partner wanted comfort;
- missing a bid for closeness;
- making a joke when the partner was frightened;
- continuing an argument when the other was physiologically overwhelmed.

Repair is therefore not evidence that a relationship has failed. The capacity to recognise and repair inevitable mismatches may be more important than an unrealistic expectation of continuous attunement.

### 4.4 Rupture

A **rupture** is a meaningful disruption in the relationship's working connection.

A rupture may affect:

- felt safety;
- emotional connection;
- cooperation;
- recognition;
- trust;
- shared reality;
- sexual connection;
- an agreement;
- expectations of reliability.

A rupture can be brief and readily repairable or become chronic. A rupture can arise from one event or accumulated failures.

Not every rupture is a betrayal.

### 4.5 Attachment injury

An **attachment injury** is a more specific construct.

In the Johnson/Makinen/Millikin lineage, it refers to an incident involving abandonment, betrayal or breach of trust at a critical moment of need that becomes a recurring test of the partner's dependability and an impasse in repair.

Therefore:

> **attachment injury = not simply "something my partner did that hurt"**

The attachment significance matters.

### 4.6 Betrayal

A **betrayal** involves violation of an expectation, commitment or relational understanding on which the other person reasonably relied.

Betrayal can involve:

- sexual exclusivity;
- emotional exclusivity;
- money;
- confidentiality;
- loyalty;
- caregiving;
- protection;
- honesty;
- major decisions;
- commitments made during crisis.

The severity of betrayal is not determined only by the external act. Meaning depends partly on the agreement, reliance placed upon it, context, deception and consequences.

Betrayal may or may not become an attachment injury.

### 4.7 Affair

An **affair** is an extradyadic sexual, romantic or emotionally intimate involvement that violates the couple's explicit or reasonably established relationship agreement.

Sexual contact is not the sole criterion. The *Clinical Handbook* notes that extradyadic sexual contact is not required for an outside relationship to be experienced as breaking an agreed commitment to monogamy.

This definition therefore depends partly on the actual agreement.

A close friendship is not automatically an affair. Neither is consensual non-monogamy. Conversely, online or emotional involvement can constitute an affair when it breaches agreed exclusivity or is maintained through material deception.

### 4.8 Broken agreement

A **broken agreement** is failure to follow an explicit or adequately established relational commitment.

Examples include:

- undisclosed spending beyond an agreed threshold;
- breaking a sobriety-related agreement;
- contacting a third party contrary to a negotiated agreement;
- making a major decision unilaterally where joint decision-making had been agreed;
- disclosing private information to others contrary to agreement;
- violating agreed sexual exclusivity.

Not all broken agreements are betrayals. A forgotten household commitment is different from deliberate concealment of a major financial liability.

### 4.9 Boundary violation

A **boundary violation** occurs when a person's limits of consent, privacy, bodily autonomy, access, confidentiality, property or psychological separateness are crossed.

Boundary violations can occur without a pre-existing mutual agreement because some boundaries arise from individual autonomy rather than couple negotiation.

Examples include:

- accessing a private account without consent;
- sexual contact after consent has been withdrawn;
- sharing intimate information without permission;
- persistent unwanted contact after separation;
- involving children in adult conflict in ways that breach appropriate generational boundaries.

### 4.10 Deception

**Deception** involves knowingly causing or maintaining a materially false belief.

It includes more than direct lying. It may involve:

- false statements;
- strategic omission;
- concealment;
- fabricated explanations;
- manipulating evidence;
- maintaining incompatible stories for different people.

Deception becomes especially consequential when it interferes with another person's capacity to make informed choices about sex, money, exclusivity, health, safety or continued participation in the relationship.

### 4.11 Incompatibility

**Incompatibility** exists when important goals, values, identities, commitments or life arrangements cannot all be realised within the same relationship structure under available constraints.

Incompatibility is not simply "fighting a lot."

A highly distressed couple may be compatible but trapped in a destructive cycle. A courteous and emotionally skilled couple may nevertheless discover a substantive incompatibility.

### 4.12 Aggression

**Aggression** is behaviour intended, or functionally operating, to attack, intimidate, dominate, punish, injure or force the partner.

It may be:

- verbal;
- psychological;
- physical;
- sexual;
- financial;
- property-directed.

Aggression requires a safety formulation in addition to a relationship formulation.

### 4.13 Coercive control

**Coercive control** is a pattern in which one partner progressively constrains the other's autonomy through domination, intimidation, surveillance, threats, isolation, restriction of resources, exploitation of dependency or similar means.

The core issue is not simply the frequency of arguments. It is the organisation of the relationship around **power, fear and restricted freedom**.

Psychological aggression described in the *Clinical Handbook* includes domination, threats and restriction of freedom or access to resources, illustrating why control cannot be reduced to ordinary conflict.

### 4.14 Abuse

**Abuse** is a broader category describing harmful patterns in which another person's bodily, sexual, psychological or material integrity is violated.

Abuse may include:

- violence;
- coercive control;
- intimidation;
- sexual coercion;
- repeated degradation;
- stalking;
- threats;
- economic restriction;
- retaliatory conduct.

Not every angry exchange is abuse. Conversely, abuse must not be neutralised by describing it merely as a communication problem or reciprocal cycle.

## 5. What exactly has ruptured?

"Trust was broken" is often accurate but insufficiently specific.

A useful formulation separates at least nine possible rupture domains.

### 5.1 Factual reality

The first rupture can be epistemic:

> **Can I rely on my understanding of what has actually been happening?**

Deception can create two incompatible realities:

- the publicly shared relationship;
- the concealed relationship or concealed behaviour.

This is especially important after affairs, financial deception or sustained secrecy. The injured partner may not only grieve the act but discover that earlier memories must be reinterpreted.

Repair therefore begins partly with restoring a workable factual world.

This does not mean every detail must be exhaustively reconstructed. It means that the relationship cannot be rebuilt around continuing material falsification.

### 5.2 Agreement

The rupture may concern a specific commitment.

Questions include:

- What was actually agreed?
- Was the agreement explicit or assumed?
- Was it mutually understood?
- Was it still operative?
- Was renegotiation possible but avoided?
- Was the other partner allowed to make decisions on false premises?

Agreement analysis matters because the same behaviour can have different relational meanings in different couples.

### 5.3 Consent and autonomy

A rupture can concern the capacity to choose freely.

If relevant information was concealed, ask whether the concealment deprived the other partner of meaningful choice.

Examples:

- undisclosed sexual risk;
- concealed debt;
- secret decisions affecting shared property;
- continued sexual contact under materially false assumptions about exclusivity;
- pressure or coercion around sex.

Here the central issue is not simply emotional hurt. It is compromised autonomy.

### 5.4 Emotional recognition

A rupture may involve the experience:

> **My experience did not matter to you, or could not enter your mind.**

This is the domain in which validation, empathy and mutual recognition become important.

Ringstrom's relational model centres on self-experience, mutual recognition versus mutual negation, and the capacity to negotiate disparate subjectivities rather than forcing one reality to dominate the other.

Recognition does not require agreement with every belief. It requires treating the other person as a separate centre of experience.

### 5.5 Attachment security

The rupture may concern availability at a moment when the partner was relied upon as a source of security.

The question becomes:

> **When I was frightened, grieving, exposed, ill or dependent, were you there in the way I expected an attachment figure to be?**

This is the domain most relevant to attachment injury.

### 5.6 Predictive trust

Trust is forward-looking.

The rupture asks:

> **What should I now predict you will do when similar circumstances recur?**

This is why explanations alone cannot restore trust.

### 5.7 Identity and dignity

Some ruptures alter how a partner experiences the self:

- humiliated;
- replaceable;
- sexually undesirable;
- foolish;
- powerless;
- unseen;
- controlled;
- morally compromised;
- reduced to a function rather than recognised as a person.

Greenberg and Goldman's couple model explicitly treats attachment, identity validation and attraction as distinct motivational domains. An event can therefore injure the attachment bond, identity, erotic bond or several at once.

### 5.8 Practical partnership

The rupture may concern whether the couple can function as a cooperative unit.

Examples:

- repeated financial irresponsibility;
- unilateral parenting decisions;
- failure to provide agreed caregiving;
- chronic unreliability;
- hidden substance use;
- recurring non-performance of shared obligations.

Emotional warmth cannot substitute indefinitely for operational reliability.

### 5.9 Shared future

Finally, rupture can destabilise the couple's assumed trajectory:

> **Are we still building the same life?**

An affair, decision about children, relocation, major religious change, altered sexual agreement, caregiving demand or financial event can expose a difference that had previously remained latent.

Sometimes repair restores a shared future.

Sometimes repair makes it possible to recognise that there is no longer one.

## 6. Responsibility: what belongs where?

Couple theory often emphasises reciprocity because one partner's behaviour affects the other. This is useful for understanding interaction. It becomes dangerous when reciprocity is used to erase agency.

A more disciplined formulation separates different forms of responsibility.

### 6.1 Responsibility for the act

The person who performs an action is responsible for that action.

Examples:

- the person who lies is responsible for the lie;
- the person who has an affair is responsible for participating in the affair;
- the person who strikes the other is responsible for the assault;
- the person who secretly spends shared funds is responsible for that spending;
- the person who stalks or retaliates is responsible for that conduct.

Relationship dissatisfaction may be jointly generated. A specific deceptive or aggressive act is not therefore equally authored.

### 6.2 Responsibility for the relationship context

Both partners may have contributed to a relationship environment involving:

- distance;
- sexual dissatisfaction;
- unresolved resentment;
- poor conflict management;
- avoidance;
- loneliness;
- rigid roles;
- failures of responsiveness.

This context can matter greatly to explanation and future change.

But:

> **relationship contribution does not retroactively redistribute authorship of a unilateral breach.**

The affair treatment literature separates exploration of the couple context from the participating partner's conduct precisely because explanation is necessary without making the injured partner responsible for the choice to betray.

### 6.3 Responsibility for the cycle

In ordinary distressed interactions, both partners may participate in a reciprocal cycle.

For example:

1. A seeks reassurance.
2. B experiences pressure and withdraws.
3. A interprets withdrawal as abandonment and escalates.
4. B experiences escalation as attack and withdraws further.

Both actions help maintain the loop.

This does not mean each action is equivalent in impact, frequency or severity.

### 6.4 Responsibility for impact

No one can fully control another person's reaction.

Nevertheless, responsibility includes willingness to encounter foreseeable consequences of one's own conduct.

A person need not agree with every interpretation of the injured partner in order to say:

- this happened;
- I chose this;
- it affected you;
- the effect is intelligible;
- I will not require you to minimise that impact in order to protect me from discomfort.

### 6.5 Responsibility for repair

Repair responsibilities may be asymmetric.

After a unilateral betrayal, the person who violated the agreement may reasonably carry more responsibility for:

- factual clarification;
- ending ongoing deception;
- acknowledging the breach;
- tolerating understandable mistrust;
- making requested behavioural changes that are freely negotiated;
- demonstrating reliability over time.

The injured partner retains responsibility for:

- describing needs where possible;
- not using the injury as unlimited permission for unrelated aggression;
- deciding which risks they are or are not willing to take;
- determining whether continued participation remains acceptable.

These responsibilities are different.

### 6.6 Responsibility for safety

Violence, sexual coercion, stalking and intimidation must not be formulated as merely symmetrical communication failures.

The target's fear, withdrawal, appeasement, concealment or defensive behaviour may be highly relevant to understanding the system, but such responses do not create equal responsibility for the coercive behaviour.

### 6.7 Responsibility for the relationship decision

No partner owes reconciliation because the other has apologised, changed or completed therapy.

Likewise, one partner does not require the other's consent to decide that they no longer wish to continue an intimate relationship.

The therapeutic task can be to make the decision process clearer without manufacturing consensus.

## 7. Attachment injury: construct, research lineage and limits

### 7.1 What qualifies

The attachment-injury construct emerged within Emotionally Focused Therapy to describe a particular kind of relationship impasse.

Millikin's study operationalised an attachment injury as a specific incident in which one partner betrayed or broke the other's trust and the incident became a recurring clinical theme and stuck point.

Makinen and Johnson later described attachment injuries as perceived abandonment, betrayal or breach of trust occurring in a critical moment of need, becoming a recurring reference point for judging the other partner's dependability.

The construct therefore has several components:

1. **a significant incident or bounded episode;**
2. **attachment relevance**, often involving vulnerability or need;
3. **a perceived failure of availability, responsiveness, protection or loyalty;**
4. **changed meaning of the partner's dependability;**
5. **recurrence**, with the event repeatedly entering later conflicts;
6. **an impasse**, because normal repair has not metabolised the event.

A painful event that is discussed once and repaired is not necessarily an attachment injury.

A recurring disagreement about housekeeping is not automatically an attachment injury.

An affair can become an attachment injury, but "affair" and "attachment injury" are not synonyms.

Chronic coercive control should not be compressed into an attachment-injury formulation. The safety and power structure is more fundamental.

### 7.2 Why the event becomes a stuck point

The injured partner is not necessarily repeating the event because they simply refuse to "let go."

From an attachment perspective, the unresolved event continues to function as evidence.

The question underneath recurrence may be:

> **When I most need you, what evidence do I have that you will be there?**

The event becomes a benchmark against which later behaviour is interpreted.

A minor present-day disappointment can therefore reactivate the older event because the present event is not experienced in isolation. It is read through an unresolved prediction:

> **Here it is again. The same thing is true. I cannot depend on you.**

Meanwhile, the other partner may experience repeated reference to the injury as accusation, punishment or proof that nothing they do will ever be enough. Defensive withdrawal then becomes more likely.

The result can be a loop:

**injury reminder → protest/blame → defensiveness/minimisation → increased alarm → stronger protest → greater withdrawal**

The original injury and the current cycle then reinforce one another.

### 7.3 Millikin: discovery work

Millikin's dissertation is best treated as the **discovery phase** of the research programme.

Its task-analysis model was developed with three couples who were described as mildly to moderately distressed. The study sought to identify attachment injuries and construct a preliminary change model, explicitly characterising the findings as proposed and preliminary.

This evidentiary status matters.

Millikin can support:

- the historical development of the construct;
- hypotheses about markers and phases;
- description of the discovery-oriented task-analysis process.

Millikin cannot, by itself, establish:

- prevalence of attachment injuries;
- universal causal mechanisms;
- effectiveness across severe distress;
- suitability of the process for coercive or violent relationships;
- necessity of every proposed step.

An important discovery from the preliminary work was that the injury might appear early in assessment, but productive work on it became possible only after the negative interactional cycle had been sufficiently de-escalated. Makinen and Johnson explicitly identify Millikin's work as preliminary exploratory task analysis and describe de-escalation as a precondition for effective intervention into the injury.

### 7.4 Makinen and Johnson: verification

Makinen and Johnson's 2006 study represents the stronger empirical step.

Twenty-four couples completed treatment; 15 were classified as resolved and nine as unresolved. The resolved couples displayed more affiliative interaction and deeper emotional experiencing and improved more on dyadic satisfaction and forgiveness.

The study's resolution model can be reconstructed as follows.

### Initial injury marker

The injured partner describes the event as emotionally alive rather than as neutral history.

The other partner often responds by:

- defending;
- minimising;
- discounting;
- explaining;
- withdrawing.

### Differentiation of affect and meaning

The injured partner begins to move beneath global accusation toward the specific impact and attachment meaning.

The other partner begins to understand what the event represented.

### Vulnerable re-engagement

The injured partner becomes able to expose grief, fear, loss or vulnerability associated with the event.

This is different from merely intensifying blame.

### Responsibility and responsiveness

The partner associated with the injury becomes more emotionally engaged and can:

- acknowledge their part;
- express empathy;
- communicate regret or remorse;
- remain present with the injured partner's pain.

### New attachment interaction

The injured partner risks asking for comfort, caring or responsiveness.

The other partner responds differently from the original injurious moment.

The complete eight-step research model moves from emotionally charged injury, through differentiated meaning and responsibility, toward a new request-and-response event.

This is not merely an apology sequence. It is a change in the interaction around vulnerability and responsiveness.

### 7.5 Forgiveness in the attachment-injury literature

Makinen and Johnson place "forgiveness and reconciliation" near the end of their model. Their resolved group also showed greater improvement on forgiveness measures.

For this corpus, however, the terminology must be decompressed.

The study supports an association between successful task resolution and increased forgiveness in that treatment context.

It does **not** establish that:

- forgiveness is morally required;
- reconciliation must follow forgiveness;
- full trust has been restored;
- the couple must remain together;
- similar processes apply under coercive control;
- a person who remains hurt has "failed" treatment.

The empirical model should therefore be treated as an attachment-injury **resolution process**, not as a universal moral sequence.

### 7.6 What the attachment-injury evidence does not justify

Do not use this literature to claim:

- every betrayal is an attachment injury;
- every attachment injury is traumatic in a formal diagnostic sense;
- every injured partner needs the same form of emotional enactment;
- an apology without behavioural change constitutes resolution;
- attachment injury treatment is appropriate under ongoing violence or coercion;
- successful emotional reconnection proves relationship viability.

The construct is useful precisely because it is narrower than "hurt."

## 8. Trust: attunement, betrayal, repair and repeated reliability

### 8.1 Trust is not a feeling alone

Trust contains affective, cognitive and behavioural components.

A person may feel affectionate while remaining uncertain about reliability.

A person may believe a partner is generally well intentioned while not trusting them with money.

A person may trust a partner as a parent but not as a sexual partner.

Trust is therefore domain-specific and forward-looking.

At minimum it asks:

> **Given what I know, what should I reasonably expect this person to do when my interests are vulnerable to their choices?**

### 8.2 Gottman's interactional conception

Gottman's trust framework treats trust and betrayal as emerging from repeated interdependent choices rather than existing only as global personality traits.

In the associated Gottman couple model, trust is operationalised in terms of having the partner's interests in mind, while betrayal moves interaction toward a zero-sum structure. Attunement is then described as listening to and processing the partner's negative emotion nondefensively and empathically.

The mathematical operationalisation is model-specific. It should not be converted into a claim that all human trust is reducible to a single numerical metric.

The broader clinical insight is more portable:

> **Trust is accumulated through repeated moments in which one person's vulnerability enters the other's decision-making.**

### 8.3 Attunement

Attunement means more than agreement.

It includes:

- noticing emotional information;
- asking rather than assuming;
- listening;
- remaining nondefensive long enough to understand;
- communicating understanding;
- allowing the other's experience to influence one's response.

Attunement does not require surrendering one's own standpoint.

Two partners can say:

- "I understand what happened for you."
- "My own understanding is different."

That is more differentiated than insisting that only one subjectivity can exist.

### 8.4 Conflict

Trust does not require conflict-free relationships.

Conflict can coexist with substantial trust when partners believe:

- disagreement will not become humiliation;
- autonomy will be respected;
- violence will not occur;
- important agreements will not be secretly abandoned;
- repair remains possible;
- the other's interests still matter.

Conversely, a superficially calm relationship can contain low trust if silence is maintained by fear, disengagement or concealed behaviour.

### 8.5 Repair

Gottman-oriented work treats early and effective repair as central to relationship functioning. Stable couples in the cited research initiated repair before negativity had accumulated as far, and repair effectiveness was related to the broader emotional climate of the relationship.

This supports an important distinction:

> **A repair statement does not operate independently of relationship history.**

The same "I'm sorry" may be received differently after:

- years of reliability;
- repeated breaches;
- ongoing deception;
- prompt voluntary accountability;
- coerced confession;
- successful past repair;
- chronic failed promises.

Repair is therefore relationally contextual.

### 8.6 Repeated reliability

Trust restoration requires a time series.

Possible evidence includes:

- commitments are kept;
- deviations are disclosed rather than hidden;
- questions are answered without renewed deception;
- agreed third-party boundaries are maintained;
- financial commitments are honoured;
- sexual agreements are respected;
- the partner responds consistently in moments of need;
- mistakes are disclosed and repaired earlier;
- defensiveness decreases;
- reality is no longer continually destabilised.

None of these requires perfection.

What matters is whether the pattern increasingly supports a different prediction.

### 8.7 Understanding versus trusting

The distinction can be stated simply:

> **"I understand why you did it" is a statement about meaning.**

> **"I can trust you again" is a statement about prediction.**

Understanding can emerge in one conversation.

Trust usually cannot.

A developmental explanation may make behaviour coherent:

- fear of rejection;
- conflict avoidance;
- shame;
- desire for validation;
- unmet sexual needs;
- family-of-origin patterns;
- attraction to novelty;
- difficulty tolerating dependence.

That explanation may reduce bewilderment.

It does not establish what the person will do next time the same pressures arise.

That requires behavioural evidence.

### 8.8 Trust update

A useful synthesis is:

**old prediction + new evidence over time → revised trust judgment**

The update may be:

- upward;
- downward;
- domain-specific;
- conditional;
- incomplete;
- suspended.

Trust restoration is not an obligation owed in exchange for remorse.

Nor is permanent mistrust necessarily the only rational response after betrayal.

The relevant issue is whether enough new evidence exists to update the prediction.

## 9. Affairs and betrayal

### 9.1 Start with the agreement, not with a universal definition of fidelity

Couples differ in their arrangements.

Possible structures include:

- monogamy;
- consensual non-monogamy;
- negotiated sexual openness;
- asymmetrical agreements;
- relationship-specific rules regarding pornography, online interaction or former partners.

The clinical question is therefore:

> **What did these partners understand themselves to be consenting to?**

Gabb and Fink's treatment of contemporary couple relationships includes non-monogamies alongside other ordinary relational practices, reinforcing the importance of not assuming one universal couple structure.

### 9.2 Discovery versus disclosure

The pathway by which an affair becomes known can matter.

Voluntary disclosure communicates something different from:

- accidental discovery;
- third-party exposure;
- repeated denial followed by proof;
- partial disclosure followed by further discoveries.

This is because a betrayal can contain at least two injuries:

1. the extradyadic involvement;
2. the deception surrounding it.

The second may continue after the first has ended.

### 9.3 Competing realities

Secrecy produces a structural problem in shared reality.

The involved partner may have lived with information unavailable to the other.

The injured partner may retrospectively discover that:

- explanations were false;
- absences had different meanings;
- apparent choices were made without relevant information;
- memories now look different.

This can produce a distinctive form of uncertainty:

> **Which parts of my past understanding remain trustworthy?**

Repair therefore requires more than reassurance about the future. It usually requires enough factual clarity to establish what relationship actually existed.

### 9.4 What disclosure is for

Disclosure should not be conceptualised as unlimited extraction of detail.

Its core functions are:

- restore a sufficiently accurate account of events;
- end active deception;
- allow informed decision-making;
- establish whether the outside relationship is continuing;
- clarify sexual-health or financial implications where relevant;
- reduce the destabilising effects of serial discovery.

The *Clinical Handbook* notes that open discussion and responding to the injured partner's questions can contribute to predictability and control, while acknowledging that the evidence on the optimal form of disclosure is limited.

This argues for **relevant truth**, not compulsive detail.

### 9.5 "Drip disclosure"

Repeated emergence of material facts can restart the injury process.

Each new discovery may alter:

- the factual timeline;
- the meaning of previous statements;
- the estimated scale of deception;
- the injured partner's prediction of future honesty.

The problem is therefore not simply that "the partner keeps revisiting the affair." The factual object being processed may still be changing.

### 9.6 Accountability

The most important distinction is between:

- **why the relationship was vulnerable**, and
- **who chose the affair or deception**.

Stage 2 of the affair treatment model examines multiple contributing domains, including relationship factors, external context, characteristics of the participating partner and characteristics of the injured partner.

That exploration should not be misread as equalising responsibility for the affair.

A useful formulation is:

> **Both partners may need to understand the relationship. Only the person who enacted the breach can take responsibility for enacting the breach.**

### 9.7 Impact before interpretation

Premature explanation often sounds like excuse.

For example:

- "I was lonely."
- "We had not had sex."
- "You were always working."
- "I needed validation."

These statements may later become relevant.

But if offered before acknowledgment, they can function defensively:

> **"What I did was caused by what you failed to give me."**

The repair task ordinarily requires first establishing:

- what happened;
- what was concealed;
- what agreement was breached;
- what impact followed;
- what responsibility belongs to the actor.

Only then can broader meaning be explored without collapsing accountability.

### 9.8 Stage structure in the affair-treatment literature

The *Clinical Handbook*'s affair treatment chapter uses three broad stages:

1. dealing with impact;
2. finding meaning;
3. moving on.

The first stage includes assessment, boundary setting, affect regulation, exploration of impact and management of intrusive reactions. The second explores the broader context and contributing factors. The third addresses formulation, forgiveness-related questions and future relationship decisions.

The stages are explicitly described as non-linear.

That matters. A new disclosure can move the couple back from meaning-making to factual impact. A new boundary breach can reopen questions that seemed settled.

### 9.9 Third-party contact

If the outside person remains in the environment because of:

- employment;
- shared parenting;
- community;
- family;
- unavoidable practical contact,

"zero contact" may not be feasible.

What matters is clarity about:

- what contact is necessary;
- what is voluntary;
- what is disclosed;
- what communication channels are used;
- what boundaries are mutually agreed;
- whether those agreements are subsequently honoured.

An arrangement based on involuntary surveillance is different from one based on voluntarily accepted transparency.

### 9.10 Rebuilding trust

Trust rebuilding after an affair should not be equated with demanding that the injured partner "stop checking" or "move on."

The affair-treatment chapter recognises that forgiveness and recommitment can precede full trust restoration and describes trust rebuilding as involving manageable risks followed by evidence of trustworthy conduct.

The core logic is:

**risk → observed behaviour → updated prediction**

Trust cannot be commanded.

### 9.11 Uncertainty

An affair often creates pressure for immediate certainty:

- Are we staying together?
- Is the affair definitely over?
- Can I ever trust again?
- Do you still love me?
- Was our whole relationship false?

Some questions may eventually be answerable.

Others require time.

The demand for premature certainty can itself become destabilising.

A legitimate therapeutic position is:

> **The facts can be clarified before the future is decided.**

### 9.12 Relationship decision-making

The affair-treatment literature itself does not require relationship continuation. Its "moving on" phase includes evaluation of relationship viability and, where separation is chosen, a shift toward less acrimonious separation.

The chapter also describes its core reconstruction work as potentially useful even if the couple does not remain together.

Therefore:

- repairing the narrative can matter even if the couple separates;
- understanding the affair can matter even without forgiveness;
- forgiveness can occur without reunion;
- reunion can occur before trust is complete;
- ending can follow responsible repair.

## 10. Boundaries without generic "boundary language"

"Boundaries" becomes clinically vague if it means merely "anything I dislike."

A more rigorous model differentiates several constructs.

### 10.1 Personal boundary

A **personal boundary** concerns what a person permits regarding their own:

- body;
- time;
- property;
- attention;
- private information;
- participation;
- contact;
- exposure.

A boundary usually specifies one's own action under specified conditions.

For example:

- "I will not continue a conversation while I am being threatened."
- "I do not consent to sexual contact when I have said no."
- "I will not combine finances unless debts are disclosed."

This differs from claiming unlimited authority over the other person's choices.

### 10.2 Relationship agreement

A **relationship agreement** is jointly negotiated.

Examples:

- sexual exclusivity;
- rules for outside sexual relationships;
- financial thresholds;
- childcare responsibilities;
- disclosure requirements;
- contact with former partners;
- substance-related commitments.

A broken agreement raises questions of accountability even if neither partner uses the word "boundary."

### 10.3 Autonomy

Intimacy does not eliminate separateness.

A viable couple requires some combination of:

- interdependence;
- separateness;
- shared decision-making;
- individual agency.

Morgan's "couple state of mind" explicitly includes the capacity to observe the relationship while maintaining each partner's subjective position. Her account treats the relationship as psychologically meaningful without dissolving the individuals into it.

Autonomy therefore does not mean "I owe my partner nothing."

Nor does intimacy mean "my partner is entitled to total access."

### 10.4 Privacy

**Privacy** protects legitimate selfhood.

Possible private domains include:

- private reflection;
- therapy;
- conversations with friends;
- personal correspondence;
- memories;
- individual interests;
- bodily privacy.

The legitimacy of privacy depends on context.

A private conversation with a friend is not equivalent to concealing an affair.

### 10.5 Secrecy

**Secrecy** becomes relationally significant when information is deliberately withheld because disclosure would affect the other person's:

- consent;
- interpretation of an agreement;
- health;
- financial decisions;
- sexual decisions;
- willingness to continue the relationship.

The difference between privacy and secrecy is therefore not simply whether information is undisclosed.

The question is:

> **What legitimate decision-making function is the concealment preventing?**

### 10.6 Consent

Consent is not created permanently by relationship status.

Sexual consent requires continuing respect for the person's present willingness.

Relevant distinctions include:

- desire;
- willingness;
- compliance;
- fear-based acquiescence;
- pressure;
- coercion.

A partner engaging in sex to avoid punishment is not equivalent to freely choosing sex.

Relationship therapy must therefore distinguish sexual discrepancy from sexual coercion.

### 10.7 Sexual boundaries

Sexual boundaries can concern:

- sexual acts;
- contraception;
- safer-sex practices;
- pornography;
- online sexual interaction;
- third-party sexual contact;
- sexual disclosure;
- privacy;
- frequency;
- initiation;
- refusal;
- consensual non-monogamy.

The *Clinical Handbook*'s sex-therapy chapter emphasises that sexual difficulties exist within biological, psychological, relational and broader systems rather than being reducible to one partner's isolated "dysfunction."

This systemic stance should not be used to dilute consent. Reciprocity in sexual functioning does not make responsibility for coercion reciprocal.

### 10.8 Financial boundaries

Financial rupture can involve:

- secret debt;
- concealed accounts;
- unauthorised use of joint funds;
- gambling;
- financial dependency used as control;
- unilateral major purchases;
- inconsistent disclosure;
- refusal to provide agreed access to information.

A financial problem can therefore be:

- a disagreement;
- a broken agreement;
- deception;
- a betrayal;
- economic abuse;
- incompatibility around money.

Classification matters.

### 10.9 Emotional boundaries

Emotional intimacy outside the couple is not inherently disloyal.

Partners require other relationships.

Questions become more relevant when outside emotional contact:

- substitutes systematically for the primary relationship;
- becomes romantic or erotic;
- is hidden because it would clearly breach an agreement;
- involves denigration of the partner;
- includes commitments incompatible with the primary relationship;
- becomes the basis for an undisclosed parallel attachment.

The issue is not "emotional closeness with anyone else = betrayal."

It is the interaction among closeness, agreement, secrecy, sexual/romantic meaning and consequences.

### 10.10 Contact with third parties

Third-party boundaries may concern:

- former partners;
- affair partners;
- close friends;
- family;
- colleagues;
- online contacts;
- therapists;
- children.

A rigorous formulation asks:

1. What relationship exists?
2. What is its function?
3. What has been agreed?
4. What is concealed?
5. Does the third party have decision-making power inside the couple?
6. Is a child being recruited as confidant, messenger or ally?
7. Does contact materially undermine an explicit couple agreement?
8. Are proposed restrictions proportionate, consensual and time-limited, or are they becoming coercive?

### 10.11 Boundary repair

Boundary repair may involve:

- factual acknowledgment;
- restitution;
- changed access;
- a new agreement;
- temporary transparency;
- revised financial arrangements;
- changed contact with third parties;
- clearer consent practices;
- a decision not to resume a previous level of intimacy.

Repair does not necessarily mean restoring the previous boundary structure.

Sometimes the rupture demonstrates that the old arrangement was ambiguous or unworkable.

The new task is negotiation.

## 11. Dysfunctional cycle versus substantive incompatibility

### 11.1 The cycle hypothesis

A couple may appear incompatible because each person's protective strategy intensifies the other's.

Examples:

- pursuit ↔ withdrawal;
- criticism ↔ defensiveness;
- control ↔ resistance;
- reassurance-seeking ↔ distancing;
- protest ↔ shutdown.

If the cycle changes, the apparent disagreement may become manageable.

This is a process problem.

### 11.2 The incompatibility hypothesis

Sometimes the interaction improves but the underlying conflict remains.

Examples:

### Children

One partner wants children.

The other does not.

No amount of validation creates a jointly enactable middle position if both preferences remain firm.

### Monogamy and non-monogamy

One partner requires sexual exclusivity.

The other regards non-monogamy as essential to the relationship they can authentically sustain.

Better communication may clarify the conflict without removing it.

### Location

One partner's meaningful life depends on remaining near family.

The other's career or life goal requires relocation.

### Money

One partner requires substantial financial pooling.

The other requires financial independence.

Compromise may be possible, but not necessarily.

### Lifestyle

Differences may concern:

- urban versus rural life;
- travel;
- consumption;
- social life;
- work intensity;
- retirement;
- substance use.

### Sexuality

Differences may involve:

- sexual orientation;
- erotic interests;
- preferred frequency;
- sexual practices;
- importance placed on sex;
- consensual relationship structure.

Not every sexual difference is negotiable.

### Religion

Religious difference becomes substantively incompatible when it affects non-negotiable commitments such as:

- child-rearing;
- community;
- marriage expectations;
- ritual obligations;
- conversion.

### Caregiving

Partners may differ fundamentally about responsibilities toward:

- children;
- ageing parents;
- disabled relatives;
- former partners;
- extended family.

### Future goals

A couple can love one another while moving toward different futures.

### 11.3 Irresolvable does not mean pathological

Carr's review of Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy describes an explicit focus on partners' incompatibilities, empathic understanding, tolerance and acceptance of some irresolvable differences, alongside behavioural change.

This is a useful corrective to the assumption:

> **good therapy = every conflict gets solved**

Some differences must instead be:

- tolerated;
- managed;
- compartmentalised;
- periodically renegotiated;
- accepted as a cost of the relationship.

Others cannot be jointly enacted.

### 11.4 The critical distinction

Ask:

> **If the couple could discuss this issue calmly, accurately and compassionately, would they then have a jointly workable arrangement?**

If yes, the primary obstacle may be process.

If no, substantive incompatibility remains.

This is not a decision algorithm. It is a formulation question.

### 11.5 Improved process can expose incompatibility

Therapy sometimes appears to "make a relationship worse" when it actually makes previously concealed differences speakable.

A quieter cycle may expose:

- one partner never wanted children;
- one partner no longer wants monogamy;
- one partner cannot accept the proposed relocation;
- one partner does not want the caregiving role expected of them.

In that situation, improved communication has worked.

It has not produced agreement.

## 12. Recognition, subjectivity and the relationship as a third position

### 12.1 Ringstrom: mutual recognition

Ringstrom places three broad themes at the centre of his relational couple model:

- actualisation of self-experience;
- mutual recognition versus mutual negation;
- the relationship developing a psychological organisation of its own.

This is useful for rupture because injury often collapses the capacity to hold two subjectivities simultaneously.

One person says:

> "You betrayed me."

The other experiences:

> "Everything I am has now been reduced to the worst thing I did."

Each may then require the other to surrender subjectivity as the price of being heard.

Repair requires a more complex position:

- the injured person's experience is real;
- the other's explanation can also become intelligible;
- explanation does not cancel responsibility;
- responsibility does not erase personhood.

### 12.2 Recognition is not exoneration

Mutual recognition does not mean symmetrical blame.

It means that even asymmetrical responsibility occurs between two subjects rather than between a pure victim-object and a pure offender-object.

That distinction matters particularly after betrayal.

The person who caused the injury can be understood without the injury being minimised.

### 12.3 Morgan: couple state of mind

Morgan's Tavistock formulation adds a "third" position from which the relationship itself can be observed.

Instead of only:

- my pain;
- your defence;

the couple can become able to think:

> **What is happening between us?**

This is valuable for recurring rupture because the cycle becomes an object of thought rather than something the partners can only enact.

### 12.4 The limit of systemic language

The idea that "the relationship has a mind of its own" is useful only if it does not erase concrete actions.

The sentence:

> "Our relationship produced an affair"

is too diffuse.

The sentence:

> "Our relationship had become distant, and one partner responded by entering and concealing an outside relationship"

preserves both system and agency.

Similarly:

> "We have a violent cycle"

may be dangerously imprecise if one person is coercively controlling the other.

Systems thinking is useful.

Responsibility still requires actors.

## 13. Safety changes the formulation

Safety is not an additional topic appended after ordinary couple work.

It can change whether ordinary couple work is appropriate at all.

### 13.1 Situational aggression

**Situational aggression** describes aggression occurring in particular conflicts rather than as part of an overarching system of coercive domination.

It may involve:

- shouting;
- insulting;
- pushing;
- slapping;
- throwing objects;
- threatening gestures.

"Situational" does not mean harmless.

It also does not mean both partners are equally aggressive.

Severity, fear, injury and escalation still require assessment.

**Blocking or preventing a partner from leaving requires specific coercion and safety assessment and should not be presumed to be ordinary situational aggression.**

The *Clinical Handbook* distinguishes mild-to-moderate situational aggression from severe violence and describes conjoint approaches only for selected cases after structured screening.

Carr's evidence review similarly concludes that there is evidence for couple-based intervention in selected mild-to-moderate situational intimate partner violence, not for severe forms.

### 13.2 Coercive control

Coercive control is different.

Relevant signals include patterns of:

- domination;
- intimidation;
- surveillance;
- isolation;
- restriction of money;
- control over movement;
- threats;
- sexual coercion;
- punishment for disclosure;
- threats involving children;
- forced access to accounts or communications;
- stalking;
- retaliation after attempts to set limits or separate.

The organising principle is loss of freedom under threat.

A "communication skills" formulation is inadequate.

### 13.3 Severe violence

Severe violence raises questions of immediate risk rather than relational attunement.

Relevant factors include:

- significant injury;
- escalation in severity;
- strangulation;
- weapons;
- threats to kill;
- forced sex;
- repeated assault;
- violence during pregnancy;
- credible threats following separation;
- severe substance-related disinhibition;
- stalking;
- destruction of property used to intimidate.

These features cannot be treated as merely high-intensity conflict.

### 13.4 Intimidation

A relationship may become unsafe without physical assault.

The clinically relevant question is:

> **Can each person speak, disagree, refuse and disclose without anticipating punishment?**

If not, apparent agreement may have little interpretive value.

### 13.5 Sexual coercion

Sexual coercion includes situations in which sexual participation is obtained through:

- threats;
- intimidation;
- persistent pressure after refusal;
- exploitation of fear or dependency;
- punishment for saying no.

The *Clinical Handbook*'s aggression assessment explicitly includes sexual coercion among behaviours requiring screening.

Sexual dissatisfaction and sexual coercion therefore belong to different formulations.

### 13.6 Stalking

Stalking should not be reframed as attachment protest.

Repeated unwanted:

- following;
- surveillance;
- digital tracking;
- appearing at locations;
- monitoring;
- messaging;
- third-party information gathering

after contact has been refused raises a safety issue.

Attachment language may describe subjective fear of loss.

It does not transform stalking into acceptable relational behaviour.

### 13.7 Retaliation

One reason conjoint therapy can be unsafe is that information disclosed in the room may be punished later.

The *Clinical Handbook* warns against eliciting complaints conjointly when the therapist cannot estimate how disclosure will be interpreted or whether retaliation may occur afterward.

This is a fundamental limit on vulnerability-oriented therapy.

### 13.8 Private assessment

When aggression is possible, assessment cannot rely only on what partners disclose in one another's presence.

The *Clinical Handbook* recommends structured screening and separate interviews, partly because the target of aggression may not feel safe disclosing it conjointly.

Assessment should distinguish:

- frequency;
- severity;
- injury;
- fear;
- coercive pattern;
- retaliation risk;
- sexual coercion;
- substance involvement;
- weapons;
- whether either partner is afraid to participate honestly in conjoint treatment.

### 13.9 When conjoint vulnerability work may be unsafe

Vulnerability-oriented couple work may be unsafe when one partner cannot disclose honestly without credible fear of consequences.

Examples include:

- ongoing coercive control;
- severe or escalating violence;
- threats;
- stalking;
- sexual coercion;
- credible retaliation;
- severe intimidation;
- current danger;
- serious substance-related violence risk;
- surveillance that continues outside therapy.

In such conditions, asking a partner to become more emotionally vulnerable may expose information that can be weaponised.

"Both partners need to soften" is not an adequate response to coercion.

### 13.10 Conditions under which conjoint work has been studied

The aggression chapter limits conjoint interventions to selected cases and describes ongoing safety monitoring. It recommends shifting away from joint sessions when danger cannot be adequately controlled.

The evidence therefore supports a bounded conclusion:

> **Some carefully screened situational aggression can be addressed conjointly. This does not establish the safety of conjoint therapy for coercive control or severe violence.**

### 13.11 Safety overrides the ordinary repair sequence

Where safety is uncertain, the sequence is not:

**vulnerability → empathy → closeness**

It is:

**risk assessment → protection from retaliation → appropriate treatment format → only then relational work if safe**

## 14. Repair: what it actually requires

Repair is not a single emotional event.

It is a multi-domain process.

A useful synthesis distinguishes at least seven repair dimensions.

| Repair dimension | Core question |
|---|---|
| **factual repair** | Do we have a workable account of what occurred? |
| **responsibility repair** | Has each person taken ownership of what is actually theirs? |
| **emotional repair** | Has the lived impact been recognised and metabolised rather than continually invalidated? |
| **behavioural repair** | Has behaviour materially changed? |
| **agreement repair** | Are future expectations explicit and viable? |
| **trust repair** | Does accumulated evidence justify a different prediction? |
| **relational repair** | Can the partners again interact with sufficient recognition, safety and freedom? |

These dimensions can move at different speeds.

### 14.1 Factual acknowledgment

Before deep interpretation, establish what is sufficiently knowable.

Questions include:

- What occurred?
- What is agreed as fact?
- What remains disputed?
- What was concealed?
- What agreement was in place?
- What is genuinely unknown?

Repair does not require forced consensus about every interpretation.

It does require distinguishing facts from meanings.

### 14.2 Impact acknowledgment

Impact includes more than emotion.

Possible consequences include:

- grief;
- fear;
- humiliation;
- sleep disruption;
- hypervigilance;
- sexual withdrawal;
- financial consequences;
- altered self-concept;
- changed parenting;
- loss of future assumptions.

**Intention ≠ impact.**

A person can say:

> "I did not intend that consequence."

and also:

> "That consequence occurred."

### 14.3 Responsibility

Useful responsibility is specific.

Less useful:

> "I'm terrible."

More useful:

> "I said X, concealed Y, and broke the agreement we had about Z."

Global self-condemnation can paradoxically move emotional care back toward the person who caused the rupture.

Specific ownership keeps attention on conduct.

### 14.4 Acknowledgment

Acknowledgment combines:

- factual ownership;
- recognition of impact;
- willingness to hear the other;
- absence of immediate counterattack.

It does not require agreement with every downstream interpretation.

### 14.5 Emotional contact

When safe, deeper emotional contact can help move the interaction beyond prosecution and defence.

Relevant emotions may include:

- grief;
- fear;
- shame;
- loneliness;
- helplessness;
- sadness;
- longing.

Greenberg and Goldman argue that couple change involves both empathic responsiveness from the other and increased capacity for self-regulation.

This is important because repair should not require one partner to become the other's entire regulatory system.

### 14.6 Meaning

Only after sufficient acknowledgment should the couple ask:

- Why did this become possible?
- What did the event mean to each person?
- Which vulnerabilities were activated?
- What relationship conditions contributed?
- What individual history contributed?
- What contextual pressures mattered?
- What remains unexplained?

Meaning is valuable.

Meaning is not acquittal.

### 14.7 Behavioural change

Behavioural change is where the repair claim becomes testable.

Examples:

- no further deception;
- agreed financial disclosure;
- different conflict behaviour;
- treatment of a substance problem;
- changed contact with a third party;
- improved follow-through;
- respect for sexual refusal;
- adherence to co-parenting boundaries;
- ending intimidation.

**Remorse ≠ behavioural change.**

### 14.8 Boundary or agreement

A repaired relationship cannot rely indefinitely on vague hope.

It may require a new explicit agreement.

Questions include:

- What is being promised?
- What is not being promised?
- What counts as a breach?
- What happens if circumstances change?
- How will renegotiation occur before rather than after unilateral action?

### 14.9 Repeated reliability

Behavioural change once is evidence.

Behavioural change repeatedly is stronger evidence.

This is why trust restoration has a temporal component.

### 14.10 Trust update

The injured partner eventually decides, implicitly or explicitly, what the accumulated evidence now supports.

Possible conclusions include:

- trust has substantially returned;
- trust has returned in some domains but not others;
- trust remains conditional;
- insufficient time has passed;
- behaviour remains inconsistent;
- the relationship can continue despite incomplete trust;
- the relationship will end despite meaningful repair.

All are conceptually possible.

## 15. The applied repair map

The reusable sequence for this corpus is:

```text
RUPTURE
   ↓
IMPACT
   ↓
RESPONSIBILITY
   ↓
ACKNOWLEDGMENT
   ↓
EMOTIONAL CONTACT
   ↓
MEANING
   ↓
BEHAVIOURAL CHANGE
   ↓
BOUNDARY / AGREEMENT
   ↓
REPEATED RELIABILITY
   ↓
TRUST UPDATE
```

The sequence is deliberately more demanding than:

```text
HURT → APOLOGY → FORGIVENESS
```

### 15.1 RUPTURE

Specify the event or process.

Do not start with personality labels.

Ask:

- What happened?
- Was it one event or a pattern?
- Is it still happening?
- Which category best describes it?
- Is there a safety issue?

### 15.2 IMPACT

Map the consequences.

Separate:

- emotion;
- body;
- behaviour;
- meaning;
- practical consequences;
- safety;
- identity;
- attachment significance.

### 15.3 RESPONSIBILITY

Allocate responsibility at the correct level.

Distinguish:

- act responsibility;
- contextual contribution;
- cycle contribution;
- safety responsibility.

Do not force symmetry.

### 15.4 ACKNOWLEDGMENT

Establish whether each relevant person can recognise:

- facts;
- their part;
- foreseeable impact;
- the other's subjective experience.

### 15.5 EMOTIONAL CONTACT

Where safe, move beneath rehearsed accusation and defence.

This may involve:

- grief;
- fear;
- longing;
- shame;
- sadness.

It is not mandatory under unsafe conditions.

### 15.6 MEANING

Develop a fuller account.

Possible lenses include:

- attachment;
- emotion regulation;
- identity;
- relationship history;
- developmental learning;
- practical context;
- values;
- sexuality;
- power;
- external stress.

Keep explanation separate from excuse.

### 15.7 BEHAVIOURAL CHANGE

Ask what observable conduct must become different.

No repair model is complete without a behavioural hypothesis.

### 15.8 BOUNDARY / AGREEMENT

Translate learning into future structure.

Ambiguous rules often recreate old disputes.

### 15.9 REPEATED RELIABILITY

Observe whether the new structure survives:

- stress;
- disappointment;
- temptation;
- disagreement;
- time.

### 15.10 TRUST UPDATE

Do not prescribe the result.

Ask:

> **What does the accumulated evidence now justify believing?**

### 15.11 The map is not linear

The process loops.

New information may return the couple to factual rupture.

A failed agreement may return the couple to responsibility.

A successful emotional conversation may reveal an incompatibility.

A safety event can suspend the entire sequence.

An ending can occur before trust restoration.

A couple may also remain uncertain for a substantial period.

## 16. Apology, remorse, forgiveness, reconciliation and trust

These concepts are frequently collapsed.

They should be kept separate.

### 16.1 Apology

An apology is a communicative act acknowledging wrongdoing or harm.

A high-quality apology may include:

- specificity;
- ownership;
- recognition of impact;
- regret;
- lack of qualification;
- commitment to change.

But:

> **apology ≠ repair**

An apology can be sincere and still insufficient.

### 16.2 Remorse

Remorse is an emotional response to one's own conduct and its consequences.

It can motivate repair.

It is not proof that the behaviour will not recur.

Therefore:

> **remorse ≠ behavioural change**

### 16.3 Forgiveness

Forgiveness concerns the injured person's relationship to:

- resentment;
- blame;
- revenge;
- the offender;
- the injury's continuing hold on their life.

Forgiveness can mean different things across therapeutic, philosophical, cultural and religious traditions.

The corpus does not treat it as compulsory.

A person can:

- understand without forgiving;
- forgive without trusting;
- forgive without reconciling;
- reconcile before fully forgiving;
- separate without deciding the forgiveness question.

The affair-treatment chapter itself notes that some clients find "forgiveness" unhelpful and may work instead with concepts such as moving forward or letting go.

### 16.4 Reconciliation

Reconciliation is a relational event.

It means re-establishing some form of relationship after rupture.

It may involve:

- intimate partnership;
- friendship;
- cooperative co-parenting;
- civil contact;
- family relationship.

Reconciliation is therefore distinct from the internal process of forgiveness.

### 16.5 Trust restoration

Trust restoration is evidentiary.

It asks whether repeated observed behaviour now warrants a changed prediction.

It is possible to reconcile while still saying:

> "I am choosing to try again, but trust is not yet restored."

That is conceptually coherent.

### 16.6 Relationship preservation

The couple remains together.

That fact alone says nothing conclusive about:

- safety;
- satisfaction;
- forgiveness;
- trust;
- emotional repair;
- compatibility.

Relationship status is an outcome variable, not proof of therapeutic success.

---

## 17. Pseudo-repair and incomplete repair

Repair language can conceal continuing problems.

### 17.1 Apology without specificity

> "I'm sorry you were hurt."

This may acknowledge distress while avoiding authorship.

### 17.2 Explanation before acknowledgment

> "I only did it because we were disconnected."

The explanation may be relevant later.

Used too early, it relocates responsibility.

### 17.3 Remorse without changed behaviour

Repeated remorse followed by repeated breach provides information.

The relevant trust update should incorporate the recurrence.

### 17.4 Forgiveness pressure

Statements such as:

- "How long are you going to hold this over me?"
- "If you forgave me, you would stop asking."
- "Therapy says we need to move on."

can convert forgiveness into pressure for emotional compliance.

### 17.5 Reconciliation without repair

A couple may resume normal routines because:

- separation feels frightening;
- practical constraints are strong;
- children need immediate stability;
- sexual connection returns;
- acute distress fades.

None establishes that the underlying rupture has been repaired.

### 17.6 Surveillance presented as trust

Continuous monitoring may temporarily reduce uncertainty.

It is not identical to trust.

There is a difference between:

- consensual, temporary transparency designed to generate evidence;
- permanent coercive access to another person's communications.

### 17.7 Sex as proof of forgiveness

Renewed sexual contact does not prove:

- forgiveness;
- emotional safety;
- trust;
- resolution.

Sex may reflect desire, attachment, reassurance-seeking, habit, ambivalence or many other processes.

### 17.8 Silence as closure

The absence of discussion can reflect:

- genuine resolution;
- exhaustion;
- avoidance;
- fear;
- hopelessness;
- a decision to tolerate the unresolved issue.

"Nobody brings it up anymore" is therefore ambiguous.

---

## 18. Aggression and responsibility: why mutual cycles have limits

Couple models are built to identify reciprocal processes.

Violence and coercion expose the limits of that language.

### 18.1 Reciprocal sequence does not imply equivalent conduct

A sequence may be:

**criticism → withdrawal → louder criticism → insult → threat**

The fact that every behaviour has an antecedent does not mean every behaviour is equally permissible or consequential.

Antecedents explain sequence.

They do not erase thresholds of responsibility.

### 18.2 Provocation is not transfer of agency

A person can be:

- insulted;
- rejected;
- frightened;
- humiliated;
- jealous;
- intensely activated

and remain responsible for whether they use violence or coercion.

### 18.3 The safety question precedes the attachment question

A person may desperately fear abandonment.

That attachment fear may help explain:

- protest;
- pursuit;
- jealousy;
- panic.

It does not make:

- stalking;
- assault;
- sexual coercion;
- threats

into ordinary attachment protest.

### 18.4 Why ordinary conjoint repair can fail under coercion

Many couple methods assume:

- both people can speak;
- both can refuse;
- both can disclose;
- both can influence treatment;
- neither will retaliate after the session.

Coercive control violates those assumptions.

A highly eloquent joint conversation can therefore produce misleading evidence if one person is speaking under threat.

---

## 19. Sexual rupture

Sexual difficulties occupy a special position because sexuality combines:

- attachment;
- desire;
- bodily autonomy;
- identity;
- shame;
- attraction;
- consent;
- power;
- relational meaning.

Greenberg and Goldman treat attachment, identity and attraction as partly distinct motivational systems, with emotion regulation operating across them.

Therefore a sexual rupture should not be reduced automatically to attachment insecurity.

Possible formulations include:

- discrepant desire;
- changed attraction;
- medical difficulty;
- medication effect;
- body-image difficulty;
- unresolved resentment;
- sexual boredom;
- identity development;
- trauma history;
- coercion;
- pain;
- incompatibility;
- relationship distress.

### 19.1 Consent precedes couple goals

No therapeutic aim to restore sexual connection overrides consent.

A "successful" sexual intervention does not mean one partner becoming more compliant.

### 19.2 Desire cannot be negotiated in the same way as behaviour

Partners can negotiate:

- time;
- context;
- forms of touch;
- how initiation occurs;
- how refusal is communicated.

They cannot straightforwardly contract themselves into spontaneous desire.

This limits moralising interpretations of low desire.

### 19.3 Sexual incompatibility

If preferences remain genuinely non-overlapping despite improved communication and absence of coercion, the issue may be substantive incompatibility rather than dysfunction.

That conclusion should be reached cautiously.

It should not be avoided merely because it complicates preservation of the couple.

---

## 20. Endings

### 20.1 Outcome neutrality

Couple therapy has historically faced an obvious tension:

> Is the client the relationship, or are the clients the people in it?

If relationship preservation becomes the unquestioned outcome, therapy risks pressuring people toward a result they may no longer choose.

Morgan states the psychoanalytic couple-therapy aim in explicitly non-prescriptive terms: the aim is not simply to make partners stay together or become a predetermined type of couple. Her broader model centres the capacity to think about the relationship, tolerate separateness and develop a reflective third position.

Lebow's divorce chapter similarly starts from the fact that separation or divorce is a possible outcome of couple therapy rather than an anomaly.

### 20.2 Attempted repair

An attempted repair can produce several legitimate outcomes:

- restored closeness;
- limited improvement;
- improved communication but unresolved trust;
- clearer incompatibility;
- decision to separate;
- continuing uncertainty.

The quality of the process and the relationship outcome are separate variables.

### 20.3 Failed repair

"Repair failed" can mean different things.

Possibilities include:

- facts remain disputed;
- responsibility is denied;
- remorse is absent;
- behavioural change does not occur;
- the injured person cannot or does not wish to resume vulnerability;
- trust fails to update;
- new breaches occur;
- safety remains inadequate;
- the goals are substantively incompatible;
- the process was mistimed;
- the treatment model did not fit;
- one partner had already left psychologically.

Failure should therefore be formulated rather than merely labelled.

### 20.4 Chronic recurrence

Repeated cycles matter because they become data.

For example:

**breach → apology → temporary change → recurrence → stronger apology → recurrence**

At some point, the behavioural history may carry more weight than statements of intention.

This does not dictate a stay-or-leave decision.

It changes the evidentiary basis on which the relationship is being evaluated.

### 20.5 Separation as transition

Separation may be:

- temporary;
- experimental;
- practical;
- protective;
- transitional;
- final.

The *Clinical Handbook* notes that separation can sometimes provide space for assessment, but its usefulness depends on clarity regarding expectations such as contact, dating, children and joint decisions.

An unstructured separation can intensify uncertainty.

A structured separation does not guarantee reconciliation.

### 20.6 One partner wants to leave

Couple decisions need not be unanimous.

Lebow's chapter notes that when one partner wants divorce and the other does not, therapy must often first negotiate a workable focus; if one partner has clearly and persistently decided to leave, the work can shift toward managing the transition.

The principle is straightforward:

> **A relationship requires continuing participation by both. Ending it does not.**

Therapy can help process the asymmetry without pretending that consensus is required.

### 20.7 Ending with accountability

Ending does not remove the possibility of repair.

Partners may still work toward:

- factual clarity;
- acknowledgment;
- apology;
- restitution;
- co-parenting agreements;
- financial settlement;
- boundaries;
- reduced hostility;
- protection of children from triangulation;
- grief.

This can be meaningful repair without reconciliation.

### 20.8 Grief

Grief after ending does not prove the ending was mistaken.

People grieve:

- the partner;
- the shared identity;
- imagined future;
- home;
- routines;
- family structure;
- social network;
- erotic relationship;
- version of themselves that existed in the relationship.

A decision can be necessary and painful simultaneously.

### 20.9 Continuing contact

Some relationships do not end cleanly because former partners remain connected through:

- children;
- shared property;
- business;
- family;
- community;
- legal proceedings.

The task then becomes redesigning the relationship.

Questions include:

- what communication is necessary;
- what channels are used;
- which topics are relevant;
- how children are protected from adult conflict;
- what privacy exists;
- what boundaries govern new partners;
- what happens if communication becomes aggressive.

### 20.10 Uncertainty

Not all endings require immediate certainty.

People may move through periods of:

- ambivalence;
- trial separation;
- partial recommitment;
- grief;
- reconsideration.

Outcome neutrality does not mean indefinite avoidance.

It means that therapy does not choose the answer in advance.

---

## 21. A formulation framework for relationship viability

This section is deliberately **not** a stay-or-leave algorithm.

Its purpose is to identify what evidence matters.

### Domain 1: Safety

Ask:

- Is either person afraid of the other?
- Is there violence?
- Sexual coercion?
- Stalking?
- Intimidation?
- Retaliation?
- Coercive control?
- Threats?
- Access to weapons?
- Escalating risk during separation?

Safety can dominate the entire formulation.

### Domain 2: Factual reality

Ask:

- Do both partners have a sufficiently accurate understanding of what occurred?
- Is material deception continuing?
- Are relevant facts still emerging?

### Domain 3: Responsibility

Ask:

- Can each person identify their own actions?
- Is responsibility specific?
- Is accountability replaced by counteraccusation?
- Is "the cycle" being used to erase asymmetric conduct?

### Domain 4: Emotional recognition

Ask:

- Can impact be heard?
- Can disagreement coexist with recognition?
- Is the injured person's experience minimised?
- Is the partner associated with the injury allowed to remain a subject rather than reduced to a caricature?

### Domain 5: Behavioural evidence

Ask:

- What has actually changed?
- For how long?
- Under what stress?
- Do actions match commitments?

### Domain 6: Trust trajectory

Ask:

- Is new evidence changing predictions?
- Is trust returning in some domains?
- Is mistrust still being confirmed?

### Domain 7: Repair capacity

Ask:

- Do partners repair earlier?
- Can they revisit difficult events without reproducing them?
- Can they take influence?
- Can they tolerate difference?
- Can they recover after mistakes?

Gottman-oriented research places considerable weight on repair threshold and effectiveness rather than absence of conflict.

### Domain 8: Compatibility

Ask:

- If the cycle improved, would the partners' goals fit?
- What differences can be accepted?
- Which require compromise?
- Which have no jointly enactable solution?

### Domain 9: Autonomy and recognition

Ask:

- Can both partners remain psychologically separate?
- Is disagreement permitted?
- Can one person's agency exist without being experienced automatically as abandonment?

### Domain 10: Motivation

Ask separately:

- Does each person want repair?
- Does each want continued partnership?
- Do they want the same type of relationship?
- Are they participating freely?

### Domain 11: Practical constraints

Relevant realities may include:

- children;
- finances;
- housing;
- health;
- immigration;
- caregiving;
- community;
- employment.

These constraints are real.

They should not be confused with emotional preference.

### Domain 12: Time

Ask:

- Is this the first breach?
- A chronic pattern?
- A recent change?
- Has behaviour been stable long enough to evaluate?

Time converts promises into evidence.

---

## 22. Repair and unresolved difference

A relationship does not need to eliminate every difference to remain viable.

Indeed, recurring differences are normal.

The relevant questions are:

- Can the difference be spoken?
- Can each partner's position exist?
- Is one person's identity erased?
- Can practical arrangements be made?
- Does the issue continually injure the relationship?
- Is the cost acceptable to both?
- Is the difference stable or evolving?

Ringstrom's emphasis on mutual recognition is useful here. The developmental achievement is not convergence into one mind but greater ability to negotiate differences without mutual negation.

Morgan's third-position idea points in a similar direction from a different psychoanalytic tradition: the couple can become capable of thinking about what they are creating together instead of remaining captured entirely by two opposed subjective positions.

Neither framework means that every difference is reconcilable.

Recognition can clarify an impasse.

---

## 23. Trust, surveillance and transparency after betrayal

This area requires particular precision.

### 23.1 Transparency

Transparency after betrayal can function as voluntary evidence generation.

Examples:

- disclosing relevant contact;
- making financial information available;
- proactively explaining deviations from agreed plans;
- answering material questions.

Its function is:

> **reduce the gap between promise and observable reality**

### 23.2 Surveillance

Surveillance operates differently.

It relies on:

- monitoring;
- checking;
- tracking;
- inspecting;
- controlling access.

Some limited checking may occur during an early trust-rebuilding period, and the affair-treatment literature explicitly recognises graduated testing of reliability.

But permanent surveillance is not equivalent to restored trust.

### 23.3 The direction of travel matters

A reparative trajectory usually moves from:

**high uncertainty → consensual transparency → observed reliability → reduced need for checking**

A non-reparative trajectory may remain:

**high uncertainty → surveillance → temporary reassurance → new doubt → more surveillance**

The second loop can persist even without new betrayal.

At that point, the couple may need to ask whether sufficient trust is actually being rebuilt.

---

## 24. Power

Greenberg and Goldman's emotion-focused model explicitly broadens couple formulation beyond attachment to include identity, influence and power. Their work emphasises self-soothing as well as partner responsiveness.

Power matters in rupture because partners rarely enter every conflict with equal resources.

Relevant forms include:

- financial power;
- social power;
- immigration status;
- physical size;
- sexual leverage;
- control of housing;
- control of information;
- caregiving dependency;
- access to children;
- professional status;
- emotional dependence.

A purely circular formulation can obscure these asymmetries.

For example:

> "She pursues, he withdraws."

may be accurate at the behavioural level.

It is incomplete if his "withdrawal" includes withholding all household money until she complies.

Likewise:

> "He becomes jealous, she distances."

is incomplete if jealousy includes digital surveillance and threats.

Interaction must be understood at both the **cycle level** and the **power level**.

---

## 25. Repair when responsibility is disputed

Some of the hardest cases involve incompatible accounts.

Possible positions include:

- "That never happened."
- "It happened, but not the way you describe."
- "It happened, but it did not violate our agreement."
- "I did it, but only because you did X."
- "We never had that agreement."

A disciplined process separates four questions.

### 25.1 What facts can be established?

Do not require false certainty.

Some facts may remain unknown.

### 25.2 What was each person's interpretation?

Interpretations can differ without either being intentionally deceptive.

### 25.3 What agreement existed?

Sometimes a "betrayal" exposes that the couple never actually negotiated the assumed rule.

That does not erase hurt.

It changes the responsibility formulation.

### 25.4 What can be acknowledged despite disagreement?

A person may be able to say:

> "I do not agree that I intended to humiliate you. I understand that what I did was experienced as humiliating."

That is validation without false factual agreement.

If basic facts themselves remain deliberately falsified, deeper repair is much harder because the couple lacks a shared enough reality to work from.

---

## 26. Repair when forgiveness does not occur

Repair can still occur.

Possible outcomes include:

- anger becomes less dominant;
- factual accountability becomes clear;
- behaviour changes;
- co-parenting improves;
- boundaries become stable;
- the relationship ends respectfully;
- the injured person no longer seeks retaliation;
- the event remains morally unforgiven.

The insistence that forgiveness is necessary can become a new violation of autonomy.

Forgiveness may arise.

It should not be manufactured to complete a treatment protocol.

---

## 27. Repair when reconciliation does not occur

This is especially important.

A former partner may eventually say:

> "I understand what happened. I believe you are genuinely sorry. I can see that you have changed. I do not want to resume our relationship."

Nothing in that statement is contradictory.

The person may have repaired:

- factual reality;
- anger;
- understanding;
- self-trust;
- communication.

The relationship may nevertheless remain ended.

This is why:

> **repair ≠ staying together**

---

## 28. Reconciliation without complete resolution

The opposite is also possible.

Partners may choose to continue while saying:

- "Trust is not fully back."
- "I still have questions."
- "We remain different about this."
- "We are not ready to forgive."
- "We are choosing to see what the next year shows."

This can be a reality-based position.

Reconciliation is not required to wait until all uncertainty disappears.

The risk is pretending that continuation proves resolution.

---

## 29. Chronic unresolved difference

A couple can remain viable with disagreement when several conditions exist:

- the disagreement can be named accurately;
- neither person must deny their own position;
- the difference does not require ongoing violation of consent;
- the practical cost is tolerable;
- the issue is not continually weaponised;
- both partners retain meaningful agency;
- accommodations are revisable;
- conflict around the issue can be repaired.

A couple cannot solve some conflicts.

It can sometimes build a life that contains them.

The crucial distinction is between:

> **a difference that can be carried**

and

> **a difference that makes the intended relationship structurally unworkable**

---

## 30. Common formulation errors

### Error 1: "Everything is a cycle"

A cycle formulation is valuable but insufficient for unilateral deception, assault or coercive control.

### Error 2: "Everything is attachment"

Attachment can illuminate why events matter.

It does not replace agreement, ethics, consent, power, compatibility or safety.

### Error 3: "Pain proves betrayal"

A partner can be deeply hurt by an action that did not violate an agreement.

Impact matters.

Classification still matters.

### Error 4: "No bad intention means no injury"

Intention and impact are separate.

### Error 5: "Understanding explains it away"

Explanation can deepen accountability rather than weaken it.

### Error 6: "Both contributed, therefore both are equally responsible"

False.

Interaction can be mutual while event responsibility is asymmetric.

### Error 7: "The apology happened, so the repair is complete"

False.

Observe behaviour over time.

### Error 8: "They reconciled, so trust is restored"

False.

Relationship status and trust are distinct.

### Error 9: "They separated, so therapy failed"

False.

Separation may become the appropriate focus of therapy.

### Error 10: "Good communication will solve incompatibility"

Sometimes good communication reveals incompatibility.

### Error 11: "Forgiveness is necessary for health"

The sources do not justify making forgiveness a universal requirement.

### Error 12: "All aggression can be handled through couple work"

The evidence does not support this.

### Error 13: "Disclosure is always safe"

Disclosure under coercion or retaliation risk can be dangerous.

### Error 14: "Boundaries mean controlling the partner"

A boundary ordinarily concerns one's consent and participation. A mutual relationship rule is an agreement.

### Error 15: "Privacy is deception"

Intimacy does not eliminate legitimate private selfhood.

## 31. Formulation worksheet

The following is a research and clinical formulation scaffold, not a diagnostic instrument and not a decision algorithm.

### A. Event

**What occurred?**

- observable act:
- omission:
- words:
- date/period:
- ongoing or ended:
- disputed facts:

### B. Classification

Which constructs apply?

- [ ] disagreement
- [ ] conflict
- [ ] misattunement
- [ ] rupture
- [ ] attachment injury hypothesis
- [ ] betrayal
- [ ] affair
- [ ] broken agreement
- [ ] boundary violation
- [ ] deception
- [ ] incompatibility
- [ ] aggression
- [ ] coercive control
- [ ] abuse
- [ ] unclear / insufficient information

More than one may apply.

### C. Agreement

- What explicit agreement existed?
- What implicit understanding existed?
- Was it mutually known?
- Could it have been renegotiated?
- Did someone act unilaterally instead?

### D. Consent and autonomy

- Was meaningful consent affected?
- Was relevant information withheld?
- Could each person refuse freely?
- Was there pressure, coercion or fear?

### E. Impact

Map separately:

- emotional:
- bodily:
- attachment:
- identity:
- sexual:
- financial:
- practical:
- parenting:
- safety:
- future expectations:

### F. Responsibility

- responsibility for specific act:
- contextual contribution:
- contribution to recurring cycle:
- responsibility for repair:
- safety responsibility:

Do not force equal percentages.

### G. Attachment significance

If considering attachment injury:

- Was there a critical moment of need?
- Was availability or responsiveness expected?
- Did the event become a benchmark of dependability?
- Is it a recurring stuck point?

If not, do not use the attachment-injury label merely because the event was painful.

### H. Trust

- What was believed before?
- What evidence changed that belief?
- What prediction is now being made?
- What new evidence would justify updating it?

### I. Safety

- fear?
- violence?
- threats?
- stalking?
- retaliation?
- coercive control?
- sexual coercion?
- severe substance-related risk?
- separation-related escalation?

If yes, ordinary conjoint vulnerability work may not be appropriate.

### J. Behavioural repair evidence

- What has changed?
- For how long?
- Under what stress?
- Has the change survived recurrence of the original trigger?

### K. Compatibility

After imagining a substantially improved cycle:

- Do the goals still conflict?
- Is there a jointly enactable arrangement?
- Which differences are tolerable?
- Which are not?

### L. Relationship outcome

Current position:

- attempting repair;
- reconciled but trust rebuilding;
- undecided;
- structured separation;
- ending;
- post-separation repair/cooperation;
- other.

No option is coded as the therapeutic "win."

## 32. Evidence that repair is progressing

No single sign is decisive.

The strongest formulation uses converging evidence.

Possible indicators include:

### Factual

- fewer contradictions;
- voluntary disclosure of relevant information;
- no new material deception.

### Responsibility

- less blame shifting;
- more specific ownership;
- ability to distinguish context from excuse.

### Emotional

- injured partner can describe impact with greater differentiation;
- other partner can remain engaged rather than immediately defending;
- vulnerability can be expressed safely.

### Behavioural

- concrete commitments are implemented;
- previous breach pattern decreases or stops;
- partners use different responses under comparable stress.

### Boundary

- agreements are clearer;
- consent is respected;
- third-party contact follows the agreement;
- privacy and transparency are better differentiated.

### Trust

- checking becomes less necessary;
- promises increasingly predict behaviour;
- reliance becomes more voluntary.

### Interactional

- repair begins earlier;
- escalation becomes less severe;
- partners can take influence;
- both realities can be represented.

### Differentiation

- disagreement no longer automatically signifies abandonment;
- autonomy no longer automatically signifies rejection;
- closeness no longer requires self-erasure.

### Outcome clarity

Paradoxically, progress may include clearer recognition that the relationship should end.

---

## 33. Evidence that repair remains stalled

Possible indicators include:

- material facts are still being concealed;
- responsibility is repeatedly displaced;
- apology remains global and non-specific;
- new agreements are broken;
- one partner is pressured to "get over it";
- remorse repeatedly substitutes for change;
- monitoring expands rather than recedes;
- retaliation follows honest disclosure;
- aggression escalates;
- vulnerability is weaponised later;
- the outside relationship remains concealed or ambiguously ongoing;
- the same breach recurs;
- the partners can communicate better but still want incompatible futures;
- only one person wants continued partnership.

These observations do not automatically determine an outcome.

They alter the formulation.

---

## 34. The special problem of self-blame

Injured partners often ask:

> "What did I do to cause this?"

There are two different questions hidden inside it.

### Question 1

**What was my contribution to the state of our relationship?**

This may be valuable.

### Question 2

**Did my failures make the other person's breach my responsibility?**

Not necessarily.

A person can contribute to:

- distance;
- conflict;
- loneliness;
- poor sex;
- resentment

without being responsible for the partner's deception, coercion or violence.

Excessive self-blame can create false control:

> "If I caused it, I can guarantee it will never happen again by changing enough."

That conclusion may feel safer than recognising another person's independent agency.

The repair task is better served by precise responsibility than by maximal responsibility.

---

## 35. The special problem of offender collapse

The partner associated with a rupture may move from responsibility into global shame:

- "I'm a monster."
- "I'm worthless."
- "Nothing I say matters."

This can sound accountable while functioning defensively.

The injured partner then faces a new task:

> comfort the person whose conduct injured them.

Specific responsibility is more useful than identity collapse.

Compare:

> "I am irredeemably terrible."

with:

> "I concealed this relationship for six months. I understand that this changed your ability to consent to the relationship you thought we had. I am responsible for that choice."

The second statement is narrower and more actionable.

---

## 36. Meaning without moral simplification

Couple ruptures rarely have only one layer of meaning.

An affair can simultaneously involve:

- attraction;
- avoidance;
- resentment;
- identity;
- novelty;
- attachment;
- sexual dissatisfaction;
- opportunity;
- entitlement;
- loneliness;
- developmental repetition;
- self-expansion;
- impulsivity;
- power.

A financial betrayal may involve:

- shame;
- fear;
- status;
- autonomy;
- addiction;
- control;
- avoidance of conflict.

A single explanatory lens should therefore remain provisional.

Mikulincer and Shaver's attachment synthesis makes attachment highly relevant to adult emotion regulation, couple functioning, caregiving and sexuality, but its breadth is not a licence to reinterpret every relationship event as attachment-system activation. Their own organisation treats couple functioning, caregiving and sex as distinguishable domains within adult attachment research.

---

## 37. Relationship work after the acute rupture

Long-term repair eventually has to return to ordinary life.

Gabb and Fink's qualitative account of enduring couple relationships emphasises ordinary practices such as:

- thoughtful gestures;
- household labour;
- couple time;
- communication;
- intimacy;
- humour;
- everyday relationship work.

This matters because repaired relationships are not sustained only through dramatic emotional conversations.

Trust can be rebuilt through mundane reliability:

- coming home when agreed;
- sharing labour;
- following through;
- remembering;
- making time;
- communicating changes;
- respecting sexual refusal;
- handling money transparently;
- noticing distress.

Repair eventually has to become boring.

That is often a sign that the new pattern is entering daily life.

---

## 38. From acute repair to relational development

Once the immediate rupture is sufficiently stabilised, deeper questions may become relevant:

- Can each partner tolerate the other's separateness?
- Can each hold a view that is not mirrored?
- Can one person's growth occur without being experienced automatically as betrayal?
- Can closeness exist without possession?
- Can autonomy exist without emotional abandonment?
- Can the couple think together rather than only react?

Ringstrom frames relational development around actualisation, mutual recognition and renegotiation. Morgan frames it around developing a reflective "couple state of mind."

These are different theoretical traditions, but both resist a model of repair in which one partner simply restores the old relational equilibrium.

Sometimes the pre-rupture equilibrium itself needs to change.

---

## 39. Endings as a relational process

A good ending does not require both partners to agree that the relationship should end.

It can still involve:

- reality;
- responsibility;
- boundaries;
- grief;
- reduced retaliation;
- care for children;
- financial clarity;
- changed forms of contact.

Morgan's treatment of endings is especially useful here because it does not define preservation as the aim of couple therapy. Her model asks whether partners can increasingly think about the relationship, tolerate separateness and recognise what is actually ending.

An ending can therefore contain repair even where reconciliation is explicitly absent.

## 42. Final synthesis

The central discipline in work with rupture is **differentiation**.

Differentiate:

- event from interpretation;
- impact from intention;
- context from responsibility;
- explanation from excuse;
- emotion from fact;
- validation from agreement;
- ordinary conflict from coercion;
- rupture from attachment injury;
- betrayal from incompatibility;
- privacy from secrecy;
- autonomy from withdrawal;
- remorse from change;
- apology from repair;
- forgiveness from reconciliation;
- reconciliation from trust;
- repair from preservation;
- preservation from health;
- ending from failure.

The practical logic is then:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED?
        ↓
WHAT KIND OF EVENT OR PATTERN IS IT?
        ↓
WHAT WAS ACTUALLY RUPTURED?
        ↓
IS THERE SUFFICIENT SAFETY FOR CONJOINT WORK?
        ↓
WHAT RESPONSIBILITY BELONGS TO WHOM?
        ↓
WHAT IMPACT NEEDS TO BE RECOGNISED?
        ↓
WHAT MEANING CAN BE UNDERSTOOD WITHOUT EXCUSING?
        ↓
WHAT BEHAVIOUR MUST CHANGE?
        ↓
WHAT AGREEMENT OR BOUNDARY NOW APPLIES?
        ↓
WHAT DOES REPEATED BEHAVIOUR SHOW?
        ↓
HOW SHOULD TRUST UPDATE?
        ↓
WHAT RELATIONSHIP, IF ANY, DO THE PEOPLE FREELY CHOOSE NOW?
```

The strongest evidence of repair is not emotional intensity, eloquence or a promise.

It is increasing alignment among:

**fact → responsibility → emotional recognition → behaviour → agreement → repeated reliability**

Attachment-injury research adds an important process insight: certain betrayals or failures at critical moments become recurring tests of whether the partner is accessible and dependable, and resolution can involve moving from protest and defence toward vulnerability, acknowledgment and a new responsive encounter. Millikin's discovery work established the preliminary pathway, while Makinen and Johnson supplied the stronger verification-stage evidence.

Gottman's work adds a temporal insight: trust is built or eroded in repeated interdependent moments, and repair is embedded in the wider emotional history of the relationship rather than residing in a single statement. Attunement and early repair contribute to a relationship in which each partner's interests can remain psychologically present to the other.

The affair literature adds an accountability insight: impact must be stabilised, meaning can then be explored, and the final task is not necessarily reconciliation but an informed decision about how to move forward.

The aggression literature supplies the critical limit: not every relational problem should be processed conjointly. Severe violence, coercive control, intimidation, sexual coercion, stalking and retaliation change the therapeutic frame because safety and freedom to speak become prior conditions. Structured screening and separate assessment are therefore not peripheral to couple work.

Integrative behavioural, relational and psychoanalytic sources add another corrective: some differences are not removed by better communication. Partners may need to tolerate enduring difference, renegotiate the relationship around it, or recognise genuine incompatibility. Mutual recognition means encountering the other as a separate subject, not producing agreement at any price.

Finally, the literature on separation and endings makes outcome neutrality explicit. Couple therapy can assist people who are trying to remain together, people who do not yet know, and people who are ending.

The resulting governing proposition is:

> **Repair is the process by which reality, responsibility, recognition, behaviour and future agreements become sufficiently coherent that trust can update. The direction of that update does not predetermine the future of the relationship.**

A repaired relationship may continue.

A relationship may continue with incomplete repair.

A repaired relationship may end.

An ending may itself require repair.

And when coercion or danger is present, the central task may cease to be relational repair at all and become the restoration of safety, autonomy and freedom to choose.
